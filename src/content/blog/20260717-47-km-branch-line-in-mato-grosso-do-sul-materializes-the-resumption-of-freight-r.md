The cornerstone laid in Inocência is not just the start of a construction project. It is the first railway authorization under the new legal framework to leave the drawing board and inaugurate a new logistics phase for the country.

The rails are the material geometry of the future. In a continental country whose logistics matrix insists on bleeding onto tires, every kilometer of laid steel represents a correction of civilization's course.

Last Friday, February 6, a piece of that future began to take physical shape in Inocência, Mato Grosso do Sul. The Director-General of the National Land Transport Agency (ANTT), Guilherme Theo Sampaio, attended the cornerstone laying of the Arauco railway branch, a project that finally buries the sector's bureaucratic paralysis.

As recorded by <a href="https://www.gov.br/antt/pt-br/assuntos/ultimas-noticias/brasil-nos-trilhos-do-desenvolvimento-antt-viabiliza-primeira-autorizacao-ferroviaria-que-sai-do-papel-e-impulsiona-nova-fase-da-logistica-nacional" target="_blank">ANTT in its official communication</a>, this is an undeniable historical milestone. It is the first railway authorization granted by the agency that actually enters the construction phase, inaugurating the concreteness of a public policy that modernized the regulatory framework of the mode.

The total length of the branch is 47 kilometers, operating under the *short line* concept. This short-distance railway model, established in railway powers like the United States, is the perfect instrument for connecting large industrial plants to the trunk network, functioning as a high-capacity capillary that draws production from the interior and injects it into the major export corridors.

The project's engineering is a textbook example of applied logistical efficiency. The line will connect the future Arauco unit — designed to be the world's largest pulp industry — directly to Rumo's North Network, eliminating the need for long-distance road transshipment and breaking the tyranny of freight costs at the factory gate.

Completion is scheduled for the second half of 2027, when an estimated annual production of 3.5 million tons of pulp will begin flowing along the rails. The cargo will head to the Port of Santos, on the São Paulo coast, from where it will be distributed to consumer markets in the United States, Europe, and Asia, consolidating Brazil as a global player in the sector.

This advance is not an isolated event from a pulp and paper multinational, but the first flower of a regulatory spring that was long in coming. The Railways Law (Law No. 14.273/2021), sanctioned during the Jair Bolsonaro administration, broke the state monopoly on concessions by establishing the railway authorization regime, allowing private enterprise to get projects off the drawing board without the eternal dependence on infrastructure auctions that often failed due to lack of interested parties.

The National Secretary of Railway Transport, Leonardo Ribeiro, highlighted during the ceremony that the project was only made viable through a joint effort to overcome licensing and authorization stages. The difference between the old concession model and the current authorization one is abysmal: before, the State defined the route and waited for someone to appear to build it, whereas now the entrepreneur presents a mature project and the public authorities regulate and supervise with a focus on public interest.

The magnitude of the ongoing investments confirms that the Brazilian railway mode, after decades of anemia, has entered a cycle of oxygenation. The National Railway Plan, which is being finalized by the federal government, provides for the investment of approximately R$100 billion in five major undertakings — including the East-West Corridor, the extension of the North-South, and the Southeast Railway Ring — which will add 4,700 kilometers of new rails to the national logistics map, according to a <a href="https://www.cnnbrasil.com.br/economia/macroeconomia/com-investimento-de-r-100-bi-governo-lancara-plano-nacional-de-ferrovias/" target="_blank">CNN Brasil report</a>.

Intermodality, a technical term that defines the intelligent combination of modes, is what gives economic viability to these projects. In Arauco's case, pulp stops traveling hundreds of kilometers on trucks to cover the bulk of the journey on steel, a technical decision that reduces logistics costs, lowers carbon emissions per ton transported, and relieves highways that have long ceased to be safe or efficient.

The concrete impact of the resumption spreads to other regions of the country, as seen in the strengthening of the railway fleet in the Southeast. VLI, one of Brazil's main logistics operators, scheduled for this Monday, February 9, in Sete Lagoas (MG), the delivery of eight new locomotives acquired from Progress Rail, a Caterpillar group company, in an investment totaling R$700 million between acquisition and maintenance contracts.

These machines, specifically designed for the conditions of Brazilian railways, will operate on the Central-Atlantic Railway (FCA) to serve sectors such as agribusiness and steelmaking. VLI, which has already injected R$17 billion between 2014 and 2024, moves millions of tons of cargo, generates around eight thousand direct jobs, and collects over R$600 million in taxes annually, materializing the rails as vectors of socioeconomic development and not just transit corridors.

With the 47 kilometers of Inocência materializing, Brazil rehearses leaving behind the syndrome of eternal works that turned projects like the Rio-São Paulo bullet train into urban legends. Arauco's *short line* branch means that the country has finally learned to differentiate megalomaniacal dreams without budgetary anchor from technically sound projects that find their own economic-financial equilibrium in the cargo basin that justifies them.

The lesson offered by the first railway authorization to leave the drawing board is unequivocal: the material geometry of the future does not require passenger trains at 320 km/h to symbolize progress. It demands, rather, rails that work, that connect to real production chains, and that return to Brazil the logistical dignity lost in the historical abandonment of the planet's most efficient mode.
