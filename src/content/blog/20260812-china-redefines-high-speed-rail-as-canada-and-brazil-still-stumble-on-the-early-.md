High-speed train technology is no longer a futuristic promise, but a mirror of a nation's capacity for planning and sovereign innovation.

When technique organizes space, society gains time to live. This maxim materializes in the 35,000 kilometers of high-speed lines crisscrossing China, the largest network on the planet, where the Fuxing CR400BF train glides at 350 km/h as a commercial routine.

The country that three decades ago began its journey in high speed with the modest Guangzhou–Shenzhen line, operating between 160 and 200 km/h, today dictates global railway engineering standards. The Fuxing, successor to the Hexie model, is not just a train, but a collaborative innovation ecosystem involving thousands of researchers and 254 key proprietary technologies.

A longitudinal study from Beijing University of Chemical Technology, published in <a href='https://www.kygl.net.cn/EN/10.19571/j.cnki.1000-2995.2025.12.001' target='_blank'>Science Research Management</a>, revealed how the evolution of the innovation ecosystem enabled China to overcome the technological “stranglehold” imposed by the US. The analysis shows that changes in the core platform boosted collaboration among institutions, organizations, and knowledge, increasing innovation efficiency in megaprojects.

The Fuxing CR400BF was designed for a 30-year service life after 600,000 kilometers of testing. Its height of 4,050 mm and width of 3,360 mm, combined with a length 25 meters greater than its predecessor, maximize capacity and comfort.

According to German magazine <a href='https://www.fusionmagazin.de/article/chinas-mega-engineering-projekt-die-hochgeschwindigkeitsbahnen/' target='_blank'>Fusion Magazin</a>, the Fuxing reduced energy consumption by 10% compared to the Hexie 380 at the same speed of 350 km/h. The internal noise level at the cabin-car junction measures only 76 decibels, below the excellence threshold of 79 decibels.

Design standardization was one of the Chinese secrets to building infrastructure at costs about two-thirds lower than those of other countries, as pointed out by the World Bank. The onboard network control system (CTCS), the compact pantograph, and the high-speed bogies were developed in-house, with 84% of the Fuxing's technologies being genuinely Chinese.

The compensatory resonance vibration damping technology, which allows a coin to stand upright at 350 km/h, is a remarkable example of Chinese innovation. This technology demonstrates China's ability to develop advanced and competitive solutions in the railway sector.

While China already operates modern and efficient stations, Canada is still rehearsing its first project. The North American country plans to invest up to 80 billion Canadian dollars (53 billion euros) in the Toronto–Montréal–Québec line, with a maximum speed of 300 km/h.

According to <a href='https://www.bfmtv.com/economie/entreprises/transports/qui-va-remporter-le-megaprojet-canadien-de-tgv-entre-quebec-et-toronto_AV-202410290593.html' target='_blank'>BFMTV portal</a>, the option for TGV was pushed by Quebec politicians and giants like Alstom, which after acquiring Bombardier has the Caisse de dépôt et placement du Québec as its largest shareholder. The project would reduce travel time between Toronto and Quebec from five to three hours, with an estimated lifespan of 150 years.

Competing in the Canadian tender are consortia led by SNCF, Systra, Keolis, RATP Dev, and Meridiam for operation, while Alstom, Siemens, and Talgo compete for the supply of rolling stock. The separation of passenger and freight traffic is one of the pillars of the project, overcoming the current bottleneck where freight trains have priority and cause constant delays.

Brazil, which still stumbles in the debate over the SP–RJ Intercity Train, watches from afar the materialization of a concept that the Chinese have turned into a commodity. The disparity is not only of scale, but of method: China treated high speed as state policy, with long-term planning and continuous investment in research and development.

China's goal of achieving eight vertical and eight horizontal axes by 2030 contrasts with the decisional paralysis that has been postponing Brazilian projects for decades. While CASIC (China Aerospace Science and Industry Corporation) is already testing maglev prototypes projecting speeds of 600 km/h with a peak of 800 km/h, the South American country has yet to get its dreamed-of 300 km/h off the drawing board.

Chinese success lies not only in scale, but in building a collaborative innovation system that integrated universities, state-owned enterprises, and suppliers around clear goals. The experience shows that high-speed rail is not a luxury for rich countries, but a driver of development, emissions reduction, and territorial reorganization.

Canada, even with a robust financial system and an industrial partner like Alstom, will take years to complete its first line. Brazil, which has already had the opportunity to learn from others' mistakes and successes, urgently needs to transform discourse into technical backing and political decision, before the future of rail passes once again beyond its territory.
