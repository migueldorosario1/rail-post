Federal state-owned company takes on the role of structurer of Light Rail Vehicles, replicating the BNDES model and connecting railway engineering to the requalification of medium and large cities.

When technique organizes space, society gains time to live. The phrase echoes with surgical precision in the current moment of Brazilian urban transportation, where a silent institutional engineering shift begins to unlock mobility projects that had been shelved for decades.

The CEO of Infra S.A., Jorge Bastos, recently announced a strategic shift by the federal state-owned company, which takes on the role of structurer of Light Rail Vehicles (VLTs) in medium and large cities across the country. The expertise, inherited from the merger between the Logistics and Planning Company (EPL) and Valec's railway engineering, now turns towards the urban fabric with the same technical rigor applied to national logistics corridors.

The move replicates, in the universe of urban rails, the historical role that BNDES played in structuring sanitation and highway projects. The difference is that, this time, the technical intelligence is concentrated in a state-owned company with railway DNA, capable of producing Technical, Economic and Environmental Feasibility Studies (EVTEA) that turn political intentions into assets able to receive public and private investments.

The president of the Brazilian Association of the Railway Industry (ABIFER), Vicente Abate, highlighted that Infra S.A.'s technical capacity is a game-changer for the segment, as pointed out by <a href="https://abifer.org.br/en/projetos-de-vlts-no-brasil-ganham-impulso/" target="_blank">ABIFER in its sectoral analysis</a>. The entity sees in the state-owned company the necessary muscle to remove the main bottleneck that historically stalled VLT projects in Brazil: the lack of solid, legally sound feasibility studies.

The most emblematic concrete case is in Bauru, in the interior of São Paulo, where a city of 380,000 inhabitants commissioned Infra S.A. for the EVTEA for a 28.8-kilometer light rail system. The project envisions three branches and over 30 stations, stitching peripheral neighborhoods to the center and using the existing railway network — an underused asset that lies dormant under the asphalt in dozens of Brazilian cities.

The brownfield logic of Bauru, which relies on reusing already installed tracks, drastically reduces expropriation and earthmoving costs. While greenfield projects require tearing up the urban fabric from scratch, the railway reuse engineering turns liabilities into clean mobility corridors, with electric traction and intermodal integration potential in the most densely populated districts.

In Paraíba, Campina Grande — a city with over 400,000 inhabitants — is also moving forward with Infra S.A. studies for its own light rail, in the final phase of data collection and technical consolidation. The expected delivery of the EVTEA in the first half of 2026 puts the project on a collision course with budgetary reality, paving the way for the second largest city in Paraíba to join the select group of Brazilian municipalities that operate rail transport systems with electric propulsion.

Currently, only Rio de Janeiro and Fortaleza boast light rail systems in commercial operation in Brazil, both with electric traction and integration with other modes. The addition of Bauru and Campina Grande to the national portfolio would represent a qualitative leap, proving that medium-sized cities can — and should — benefit from light rail technology without depending exclusively on discretionary mega-budgets from the federal government.

The scale horizon, however, lies in São Paulo, where the 'Bonde São Paulo' project plans 12 kilometers of light rail in the center of the state capital, with an estimated investment of R$ 4 billion. The project, which will be executed via Public-Private Partnership (PPP) by the city government, gained unexpected traction after the consortium MEZ-RZK Novo Centro won, in February, the R$ 6 billion auction to build the new administrative complex for the state government.

The synergy between the new administrative center and São Paulo's light rail is a classic example of how urban requalification can catalyze investments in rails. When public authorities concentrate jobs and services in areas served by electric mass transit, captive demand reduces the revenue risk for the private operator, making the PPP model much more palatable to the financial market.

For the national industry, the horizon of new projects lights up a positive alert for technological competitiveness. Vicente Abate stated that the Brazilian industrial park is prepared to supply solutions beyond conventional electric traction, including hybrid systems and trains powered by green hydrogen, aligning urban mobility with the energy transition agenda that dominates infrastructure debates in the real world — and not just in official speeches.

By simultaneously structuring projects in cities of different sizes and regions, Infra S.A. builds an institutional learning curve that Brazil lacked. The standardization of EVTEAs, the formulation of replicable legal-financial models, and the dialogue with the local industry create a technical ecosystem capable of shortening the path from the drawing board to the construction site.

The light rail, historically treated as a tourist eccentricity or cosmetic solution, reveals its true vocation: a structuring transport corridor in medium-sized cities that cannot support a subway but can no longer endure the chaos of buses competing with cars for space. Rail electrification, with segregated lanes and traffic signal priority, delivers commercial speed, travel predictability, and urban requalification far superior to the saturated Bus Rapid Transit (BRT) systems that multiplied in the last decade.

Brazil has a gigantic urban railway liability that is beginning to be read not as a burden but as an opportunity. The decision to use Infra S.A. as the technical brain behind this turnaround reveals that there is, finally, a strategic understanding that mobility planning requires state engineering — not just auctions of private goodwill.

While the country discusses bullet trains and transcontinental railways, the silent revolution of urban light rail advances at the right scale, in the feasible political time, and with the technical density necessary not to become yet another Brazilian railway legend. Bauru, Campina Grande, and São Paulo are the tip of the rail of a movement that can redefine the landscape and productivity of dozens of cities that grew with their backs turned to the tracks — and that now are rehearsing to reconnect with them.
