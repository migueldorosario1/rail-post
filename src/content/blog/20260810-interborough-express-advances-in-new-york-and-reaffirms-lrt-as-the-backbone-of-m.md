The largest US light rail project in decades enters engineering and design phase, replicating the logic driving 9 Brazilian projects to reactivate disused railway corridors.

New York Governor Kathy Hochul authorized the start of the engineering and design phase for the Interborough Express (IBX), the largest Light Rail Transit (LRT) project in the United States in decades. The 14-mile (22.5 km) system will use an existing freight railway line between Sunset Park, Brooklyn, and Jackson Heights, Queens, and will be the first new rapid transit system built entirely within New York City since the full opening of the IND Crosstown Line (current G line) in 1937, except for the section between Court Square and Nassau Avenue, completed only in 1989.

The decision consolidates a global pattern that <a href="https://www.mundotrilhos.com/blog/20260709-vlt-da-baixada-santista-inovacoes-tecnicas-e-planejamento-integrado-para-a-mobil/" target="_blank">Brazil has already recognized in at least 9 LRT projects</a> at different stages of development: the reactivation of disused railway corridors as the backbone of metropolitan mobility. The IBX exactly replicates the logic adopted in the Baixada Santista LRT, which uses the exclusive right-of-way of the old Samaritá-Porto railway to stitch the urban fabric between São Vicente and Santos.

The IBX's demand projection is staggering by US standards: 160,000 passengers per day and 48 million annual trips, surpassing the Los Angeles system, which registers 46 million trips per year. About 70% of projected passengers will transfer within the Metropolitan Transportation Authority (MTA) network itself, connecting to 17 subway lines, 50 bus routes, and 2 Long Island Rail Road (LIRR) stations.

The consortium formed by Jacobs and HDR was selected by the MTA board to oversee the system's engineering and design. The design phase, officially beginning this American summer, will cover signaling and communications, vehicle design, permanent way design, station civil engineering, bridge reconstruction, retaining walls, and the operations and garage facilities.

The total estimated cost of the IBX is US$5.5 billion, with 50% of that amount already secured in the MTA Capital Plan 2025-2029 — US$2.75 billion approved by Hochul in April. The design phase is funded by US$45 million from the New York State 2025 budget and by US$15 million from the RAISE (Rebuilding American Infrastructure with Sustainability and Equity) grant from the US Department of Transportation.

As pointed out by the <a href="https://www.governor.ny.gov/news/governor-hochul-announces-interborough-express-advancing-planning-active-phase" target="_blank">official state government release</a>, the MTA adopted a tunnel solution under Metropolitan Avenue in Middle Village, Queens, replacing the originally planned mixed-traffic operation. This engineering change reduced the end-to-end travel time from 39 to 32 minutes and increased the daily passenger projection by 50,000 people, an efficiency leap that exposes the real cost of shared operation with road traffic.

The choice of Light Rail Transit (LRT) technology was not trivial. MTA President and CEO Janno Lieber described the IBX as "a game-changer for millions" and emphasized the importance of directly connecting the city's two most populous boroughs for work, education, and leisure. The corridor will serve approximately 900,000 residents and 260,000 workers along 19 planned stations.

The IBX engineering directly dialogues with the Brazilian experience accumulated in the Carioca LRT, which also eliminated the overhead catenary by adopting the ground-level power supply (APS) system. In Rio de Janeiro, the city government laid the first tracks in 2015. Although the absence of overhead wires is a gesture of respect for the landscape heritage, the cost of APS is significantly higher per km than conventional catenary, and the system faced operational problems recorded in Rio.

The Brazilian scenario of LRT projects reveals an impressive methodological alignment with the New York case. The Baixada Santista system, developed by SYSTRA Vetec engineering in Brazil, applies global mobility innovations to a complex and densely populated urban setting, taking advantage of the exclusive right-of-way of a disused railway with contemporary logistical intelligence. The region is home to 1.8 million inhabitants and the largest port in South America, the Port of Santos, and the consolidation of the functional project required demand studies and simulation of the new travel dynamics.

According to a survey by <a href="https://viatrolebus.com.br/2025/04/conheca-9-projetos-de-vlt-no-brasil/" target="_blank">Via Trolebus, Brazil has 9 LRT projects</a> in different stages, spread across São Paulo (Bonde São Paulo), Guarulhos-Santo André (Line 14-Ônix), Campinas, Sorocaba, Bauru, Curitiba-São José dos Pinhais, Vitória-Vila Velha, Niterói, and Florianópolis. All share the premise of reactivating railway beds or restructuring saturated public transport corridors, exactly as the IBX does with the CSX Corp. and LIRR freight line. The LIRR operates the Bay Ridge Branch, which is the main project corridor, while CSX has trackage rights, adding complexity to the right-of-way negotiations.

The technical convergence between the two hemispheres is not rhetorical coincidence. The Baixada Santista LRT, for example, contemplates essential modularity in the specification of fixed systems, including control signaling, telecommunications, and the operational control center, the same engineering menu that the Jacobs-HDR consortium will deliver for New York. The sizing of maintenance yards and the structuring of a modern fare collection system, integrated with bicycle parking and a network of bike lanes adjacent to the track, indicate that the LRT was conceived as the main axis of active and connected mobility, both in São Vicente and in Brooklyn.

The IBX also illuminates a reality that Brazilian planning needs to urgently absorb: the mere discussion about competition between LRT and Bus Rapid Transit (BRT) loses strength in the face of a total lifecycle cost analysis of the infrastructure. Although the initial capital investment in BRT is lower, LRT responds with superior energy efficiency, braking energy recovery, and a drastically reduced permanent way maintenance cost over the years — a decisive factor when projecting 48 million annual trips.

When Governor Hochul states that "the IBX is the kind of project that future generations will describe as an obvious success," she echoes what rails have already proven in cities like Bordeaux, Reims, and Jerusalem. The history of technology repeats itself in modern guise: when rails return to the center of mobility strategy over the heritage of a disused railway, the result is not a nostalgic museum, but the reclaiming of useful time for an entire population that spends fewer hours stuck and gains more hours to live the city.

The fact that New York has not built an entirely new rapid transit system since 1937 and now bets precisely on LRT as a vector of metropolitan transformation is an unequivocal technical message for Brazilian mayors, governors, and planners. The mode that many insist on treating as a secondary solution is, in fact, the most effective precision tool to stitch the urban fabric when one has an inherited railway corridor and a vision of intermodal integration that asphalt will never achieve.
