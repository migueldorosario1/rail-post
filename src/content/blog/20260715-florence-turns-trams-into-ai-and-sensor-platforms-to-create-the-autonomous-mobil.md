The European ELASTIC project incorporated cameras, LiDAR, and radars into vehicles on the Florence network, merging massive data with edge computing to detect real-time hazards and pave the way for driverless trams.

Busy urban spaces need innovative transport solutions and smart city applications. Advancing with the ELASTIC project, a system equipped with cutting-edge sensors was embedded in trams and soon at stops in Florence, collecting and aggregating significant data on the transport network's performance.

Three vehicles on the Florence network, operated by GEST, were transformed into state-of-the-art mobile sensing platforms. They received cameras, Inertial Measurement Units (IMUs), radars, and LiDAR scanners, as the project consortium detailed to CORDIS, the European Commission's information service.

Each tram generates a massive volume of raw data per trip, capturing the dynamic pulse of the historic city. This information is fused with data from static sensors installed around the stops, creating an instant digital twin of the monitored areas.

The ELASTIC project, whose acronym stands for a software architecture for extreme-scale big data analytics in fog computing ecosystems, had a budget of 5.9 million euros. The 3.5-year initiative, completed in 2022, was coordinated by the Barcelona Supercomputing Center (BSC) with industrial partners such as Thales and SixSq.

The technological key lies not only in collection but in the location of processing. Instead of sending the entire data deluge to the cloud, the system uses edge computing and fog computing to process information in real time on the vehicles themselves and at street stations.

Complex artificial intelligence (AI) methods distill this sensory chaos into actionable knowledge at the very moment it is generated. The system instantly detects obstacles, pedestrians, and other vehicles encroaching on the track, alerting the driver to imminent dangers with a lead time that the human eye cannot achieve.

One of the major technical challenges overcome was precise autonomous localization. Eduardo Quiñones, senior researcher at BSC and project coordinator, explained that the system provides extremely accurate positioning even in tunnels or under canopies where GPS coverage simply disappears.

Sensor fusion combines LiDAR detections, which map the surroundings in three dimensions with laser beams, with the robustness of radar, which is unhindered by rain or fog. The IMUs, in turn, track every vibration, acceleration, and curve of the vehicle, allowing the system to know exactly where the tram is and how it behaves mechanically on the track.

The consortium specifically aimed to create "smart areas" at three key intersections between tram lines, streets, and pedestrian crossings in the Metropolitan City of Florence. These zones are the meeting point of all risk vectors: the rail, the rubber tire, and the pedestrian's step.

The relevance of this Italian experiment echoes directly in the Brazilian reality, where the modernization of Light Rail Vehicle (VLT) systems still focuses on ground-level power supply and modal integration. While the VLT Carioca, a national benchmark, transports 300,000 passengers per day with a renowned operation, its electronic architecture does not yet employ the layer of autonomous environmental sensing that Florence tested.

In Italy, trams are not seen as nostalgic museum pieces, but rather as high-performance computing platforms for a safer city. The logic is relentless: if the tram can process the environment and predict collisions, it becomes an active agent in reducing fatal accidents, especially in cities with historic layouts and choked road networks.

The interaction between the public vehicle and urban infrastructure ceases to be mechanical and becomes algorithmic. Traffic lights along the tram's path can be dynamically reprogrammed, and track-bed intrusion alerts reach the control center even before the driver needs to hit the emergency brake.

The great strategic goal of ELASTIC was to pave the regulatory and technological ground for autonomous trams. With a system capable of locating itself without GPS, seeing 360 degrees at all times, and making micro safety decisions at the network edge, Florence positions itself as a living laboratory of rail autonomy.

For Brazilian cities planning to expand their VLT networks, such as Fortaleza and Salvador, the technical legacy of ELASTIC offers a map of future requirements. Laying tracks and purchasing rolling stock are merely the hardware of the 20th century; the true disruption lies in the on-board digital brain that protects life and optimizes mobility.

The efficiency of mass public transport today depends as much on its computational capacity as on its civil engineering. A tram that senses, anticipates, and communicates with the city redefines what it means to be a smart transport mode in the 21st century.
