The country rediscovers railways as a vector of development, but the regulatory and financial challenge remains colossal.

When technique organizes space, society gains time to live. The phrase resonates strongly in the current Brazilian railway moment, in which the federal government is putting together a R$100 billion plan to expand the freight network by 4,700 kilometers, according to a report by <a href='https://www.cnnbrasil.com.br/economia/macroeconomia/com-investimento-de-r-100-bi-governo-lancara-plano-nacional-de-ferrovias/' target='_blank'>CNN Brasil</a> on the National Railway Plan.

The most emblematic project is the proposal for a transcontinental railway between Ilhéus, in Bahia, and the Peruvian port of Chancay. Although still under study, the route would potentially cut through Bahia, Goiás, Mato Grosso, Rondônia, and Acre before crossing the Andean border and reaching the Pacific, as detailed by <a href='https://g1.globo.com/economia/noticia/2025/07/08/ferrovia-para-ligar-o-atlantico-ao-pacifico-veja-o-que-se-sabe-ate-agora-do-projeto-do-brasil-com-a-china.ghtml' target='_blank'>G1</a>.

The initiative falls within the Chinese logic of food security: 70% of the soy and corn imported by Beijing come from Brazil, making Brazil’s internal logistics a strategic priority for Xi Jinping’s government, as pointed out by <a href='https://investnews.com.br/negocios/brasil-tenta-de-novo-expandir-as-ferrovias-mas-ainda-falta-combinar-com-os-chineses/' target='_blank'>InvestNews</a> in an analysis of the difficulty in attracting private capital to the sector.

The mathematics of railways is relentless and explains why the State needs to commit a significant share of the resources. Each kilometer of track costs an average of R$27 million, more than three times the value of a highway, estimated at R$8 million per kilometer. The financial return can take decades, and even in wealthy countries, state railway companies depend on public subsidies to keep operations afloat.

In Brazil, commodity transport absolutely dominates the rails. Iron ore accounted for 73% of all railway freight handled in 2025, totaling 406.6 million tonnes, of which 301 million were transported exclusively by Vale on its vertically integrated railways. The mining company controls mine, railway, and port—the perfect tripod of the mineral business, as a specialist heard by the sector defined it.

Agribusiness, despite being the engine of Brazilian exports, still occupies a modest space on the rails: 104.4 million tonnes in 2025, or 19% of the total transported. Rumo, the logistics arm of Cosan, leads this segment with the North Network (Malha Norte), which connects Mato Grosso to the Port of Santos and has become the main outflow route for soy and corn from the Midwest. The company, however, will return the West Network (Malha Oeste) this year, whose contract expires in June and has not carried freight since November.

The new projects precisely seek to expand agribusiness’s share and diversify export routes. The East-West Corridor plans to link, over 2,400 kilometers, the West-East Integration Railway (Fiol) and the Midwest Integration Railway (Fico), connecting producing areas of the Midwest to the ports of Bahia. Fico is being executed by Vale as a counterpart to the extension of its concessions, while Fiol has one section already granted and another under construction as a public work.

Ferrogrão is the portfolio’s most challenging project. With 933 kilometers between Sinop (MT) and Itaituba (PA), the railway faces environmental and engineering risks that have stalled its progress for years. The route was designed to create an intermodal corridor: grains would arrive by rail at Itaituba and from there proceed via waterways to the ports of the country’s North.

The extension of the North-South Railway, in turn, will add 477 kilometers from Açailândia (MA) to the Port of Vila do Conde, in Barcarena (PA). Currently, the North-South connects to Vale’s Carajás Railway, but the line is saturated with ore and offers little spare capacity for grains from Matopiba. The new branch creates an independent logistical alternative for the outflow of agricultural production from the Northeast and North.

On the regulatory front, the National Land Transport Agency (ANTT) is preparing the resumption of railway auctions, with the expectation of holding the first tender still in the second half of the year, as announced by <a href='https://www.gov.br/antt/pt-br/assuntos/ultimas-noticias/novos-caminhos-sobre-trilhos-antt-apresenta-avancos-para-o-futuro-das-ferrovias-brasileiras' target='_blank'>ANTT</a> during an event at B3, in São Paulo. Among the projects being structured is the corridor that will connect the Port of Vitória (ES) to the Port of Açu (RJ), expanding logistical options for ore and other bulk cargoes.

Early contract renewals are also progressing as an instrument of legal certainty for private investments. ANTT will forward to the Federal Court of Accounts the processes of VLI and the Centro-Atlântica Railway (FCA), and intends to conclude the renewal of the Tereza Cristina Network still this year. The mechanism of linked accounts, which reserves resources for contractual investments, and crossed investments, which redirect financial obligations to strategic projects, are regulatory innovations that the agency recently incorporated.

The director-general of ANTT, Guilherme Theo Sampaio, summed up the moment with a precise metaphor: it is the joy of seeing, in fact, the train getting on the tracks. The phrase captures the spirit of a country that, after decades of railway stagnation, is beginning to see in the rails a living technology of development and not a museological ruin of the past.

Intermodality is the principle guiding this new phase, overcoming the logic of isolated corridors and seeking integration among railways, waterways, roads, and ports. The government is negotiating with BNDES a special financing line with appropriate terms and a grace period during works, recognizing that a railway only starts to generate revenue when the tracks are operational. The high cost of implementation requires sophisticated financial engineering and significant state participation.

The Chinese presence behind the scenes is the most relevant geopolitical factor in this equation. Companies such as CCCC, which already participates in the São Luís Port in Maranhão, and CRRC, the world's largest train manufacturer and a partner in the São Paulo Intercity Train, are following the auction portfolio with declared interest in the Fico-Fiol corridor. The Minister of Transport, Renan Filho, confirmed that five Chinese companies are studying infrastructure projects in the country, although no concrete proposal has been formalized so far.

The geopolitics of grains explains Beijing's interest: a more efficient railway in Brazil reduces the final cost of the commodity that feeds China. From Brazil's perspective, the entry of Chinese capital can unlock projects that the domestic private sector lacks the stamina to finance alone, especially in a context where the cost of capital is high and the return on railways is slow. The regulatory challenge lies in calibrating the concessions so that foreign interest does not override the country's logistical sovereignty.

The broad gauge of 1,600 mm, standard on most Brazilian freight railways, allows for heavy-haul trains with six-axle Co-Co diesel-electric locomotives, capable of pulling kilometer-long trains at stable commercial speeds. This technical configuration is the backbone of a system that moves hundreds of millions of tonnes annually with an energy consumption per tonne-kilometer far lower than that of road transport. The energy efficiency of rails is a silent but compelling argument in favor of expanding the network.

The institutional legacy that ANTT claims—a State agenda that transcends governments—will be tested in the coming years as the auctions progress and the R$140 billion in investments foreseen for the eight projects materialize. The combination of state planning, Chinese capital, and modern regulation may finally break the inertia that kept Brazil as an essentially road-based country, with only 19% of agribusiness freight on rails and a railway network that still operates mostly in the service of ore.
