The ground-level power supply system resolves the impasse between electric innovation and the heritage listing of the Plano Piloto, ushering in a new phase for the capital's mobility.

Modernity is not abstract. It has ballast, gauge, energy, and direction.
This direction became evident in the technical details of the Brasília Light Rail Vehicle (VLT) project, which resolved a classic urban planning impasse. The challenge of electrifying mass transit without tarnishing the clean horizon of the Plano Piloto, listed by the National Institute of Historic and Artistic Heritage (IPHAN), seemed like an equation without an immediate answer.
The answer came buried in the ground. The ground-level power supply (APS) system eliminates the need for overhead catenary wires, those suspended cables that trace a metallic zigzag over any historic landscape.
The French technology, already in operation in cities like Bordeaux, powers the 39 trains, each 45 meters long, via a segmented third rail installed on the track. Each segment is energized only when the low-floor, fully articulated train fully covers that section, eliminating risks for pedestrians and vehicles crossing the roadway.
By dispensing with poles and overhead wiring, the Brasília VLT preserves the monumental scale envisioned by Lúcio Costa. The solution proves there is no need to choose between the energy efficiency of an electric mode and the integrity of a world-recognized cultural site.
The project connects technical facts to urban life with robust figures. There will be 24 stations distributed along 16 kilometers of W3 Sul and Norte, a backbone that can boost the population and economic density of the capital's center.
Each new train will have the capacity to carry between 400 and 560 passengers, operating with a silent and continuous flow. It is a change of scale that any articulated bus, however modern, cannot deliver in the same road space.
The Brasília project is part of a broader context of renewed federal investment. The capital's VLT is a piece of the strategy that, within the <a href="https://www.ocafezinho.com/2026/06/07/vlt-de-brasilia-revoluciona-transporte-urbano-com-inovacao-eletrica/" target="_blank">Novo PAC</a>, seeks to reverse decades of road-centric policies by prioritizing rail as a vector of sustainable development.
The financial logic was also designed for the long term. The network's management is modeled as a 30-year concession in a Public-Private Partnership (PPP) format, tying private investment to the quality of service provided for three decades.
It is an antidote to the chronic lack of maintenance that stifles the country's transport infrastructure. The long-term PPP aligns the operator's interests with the durability of the rolling stock and the predictability of the operation, a leap in maturity in public management.
The experience of the Rio VLT, which operated until 2023, provides valuable inputs for the Brasília project. The Rio de Janeiro mode transformed the landscape of Avenida Rio Branco and the historic center by eliminating visual and noise pollution, but depended on robust fare integration to reach its projected demand.
While the Rio VLT operated with 95,000 daily passengers, below the contractual projection of over 240,000, Brasília is born with the opportunity to calibrate the operational model. The design of integration with buses from the Eixo Rodoviário Sul and Norte, with the Metrô-DF, and with intermodal terminals will define the system's success or failure far beyond the electric power supply engineering.
São Paulo is also observing this trend of rehabilitating the center via rails. The São Paulo city government is developing the 'Bonde São Paulo' project, foreseeing two lines with approximately 12 kilometers of extension that will connect Bom Retiro to Brás.
The São Paulo capital plans to integrate its VLT with five bus terminals, nine metro stations, and two CPTM stations, demonstrating that the light rail vehicle has ceased to be a decorative element for tourists. In all these cities, the VLT acts as an inducer of requalification of public space, pushing away the absolute hegemony of the private car.
The great innovation in the Brasília project lies in demonstrating that technology can be a silent ally of preservation. The APS represents the definitive overcoming of the conflict between heavy electrical infrastructure and protected urban aesthetics.
Brasília's system, by eliminating the 'sky of wires' that would be unthinkable from the heritage listing perspective, offers itself as a full-scale laboratory for other Brazilian cities. Municipalities like Ouro Preto, Olinda, Salvador, and São Luís, which face the same dilemma between modernizing transport and protecting historic buildings, now have a technical precedent to follow.
The innovation of the Brasília VLT is not in going faster, but in knowing how to adapt to the territory's constraints without losing power. The underground power supply delivers to passengers the experience of a surface metro, silent and electric, without imposing on the city the aesthetic price of an intrusive aerial network.
Modernity, when it has well-calibrated ballast and gauge, does not need to destroy memory to build the future. The Brasília project, by making this courageous technical choice, shows that a country that invests in rails can, at last, reconcile progress and civilization.
