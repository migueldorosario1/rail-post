The reinvention of Rio's downtown passes through the discreet elegance of ground-level power supply.

Tracks are the material geometry of the future. And it was precisely this geometry that began to take root in Rio de Janeiro in the early hours of March 27, 2015, when the <a href='http://www.rio.rj.gov.br/web/guest/exibeconteudo?id=5267887' target='_blank'>City Hall of Rio</a> laid the first 400 meters of permanent way on General Luiz Mendes de Morais Street, in Santo Cristo. Each rail bar, 18 meters long, was manufactured to adapt to the curves of the route without fragile welds or coarse joints. What was born there was not a tourist tram, but a mass transit system designed to reorder mobility in the central and port region.

From the outset, the Carioca Light Rail project faced an obstacle that no excavator could solve: the heritage listing of the architectural and landscape ensemble of the Center. The solution could not hang overhead wires over colonial churches and century-old townhouses, and that's where Brazilian engineering dove headfirst into Ground-Level Power Supply, known by the acronym APS.

APS is a segmented third-rail system, installed on the surface, that only releases power when the vehicle is exactly over it. <a href='https://www.seesp.org.br/site/comunicacao/noticias/item/15341-vlt-modal-utilit-rio-tecnol-gico-e-n-o-agressivo-ao-ambiente' target='_blank'>According to CCR engineers</a>, the responsible concessionaire, an underground powerbox activates collector shoes only at the moment the car covers the segment, eliminating the risk of shock for pedestrians and automobiles.

This means the Light Rail glides through the Center and Port Zone without visible wires, as if it were an autonomous vehicle on live rails. The absence of overhead catenaries is not an aesthetic whim: it is a technical-legal requirement that enabled the mode to coexist with the listed historical heritage.

The teams excavated only half a meter to find shards from the time of Dom Pedro I on Constitution Street, where a 15-meter stretch of original paving was preserved and integrated into the route. The final concreting was carefully adjusted to the urbanization of each point, respecting the material memory of the city without sacrificing operational efficiency.

The operational results are impressive: each car, 2.65 meters wide and 2.17 meters high, carries up to 420 passengers, running at an average speed of 17 km/h. When all 46 planned stations along the 28 kilometers of total extension are in operation, daily capacity will reach 300,000 people, with headways ranging from 3 to 15 minutes depending on demand.

In case of powerbox supply failure, emergency battery banks guarantee autonomy to travel 400 meters, a distance more than sufficient for the vehicle to reach a safe point without interrupting traffic flow. This redundancy architecture stems from the choice of a constant recharging system, essential to sustain air conditioning in the Rio climate without compromising energy reserve.

The total investment of R$ 1.5 billion, under a Public-Private Partnership, funded not only the assembly of the permanent way with sleepers, signaling, and command cables, but also the construction of the Integrated Operations and Maintenance Center in Gamboa. The fare payment, integrated with Bilhete Único, adopts a voluntary validation system, trusting on passenger adherence.

The technology that debuted in Rio was not isolated. <a href='https://www.ocafezinho.com/2026/06/07/vlt-de-brasilia-revoluciona-transporte-urbano-com-inovacao-eletrica/' target='_blank'>In Brasília</a>, the W3 Light Rail project also adopted APS to respect the heritage listing of the Pilot Plan, with 39 trains of 45 meters capable of transporting between 400 and 560 passengers over 16 kilometers. The elimination of catenaries in the federal capital follows the same principle that protected the townhouses of Rio.

In São Paulo, the <a href='https://prefeitura.sp.gov.br/web/sp_urbanismo/w/prefeitura-conclui-estudo-urban%C3%ADstico-e-avan%C3%A7a-com-projeto-de-ve%C3%ADculo-leve-sobre-trilhos-vlt-para-o-centro-de-s%C3%A3o-paulo' target='_blank'>São Paulo Tram</a> is advancing with an urban planning study completed and a forecast to serve 134,000 people per day in the historic center, integrating with five bus terminals and nine metro stations. Although the São Paulo model is still discussing between electricity and hydrogen, the challenge is the same: requalify without removing character.

The Carioca Light Rail showed that APS technology is not an inaccessible European luxury, but a realistic and applicable engineering tool on Brazilian soil. Regenerative braking, low-floor bogies, and the absence of direct emissions complete a package that puts the rail back as the backbone of urban development.

This is not nostalgia for the trams of the past century, but a cold calculation of productivity, passenger density, and respect for heritage. The integration with the bus station, metro, ferries, Santos Dumont Airport, and BRTs creates a network that distributes people more intelligently than any isolated bus corridor could ever achieve.

The numbers confirm the relevance: with 32 cars, the current system already transports thousands of passengers per hour, connecting the financial center to the cultural corridor and Porto Maravilha. The bottom line is less urban noise, fewer collisions, more pedestrians, and a city that once again looks to the rail as synonymous with cutting-edge.

The Rio lesson is that preserving the past does not need to conflict with cutting-edge technology. It is enough that the energy comes from below, that the rail is treated as the geometry of the future, and that each 18-meter bar carries along the weight of memory and the lightness of progress.
