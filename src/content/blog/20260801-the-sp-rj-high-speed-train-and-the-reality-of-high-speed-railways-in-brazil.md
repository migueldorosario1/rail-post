High-speed railway modernity requires sophisticated engineering, but Brazil still struggles with technical and economic challenges to realize this dream.

Modernity is not abstract. It has ballast, gauge, energy, and direction. In the context of rail transport, the ambition for high speed represents a technological leap that demands specialized engineering and advanced infrastructure. While Brazil advances in freight railway projects, such as Fiol and North-South, the São Paulo–Rio de Janeiro high-speed train (TAV Brasil) remains a symbol of an unfulfilled promise. Not for lack of political will, but due to deep technical gaps, absence of specialized TAV engineering, and incompatibility between cost, economic viability, and the prevailing operational model.

The TAV Brasil project, with a maximum designed speed of 320 km/h, faces significant structural obstacles. Brazilian railway engineering has historically operated at maximum speeds between 80 km/h and 100 km/h, which implies a radical technological leap. To reach 320 km/h, the track geometry needs a minimum curve radius compatible with these speeds, requiring massive expropriations and deep tunnels through the Serra do Mar. These challenges are enormous and demand a technical capacity that the country is still developing.

There is no national experience in high-speed signaling (ETCS Level 2 or 3), stabilized overhead catenary for 320 km/h, or predictive maintenance of tracks for this regime. These technical gaps represent a significant barrier to the realization of the project. According to Cláudio Frischtak, economist and president of Inter.B International Business Consultancy, "Our historical experience involves maximum speeds between 80 km/h and 100 km/h. A high-speed train operates above 300 km/h, and the nature of the project, equipment, and components is completely different."

Law No. 14.273/2021 allowed private authorizations for railways, but it did not resolve the technical gaps in design, construction, and operation of TAV. TAV Brasil, the company responsible for the project, estimates an investment of around R$ 60 billion, with tickets estimated between R$ 300 and R$ 500. However, the economic viability of the project is questioned. "It is an extremely expensive project and very difficult to justify from an economic standpoint," says Frischtak. Brazil's fiscal scenario, marked by budgetary constraints, makes it even more challenging to obtain the necessary government subsidies to make the project viable.

The TAV Brasil project also faces environmental and legal challenges. The proposal includes four stations along the route, in the capitals São Paulo and Rio de Janeiro, as well as São José dos Campos (SP) and Volta Redonda (RJ). Integration with metros and urban trains is essential, but the environmental license is still under review. The Brazilian Institute of Environment and Renewable Natural Resources (Ibama) informed that the environmental licensing for the high-speed train is still in process, and the EIA/Rima document has not yet been submitted by TAV Brasil.

Thomaz Schoeffel, secretary-director of the Brazilian Railway Preservation Association (ABPF), highlights that the country has lost the capacity for works of this scale. "A project of this magnitude, if it gets off the ground, will face enormous technical challenges, such as crossing the Serra do Mar and complex urban interferences," he states. In Schoeffel's assessment, it would be necessary to bring in teams and know-how from abroad, which would increase the costs for implementation and maintenance.

Despite maintaining support for railway expansion, the Lula government does not foresee direct participation in financing the high-speed train. The execution will be entirely private, with BNDES acting as a financial agent, without direct funding from the Union. According to TAV Brasil, passenger operations should start in 2032, but the start of construction, which was announced for 2027, has been postponed to 2028.

Brazilian reality shows that, although there is ambition and potential, the realization of the SP-RJ high-speed train depends on overcoming technical, economic, and operational challenges. Meanwhile, the country continues to advance in freight railway projects, which, although less glamorous, have a significant impact on logistics and economic development. The National Railway Plan, with an investment of R$ 100 billion, foresees the concession of five major railway projects, such as Fiol, Fico, and the extension of the North-South, demonstrating the government's commitment to expanding the railway network.

Therefore, high-speed railway modernity in Brazil is still under construction. While the country advances in freight projects, the SP-RJ high-speed train remains a symbol of an unfulfilled promise, reflecting the complexity and inherent challenges in implementing cutting-edge technologies in an environment with technical and economic limitations. The ambition for high speed, although noble, requires a profound transformation and a joint effort to become a reality.
