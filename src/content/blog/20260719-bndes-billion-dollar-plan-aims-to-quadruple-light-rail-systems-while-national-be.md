The apostasy of Brazilian mobility lies not in the scarcity of visionary plans, but in the cynical abyss that separates the designer's line from the concrete roadbed that receives the tram.

When technique organizes space, society gains time to live. This maxim, which should guide any mobility policy, echoes with a bitter taste when analyzing the current state of Light Rail Vehicle (LRV) implementation in Brazil. The National Bank for Economic and Social Development (BNDES) has just revealed, in its National Urban Mobility Study, a colossal ambition aiming to quadruple the national network of BRTs, monorails, and light rail systems over the next three decades, leaping from the current 631 kilometers to an impressive 2,500 kilometers.

The total estimated investment to reconfigure mobility in 21 metropolitan regions, each home to more than one million inhabitants, reaches R$ 500 billion by 2054. In this map of projected financial abundance, light rail emerges as an extremely seductive financial and operational engineering alternative, with an implementation cost of approximately R$ 50 million per kilometer. For comparison, an underground metro system can consume up to R$ 1 billion to cover the same distance, as detailed by the BNDES study <a href="https://oglobo.globo.com/brasil/noticia/2025/08/04/estudo-do-bndes-aponta-que-brasil-precisa-dobrar-metro-e-quadruplicar-os-brts-e-vlts.ghtml" target="_blank">mapped by the newspaper O Globo</a>.

The technical logic is sound in advocating the complementarity between low- and high-capacity transport modes, using light rail for urban capillarity and the metro for major mass transit corridors. However, the gap between federal planning and local execution finds its cruelest metaphor in the VLT Carioca, which completed a decade of operation in 2025. This mode, which cost R$ 1.157 billion—with R$ 532 million from the PAC Mobilidade program and R$ 625 million structured via a Public-Private Partnership (PPP)—transformed the landscape of downtown Rio de Janeiro by eliminating vehicle traffic on Avenida Rio Branco, but struggles not to become a low-floor white elephant.

The data are stubborn in their accounting coldness: while the feasibility studies projected a demand of 243,777 passengers per business day when the four lines were fully operational, the current reality stagnates at just 95,000 daily users. The economic crisis, the emptying of downtown after the pandemic, and the delay in delivering the full system—completed only in April 2024—conspired to create this structural passenger deficit that corrodes the logic of any PPP. Silvia Bressan, unit director of VLT Carioca, recalls that the reference contract was modeled in a completely different socioeconomic context and that the recent recovery is anchored in new urban facilities, such as the Gentileza Intermodal Terminal.

Inaugurated in 2024, Gentileza injected 78,000 daily passengers into the integration between light rail and 23 bus and BRT lines, creating a capillarity hub for the port area. To definitively unlock the idleness of the bogies gliding along the 28 kilometers of network, Rio's City Hall implemented the Margaridas Single Ticket (BUM), allowing fare integration for passengers coming from the Baixada Fluminense with the downtown rail system. Meanwhile, the concessionaire tries to minimize revenue evasion—the notorious fare dodging—through fines of R$ 170 and the recent implementation of Pix payment at validators, a palliative for a system that operates from 5 a.m. to 11 p.m. without physical turnstiles, relying on random inspections in a sea of distracted people with headphones crossing the tracks oblivious to the bell of the 'little train'.

From an engineering standpoint, the equipment gliding smoothly along Rio's asphalt is a marvel of electric traction technology, partly powered by overhead catenary and on wireless sections by supercapacitors, eliminating the visual pollution of overhead wires in sensitive areas. The pantograph is activated only at specific moments, while the low-floor bogies just 30 centimeters from the ground ensure universal accessibility that the old trams never dreamed of. The planned expansion of the network—whether the promised extension to São Cristóvão or the hypothetical conversion of BRT corridors to rails—stumbles upon contractual amendments that, since 2016, have already removed sections from the scope, such as the 2.6 km connection to the Novo Rio Bus Station, generating multimillion-real revenue losses over the years.

The new urban order proposed by BNDES no longer tolerates the road improvisation that dominated the 20th century, and the study of 194 projects makes this clear by conditioning success on a policy of disincentivizing cars, such as reducing parking spaces and giving traffic signal priority to public transport. The optimistic scenario calculated by the development bank predicts that, with unified fare integration and a maximum 6% of workers' average income committed, the number of people served by high- and medium-capacity transit in Rio could jump 270%, from 1.6 million to 5.9 million users. It is a vision of a city where commuting time is not stolen by traffic but invested in productive life and leisure.

However, the lesson of the lost decade of VLT Carioca is a red alert for the euphoria of the promised R$ 500 billion. It is not enough to lay tracks on the asphalt and expect the city to magically densify around Cristiano Ottoni or Candelária stations, the busiest stops on the central network. The cheapest and most efficient tool, compared to the metro, demands tyrannical urban planning that forces verticalization and economic activity along its axis, or else the modern vehicle will glide empty while ride-hailing motorcycles continue to bleed the demand of public transport, which accumulated a 43% loss in passengers between 2014 and 2023. Without the political courage to reinvent land use upon the roadbed, BNDES plans risk becoming just another good intention paved in the hell of Brazilian bureaucracy and urban stagnation.
