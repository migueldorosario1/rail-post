The T-1 tunnel, excavated in just 12 months using a TBM, demonstrates how advanced technology is consolidating as an essential tool to overcome complex geologies in high-speed corridors.

Every great city reveals its intelligence by how it moves people. And when moving them requires drilling through mountains instead of going around them, engineering exposes its most sophisticated face.

That is exactly what happened in the Palghar district of the Indian state of Maharashtra, where the Mumbai–Ahmedabad High-Speed Rail (MAHSR) project has just recorded its second mountain tunnel breakthrough in less than a month. The T-1 tunnel, 454 meters long and 14.4 meters wide, was designed for bidirectional high-speed train circulation and had its excavation completed in just 12 months.

The feat takes on even more impressive contours when one looks at the schedule: the T-1 tunnel, located near Boisar, achieved its breakthrough on January 27, 2026. Two successful breakthroughs in a short period of time reveal a technical mastery that deserves meticulous attention.

The method employed in the excavation was the use of TBM (Tunnel Boring Machines), an approach that combines efficiency and safety, especially suited for irregular geological conditions. Unlike the NATM, which requires constant geological monitoring, TBMs operate with greater autonomy and speed, adapting to terrain variations as excavation progresses.

As pointed out by the <a href='https://www.megaproject.com/news/railway/mumbai-ahmedabad-bullet-train-project-achieves-second-mountain-tunnel-breakthrough-in-palghar' target='_blank'>specialized portal Megaproject</a>, the technique proved particularly suitable for the Palghar region, where geological formations are notoriously irregular and challenging. TBMs use flexible support systems that stabilize the tunnel structure without rigidifying it, allowing the surrounding rock itself to become an active part of the support system.

Three components form the backbone of this support system: shotcreting, rock bolts, and lattice girders. Shotcreting consists of spraying concrete at high velocity directly onto the freshly excavated surface, creating an immediate lining layer that prevents spalling.

Rock bolts, in turn, are steel rods anchored deep in the rock to stitch unstable geological layers and redistribute stresses. Lattice girders — lightweight metal trusses — serve as a temporary structural frame, offering mechanical support until the final lining is installed.

What makes TBMs particularly ingenious is their ability to operate in various types of rock and soil, reducing excavation time and increasing worker safety. With each advance of the excavation, the TBM sensors assess the rock behavior and immediately adjust the type and intensity of support required.

In the case of T-1, excavation was conducted simultaneously from both ends of the tunnel, with the teams meeting in the middle after 12 months of continuous work. This meeting — the breakthrough — is the moment that tests the precision of the topographic alignment and the quality of the executive planning.

India's Minister of Railways, Information and Broadcasting and Information Technology, Ashwini Vaishnaw, described the advance as a demonstration of speed, precision, and innovation. Vaishnaw also highlighted that a large part of the machines and advanced technologies used in the project are manufactured in India itself, reflecting the strengthening of domestic engineering capacity.

The MAHSR project as a whole advances on multiple fronts. By the end of 2025, approximately 120 kilometers of viaducts had already been completed, along with 17 river bridges and 12 major crossings, consolidating a transformation corridor that stretches for about 508 kilometers between Mumbai and Ahmedabad.

The Gujarat section is expected to start commercial operations next year, with extension to Thane planned for 2028 and arrival in Mumbai in 2029. The pace is impressive, but what really draws attention is the technical choice to tackle the mountains instead of bypassing them, something that is only justified with engineering of the highest level.

The intensive use of prestressed concrete viaducts over 120 kilometers reveals another dimension of the project: the pursuit of geometric stability in a country where thermal variations and humidity punish conventional structures. Each pillar, each span, and each expansion joint must be calculated to withstand not only the weight of trains at 320 km/h but also the dynamic forces generated by high-speed passage.

The Indian experience with TBM in high-speed tunnels carries valuable lessons for countries facing similar geological challenges in their own railway projects. Regions with irregular rock formations, heterogeneous soils, and rugged terrain find in this method a more flexible and often more economical alternative than traditional techniques.

What Palghar demonstrates, with its seven mountain tunnels at different stages of construction, is that precision drilling has ceased to be an insurmountable obstacle and become a manageable step in high-speed planning. The secret is not in a miraculous machine, but in the intelligent combination of consolidated techniques, constant geological monitoring, and teams capable of making quick decisions in the face of the unexpected.

When the first Indian bullet train crosses the mountains of Maharashtra, few passengers will realize the technical effort that made that straight and smooth path possible. But it is precisely in that invisibility that the greatness of engineering resides: making the extraordinary seem natural.
