While the world advances with 350 km/h trains, Brazil focuses on recovering its minimum railway transport capacity, prioritizing logistics and intermodality.

Every major city reveals its intelligence by how it moves people. However, in Brazil, the reality of the tracks is far from what is seen in countries like China, where high-speed trains already reach 350 km/h. Here, the current focus is to literally rebuild the railway base, prioritizing the recovery of the minimum cargo transport capacity, with average operational speeds between 40 km/h and 60 km/h. There is no high-speed passenger railway (HSR) line in operation, completed project, or tendered in Brazil. Even the Intercity Train SP-Campinas does not meet the technical standard for high speed. The true 'speed leap' in Brazil is institutional: from regulatory stagnation to the first railway authorization that has become a construction project, from road dependency to the strategic resumption of rail as a vector of logistical sovereignty.

In Inocência, Mato Grosso do Sul, a historic milestone for the sector was launched with the cornerstone of the Arauco Railway Branch. With an extension of 47 km, this branch will be operated under the 'short line' model, with conventional diesel-electric traction, without ETCS or CATS signaling. The expectation is to promote greater logistical efficiency, fewer trucks on the highways, cost reduction, more safety, and greater competitiveness for Brazil in the international market. Completion of the works is scheduled for the second half of 2027.

The Arauco Railway Branch project will connect the future Arauco factory, which will be the largest pulp industry in the world, to the national railway network. The estimated annual production of 3.5 million tons of pulp will travel by rail to Rumo's Northern Network and from there to the Port of Santos (SP), destined for the United States, Europe, and Asia. This is a concrete example of how the recovery and expansion of the cargo railway network can boost regional development and job creation.

VLI, one of the country's leading logistics companies, is also investing in the modernization of its fleet. The new locomotives acquired from Progress Rail, of the EMD SD70ACe series, are designed for heavy haulage on curves and gradients typical of the Brazilian network, with 4,300 HP power and a maximum speed of 120 km/h. However, these locomotives will be operated in a freight regime with a functional limit of 65 km/h, reinforcing the country's priority in improving capacity and operational efficiency rather than seeking high speeds.

Railway authorization (Law 11.442/2007) has been fundamental in making these projects viable. The National Land Transport Agency (ANTT) has played a crucial role in regulation, supervision, and enabling solutions that impact people's real lives, promoting the country's socioeconomic development. According to Guilherme Theo Sampaio, General Director of ANTT, "This is the first railway authorization that has moved from paper to reality. It is a public policy built with strategic alignment, regulation, management, and supervision, delivering development, logistical efficiency, and progress."

Intermodality, or the combination of different modes such as railways, waterways, and roads, is another fundamental aspect of Brazilian logistics. Projects like the East-West Railway, which aims to connect producing areas in the Midwest to the ports of Bahia, and the extension of the North-South Railway, which seeks to expand the network northward to Barcarena, in Pará, are examples of how the integration of different means of transport can improve the efficiency and competitiveness of the sector.

Furthermore, the Union is funding part of the necessary investments to ensure the economic viability of these ventures. According to the Minister of Transport, Renan Filho, the government has already closed agreements with Rumo and MRS Logística, and announced an understanding with Vale. These agreements aim at the renegotiation of concessions, allowing the extension of contracts and the realization of new investments.

The National Railway Plan, which depends only on final adjustments to be launched, foresees the concession of five major railway projects to the private sector. In total, almost five thousand kilometers of new railways and an estimated investment of approximately R$ 100 billion. The Union should assume 20% to 30% of the investments, depending on the project, to make the railways viable.

However, state financing, whether through BNDES or through international partners such as China, is essential to get these projects off the ground. China, for example, has already shown interest in investing in infrastructure in Brazil, especially in projects that can improve logistics and food security. Chinese companies, such as CCCC and CRRC, are studying railway and road projects in the country, according to statements by Minister George Santoro.

In summary, while the world advances with high-speed trains, Brazil is focusing its efforts on rebuilding its railway base, prioritizing the recovery and expansion of the cargo network. The priority is logistical efficiency, intermodality, and economic viability, preparing the country for a more integrated and competitive future. After all, the true speed of Brazil on the tracks is the speed of development and logistical sovereignty.
