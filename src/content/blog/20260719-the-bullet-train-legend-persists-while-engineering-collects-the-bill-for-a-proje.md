The dream of the São Paulo-Rio TAV, which has already consumed almost two decades in promises and announcements, remains a Brazilian railway myth. With an updated cost of R$60 billion and no environmental licensing, the project has become more of an illusion than a viable reality.

The rails are the material geometry of the future. However, in Brazil's most populous axis, between São Paulo and Rio de Janeiro, what has materialized so far are only announcements, digital mock-ups, and electoral promises repeated for almost two decades.

The High-Speed Train (TAV) designed to cover the 417 km separating the two capitals at up to 320 km/h remains the greatest icon of what engineering dreamed and Brazilian economic reality rejected. Four stations are planned along the route: São Paulo, São José dos Campos, Volta Redonda, and Rio de Janeiro.

A travel time of under two hours seduced generations of planners and politicians, but a look at the environmental licensing process exposes the ghostly nature of the undertaking. The Environmental Impact Study and Report (EIA/Rima), a fundamental document for any major project, has not yet been submitted to Ibama, as reported by the newspaper <a href="https://www.gazetadopovo.com.br/brasil/promessa-do-pt-obras-adiadas-trem-bala-sao-paulo-rio-de-janeiro/" target="_blank">Gazeta do Povo</a>.

The project's fragility contrasts with the ambition stated in the documents submitted to the National Land Transport Agency (ANTT). Each technical term carries cost and complexity implications that the Brazilian railway history simply does not master. The country has never operated a passenger train above 160 km/h, and its engineering is calibrated for heavy freight transport at commercial speeds between 80 and 100 km/h.

Economist Cláudio Frischtak, president of Inter.B Consultoria Internacional de Negócios, recalled that only four countries have real experience with high-speed trains over long distances: China, France, Japan, and Spain. In Brazil, the required technological leap would be radical, starting with the crossing of the Serra do Mar and the complex urban interferences in the metropolitan fabric of São Paulo.

The estimated cost of R$60 billion does not foresee direct public subsidy, but the government's official position has changed dramatically over the years. The Minister of Transport, Renan Filho, declared in 2023 that the government would cooperate with part of the necessary amount, completing whatever was missing to make the project viable.

Now, the Ministry states that the TAV “is not the government’s” and that funding will be private, with BNDES acting only as a financial agent. The change in discourse reflects the harshness of a permanent fiscal crisis and the absence of any social rate of return that justifies Treasury contributions of this magnitude.

The consortium planned with European and Chinese companies is cited as a trump card, but importing specialized engineering makes implementation more expensive and creates long-term technological dependence. The director-secretary of the Brazilian Association of Railway Preservation (ABPF), Thomaz Schoeffel, was categorical: Brazil does not have railway engineering prepared for this, and even the operating subways are not genuinely Brazilian.

In the vacuum of this technical inability, a more rational alternative was discarded during the Dilma Rousseff government. It was a medium-speed train, operating at approximately 150 km/h during the day and sharing tracks with freight trains at night, with journeys of about three hours between the capitals.

The model would allow two different concessionaires — one for passengers, another for freight — and would drastically reduce implementation costs by taking advantage of the modernization of the existing line. This operational architecture, common in medium-density European corridors, was rejected by the Workers' Party government in favor of the full high-speed version.

The history of unfulfilled promises fuels suspicions that border on folklore. In 2007, the Lula government announced the TAV as part of the Growth Acceleration Program (PAC) and projected the start of operations for 2014, in time for the football World Cup.

In 2011, already under Dilma, the High-Speed Rail Transport Company (Etav) was created, later renamed the Planning and Logistics Company (EPL) after successive auction failures. The estimated cost already exceeded R$30 billion, and the project was shelved without execution during the fiscal crisis of 2015 and 2016.

Now, in his third term, President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva sees the work transferred to 2028, with operations beginning only in 2032. Modal integration with subways and urban trains, foreseen in the plans presented to ANTT, depends on investments that no sphere of government is willing to fund in the short term.

Meanwhile, Brazil watches the advancement of railway projects that actually get off the ground. The National Railway Plan, with an investment of R$100 billion and expansion of 4.7 thousand kilometers of network, focuses on freight corridors such as the East-West and the extension of the North-South, as reported by <a href="https://www.cnnbrasil.com.br/economia/macroeconomia/com-investimento-de-r-100-bi-governo-lancara-plano-nacional-de-ferrovias/" target="_blank">CNN Brasil</a>.

The first railway authorization granted by ANTT that effectively started construction is a 47-kilometer branch line in Inocência (MS), intended to transport pulp to the Port of Santos. The <a href="https://www.gov.br/antt/pt-br/assuntos/ultimas-noticias/brasil-nos-trilhos-do-desenvolvimento-antt-viabiliza-primeira-autorizacao-ferroviaria-que-sai-do-papel-e-impulsiona-nova-fase-da-logistica-nacional" target="_blank">ANTT</a> celebrated the milestone as a concrete step toward more efficient logistics, but the contrast with the TAV is brutal: there, demand exists, financing is solved, and engineering is mastered by the contractor.

Insistence on the bullet train between São Paulo and Rio de Janeiro without solid technical and environmental backing risks immortalizing it as an urban legend of Brazilian politics. Eighteen years after becoming a PT campaign promise, the project accumulates a new postponement and zero kilometers of rails laid.

The geometry of the future continues drawn in PowerPoint presentations and ministerial statements, but the Brazilian ground remains untouched. And with each new announcement without an environmental license, the credibility of high-speed in the country dissolves a little more in the fog of real estate speculation and political marketing.
