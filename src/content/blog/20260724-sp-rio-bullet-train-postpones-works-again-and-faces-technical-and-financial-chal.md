The R$60 billion project accumulates 18 years of promises, now without federal subsidy, while national engineering deals with the challenge of jumping from 80 to 320 km/h.

<p>Every great city reveals its intelligence by the way it moves people. The phrase, attributed to Danish urbanist Jan Gehl, has never been as cutting as when applied to the longest-lived and most fanciful mobility project in Brazil, the High-Speed Train (TAV) between São Paulo and Rio de Janeiro. The São Paulo megacity and Rio's showcase remain connected by a saturated air corridor and a Presidente Dutra highway that often collapses under heavy logistics.</p>
<p>The idea of overcoming them on rails at 320 km/h has dragged on for eighteen years as a PT campaign promise that never found rest on a construction site. The Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva government, which in 2007 included the TAV in the Growth Acceleration Program (PAC), now transfers the financial lead to the private sector, arguing that the project 'is not the government's'.</p>
<p>The updated cost of the project hovers around R$60 billion, an amount entirely to be sought in the market. The basic route envisages 417 kilometers in length with four stations: the two capitals, plus São José dos Campos and Volta Redonda. The promise of covering the stretch in less than two hours rests on a maximum speed of 320 km/h, something absolutely unprecedented in railway engineering practiced in Brazil.</p>
<p>Our current technical park operates mostly with freight trains between 80 and 100 km/h, which represents a brutal technological gap for a real high-speed system, as pointed out by the <a href="https://www.gazetadopovo.com.br/brasil/promessa-do-pt-obras-adiadas-trem-bala-sao-paulo-rio-de-janeiro/" target="_blank">Gazeta do Povo portal</a> in a recent report.</p>
<p>The economist and president of Inter.B Consultoria Internacional de Negócios, Cláudio Frischtak, stresses that the nature of equipment and components of a TAV is completely different from that of heavy trains that carry ore and grains in the interior. The country does not even master the engineering of its own subways, which, at most, are assembled on national territory.</p>
<p>The crossing of the Serra do Mar and the complex urban interferences between the two metropolises are geotechnical bottlenecks of monumental scale, which would require massive importation of specialized European or Chinese know-how, making the implementation and maintenance of the system more expensive in a spiral of external dependence.</p>
<p>The regulatory situation of the project adds layers of uncertainty. The environmental licensing remains stalled in the preliminary phase, without the Environmental Impact Study and Environmental Impact Report (EIA/Rima) having been filed with the Brazilian Institute of the Environment and Renewable Natural Resources (Ibama), which has already formally requested the document. Without the EIA/Rima, there is no prior license, and without it, the talk of starting works slips again on the schedule, now postponed to 2028, with commercial operation perhaps in 2032.</p>
<p>The Railways Law (Law 14.273/2021), sanctioned in the previous government, unlocked the authorization grant model and paved the way for this private proposal, but detached the viability from the lap of the State. The financial vacuum is the critical point of the equation. The National Bank for Economic and Social Development (BNDES) will act as structuring agent, but there will be no input of resources from the National Treasury nor any direct federal subsidy so far.</p>
<p>The structuring of an international consortium to fund R$60 billion in a country with permanent fiscal crisis and no tradition in the mode requires a rate of return that the project by itself hardly delivers. Frischtak is categorical in stating that the economic viability is very difficult to justify without intense real estate development around the stations or without a hybrid revenue model that the current notice does not clearly contemplate.</p>
<p>There is a technically less glamorous but rational alternative, which was discarded by the Dilma Rousseff administration and returns to the debate among experts: the medium-speed train, operating at about 150 km/h during the day and sharing the track with freight trains at night. This bimodal model, with interoperability between passenger and freight concessionaires, would require the modernization of the existing railway line, with drastically lower implementation costs and a technological leap assimilable by national engineering.</p>
<p>A three-hour journey between the two capitals, in this format, would eliminate the dependence on large public subsidies and create a high value-added logistical backbone, with full use of the infrastructure 24 hours a day. The insistence on the direct leap to 320 km/h, without intermediate stages of technological learning, challenges the logic of industrial development that the Brazilian railway agenda tries to rescue with the National Railway Plan and the early contract renewals.</p>
<p>While the country resumes auctions of large freight corridors and structures the exit to the Pacific, the TAV remains as a showcase of a desire that the geofiscal and geotechnical reality stubbornly refuses to validate. Losing the chance to implement a functional and integrated medium-speed solution, in the name of the bullet train utopia, is to indefinitely postpone the urban intelligence that Gehl advocated for large cities.</p>
