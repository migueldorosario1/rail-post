The contrast between the ambition of mega railway projects and the paralysis of the high-speed train reveals a country that dreams of modernity but hesitates in the face of everyday engineering.

Every major city reveals its intelligence by how it moves people. No other urban connection in Brazil embodies this test better than the São Paulo–Rio de Janeiro axis, where 417 kilometers separate the two largest metropolises in the country, and where the high-speed train remains an illusion eighteen years after it was announced.

While the federal government signs memorandums with China for a transcontinental railway from Ilhéus to the Pacific and prepares a National Railway Plan worth R$ 100 billion, the sector's most emblematic project — the TAV Brasil — accumulates delays, lacks environmental licensing, and has a revealing confession: the work “is not the government’s.” According to <a href='https://www.gazetadopovo.com.br/brasil/promessa-do-pt-obras-adiadas-trem-bala-sao-paulo-rio-de-janeiro/' target='_blank'>a report by Gazeta do Povo</a>, the start of construction, previously scheduled for 2027, has been officially moved to 2028.

TAV Brasil, the company authorized by ANTT to build and operate the high-speed train for up to 99 years under the Railway Law, now projects the start of commercial operations only for 2032. The 417-kilometer route promises a maximum speed of 320 km/h and a travel time of less than two hours, with tickets estimated between R$ 300 and R$ 500.

Four stations are planned — São Paulo, São José dos Campos, Volta Redonda, and Rio de Janeiro — but the updated cost of R$ 60 billion does not have guaranteed public financing. In March 2024, IBAMA requested the EIA/Rima, and the environmental study has not yet been delivered, keeping the licensing suspended.

The government of President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, which in 2023 signaled direct participation in financing, now states that execution will be entirely private. BNDES would act only as a financial agent, without funding from the Union, a change in stance that contrasts with decades of PT promises.

The historical maximum speed of Brazilian freight railways — between 80 km/h and 100 km/h — gives a measure of the technological leap required. Economist Cláudio Frischtak, interviewed for the report, recalls that countries like China, France, Japan, Spain, Germany, Italy, South Korea, and Turkey dominate high-speed trains over large distances.

“Brazil does not have railway engineering prepared for this,” said Thomaz Schoeffel, director of the Brazilian Association of Railway Preservation. For experts, the project would require massive importation of knowledge and equipment, further increasing implementation costs.

While the high-speed train stumbles, the government is preparing the National Railway Plan with 4,700 kilometers of new tracks and an investment of approximately R$ 100 billion. The Union should assume 20% to 30% of the funds to make the projects viable, as <a href='https://www.cnnbrasil.com.br/economia/macroeconomia/com-investimento-de-r-100-bi-governo-lancara-plano-nacional-de-ferrovias/' target='_blank'>reported by CNN Brasil</a>.

The menu includes the East-West Corridor, connecting Fiol and Fico in 2,400 kilometers from Ilhéus to the Central-West, and the extension of the North-South Railway by another 477 kilometers to the port of Vila do Conde. Also included are the Southeast Railway Ring, linking Vitória to Itaboraí in 300 kilometers, and the Ferrogrão, with 933 kilometers between Sinop and Itaituba, whose studies are underway following a request from the STF.

In the same horizon, Brazil and China took a strategic step with the signing of a memorandum of understanding to study the Atlantic-Pacific transcontinental railway, as reported by <a href='https://g1.globo.com/economia/noticia/2025/07/08/ferrovia-para-ligar-o-atlantico-ao-pacifico-veja-o-que-se-sabe-ate-agora-do-projeto-do-brasil-com-a-china.ghtml' target='_blank'>G1</a>. The agreement, signed between Infra S.A. and the China Railway Economic Research and Planning Institute, has an initial duration of five years and envisages crossing the states of Bahia, Goiás, Mato Grosso, Rondônia, and Acre to the Peruvian port, possibly Ilo or Bayóvar.

Projections from the Peruvian government indicate that the new route could reduce the transport time from Brazil to Asia from 40 to 28 days. However, construction still depends on technical, economic, and environmental feasibility studies, with no completion date or start of works set.

The contrast between the slowness of the high-speed train and the ambition of the cargo corridors exposes a known wound in Brazilian railway planning: the almost absolute priority given to commodity transport and the chronic difficulty in making medium and high-speed passenger trains viable. Frischtak recalls that a more realistic alternative — a medium-speed train at 150 km/h, with shared nighttime use for cargo — was dismissed by then-President Dilma Rousseff.

Meanwhile, the 2026 agenda of the Ministry of Transport includes eight railway auctions, combining concessions, early renewals, and re-tendering, as detailed in <a href='https://www.cnnbrasil.com.br/infra/com-foco-em-ferrovias-governo-avanca-nos-primeiros-projetos/' target='_blank'>another report by CNN Brasil</a>. The West Network, the South Network, and the FCA are among the contracts that may be renewed, and the Minas Rio Corridor should have its notice published in the first half of the year.

The Ferrogrão, following the update of studies at the request of the STF, and the Southeast Railway Ring emerge as the most anticipated projects. If the schedule is met, 2026 will mark the greatest movement in the sector since the auction of Fiol 1 in 2021, but interlocutors already admit that two auctions would be a satisfactory result.

Therefore, Brazil mobilizes sophisticated instruments — public calls, shortlines, railway authorizations — to reorganize its cargo network and envisions unprecedented continental integration with China. However, the train that should embody the intelligence of daily movement between São Paulo and Rio continues to be the most eloquent metaphor for the railways the country does not build.
