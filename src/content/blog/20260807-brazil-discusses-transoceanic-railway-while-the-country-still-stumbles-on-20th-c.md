The partnership with China reignites the dream of a bioceanic railway, but the gulf between strategic planning and the reality of works exposes the Brazilian difficulty in treating tracks as a living technology for development.

<p>When technique organizes space, society gains time to live. It is a maxim that should be a blade to cut the inertia of a country that has historically turned its back on its railways, and that now tries, in partnership with China, to sew the Atlantic to the Pacific with steel and ballast.</p>

<p>The memorandum of understanding signed between Infra S.A., linked to the Ministry of Transport, and the China Railway Economic Research and Planning Institute, in July 2025, puts on the table the possibility of a railway departing from Bahia and crossing Goiás, Mato Grosso, Rondônia, and Acre to reach the Peruvian port of Chancay, inaugurated in 2024 by Chinese President Xi Jinping. Peruvian government projections indicate a drastic reduction in cargo travel time between continents, <a href="https://g1.globo.com/economia/noticia/2025/07/07/brasil-e-china-assinam-memorando-para-planejamento-ferroviario.ghtml" target="_blank">from 40 to 28 days</a>, a logistical shortening that disrupts the traditional geography of foreign trade.</p>

<p>National Secretary of Railway Transport, Leonardo Ribeiro, defined the agreement as a strategic step, but the announcement carries the weight of a promise that already failed between 2015 and 2016, when similar studies were shelved. Now, the argument is that Brazil has developed in infrastructure and can take advantage of existing networks to make the route feasible, an attempt to tame the brutality of the numbers: each kilometer of new railway costs, on average, R$27 million, more than triple that of an equivalent highway.</p>

<p>The country that plans a transoceanic railway is the same that, in 2025, carried 406.6 million tons of iron ore on its lines, <a href="https://investnews.com.br/negocios/brasil-tenta-de-novo-expandir-as-ferrovias-mas-ainda-falta-combinar-com-os-chineses/" target="_blank">a mastodonic 73% of all national railway freight</a>, while soybeans and corn, the engines of the trade balance, represented only 19% of the volume transported. Brazil's rail infrastructure is essentially a mineral pipeline for Vale and a grain route for Rumo, a system that reflects the historical choice for extractive logistics, rather than a network designed to organize the territory and urban life.</p>

<p>The vertical integration of the mining business — mine, railway, and port under the same control — gave Vale the muscle to move 301 million tons last year, but it stifled the diversification of the mode. Outside this circuit, Brazil has only two interstate passenger services, both operated by the mining company under contractual obligation: the Vitória-Minas Railway and the Carajás Railway, remnants of an era when trains still carried people, not just commodities.</p>

<p>The National Railways Plan, with a pipeline of R$100 billion and nearly five thousand kilometers of new tracks, <a href="https://www.cnnbrasil.com.br/economia/macroeconomia/com-investimento-de-r-100-bi-governo-lancara-plano-nacional-de-ferrovias/" target="_blank">attempts to break this logic by providing for projects such as the East-West Corridor</a>, the extension of the North-South line to the port of Vila do Conde, and the Southeast Railway Ring, connecting Vitória to Itaboraí. The Ferrogrão, between Sinop and Itaituba, remains the most challenging project, stuck in environmental and legal disputes, although the Supreme Federal Court recently validated the law that altered the boundaries of the Jamanxim National Park.</p>

<p>The relentless mathematics of railways, with returns measured in decades and the need for significant state subsidies, pushes the government toward international capital, especially Chinese, as then-Executive Secretary George Santoro pointed out. Five Chinese companies, including CCCC, already established in the Port of São Luís, and manufacturer CRRC, partner in the São Paulo-Campinas Intercity Train and about to open a factory in Araraquara, are studying projects in the country, but the first auction scheduled for September still lacks a published notice.</p>

<p>Chinese interest is not geopolitical philanthropy: with 70% of the soybeans and corn that Beijing imports coming from Brazil, fixing Brazilian internal logistics is a matter of food security for Xi Jinping's government, which operates through state-owned companies like Cofco. The reasoning is that, even if the railway itself does not provide a direct financial return, more efficient transportation reduces the final product cost for the big buyer, in a state-level calculation that Brazil still hesitates to make.</p>

<p>Meanwhile, the bullet train between São Paulo and Rio de Janeiro, budgeted at R$60 billion, has entered the list of urban legends: the start of works, already postponed to 2028, remains without an environmental license, without defined subsidies, and with the government stating that the project is not theirs. Economist Cláudio Frischtak, from Inter.B Consultoria, summarizes the skepticism by recalling that Brazil has never built a high-speed train, operates with railway engineering limited to freight speeds of 80 km/h to 100 km/h, and would have to import practically all the know-how to leap to the promised 320 km/h.</p>

<p>A more rational alternative, a medium-speed train (150 km/h) with nighttime shared track use for freight, was discarded by former President Dilma Rousseff, leaving the country without an intermediate technological step between heavy freights and the dream of the TAV (High-Speed Train). National railway engineering, which has already lost the construction capacity for works of this magnitude, would face monumental challenges such as crossing the Serra do Mar mountain range and complex urban interferences, raising costs that already challenge the project's economic viability.</p>

<p>The transoceanic railway, the Ferrogrão, the East-West Corridor, and the Intercity Train itself represent pieces of a chessboard where Brazil tries to move from the condition of an exporter of raw materials transported by trucks to that of a country that uses rails as a technology of sovereignty. The partnership with China offers the capital and experience of those who deployed the largest high-speed network on the planet, but the decision to treat rails as development infrastructure, and not as a campaign promise, still needs to be made in Brasília.</p>

<p>The gap between the signed memorandum and the first laid track will be filled by the Brazilian capacity to plan with technical density, and not just with diplomatic enthusiasm, the seam between the oceans that geography made possible and logistics made urgent. If technique organizes space, as the maxim states, the time that Brazilian society loses waiting for trains that do not come is the cruelest measure of our backwardness.</p>
