The expansion of railway logistics is essential for economic development, cost reduction, and emissions. Projects in Australia, Turkey, and South America offer valuable lessons for Brazil.

Every major city reveals its intelligence by how it moves people. This statement can be extended to cargo logistics, where efficiency and the ability to move large volumes are crucial for economic development. Countries that prioritize railway infrastructure are standing out on the global stage, with mega projects underway in Australia, Turkey, and South America, demonstrating the strategic importance of rail tracks.

In Australia, the Inland Rail represents one of the largest logistical bets of the century. The 1,600-kilometer corridor aims to connect Melbourne to Brisbane, with capacity for double-stacked trains, reducing cargo travel time. The project, valued at around 31 billion Australian dollars, is compared by analysts to a Suez Canal on rails. The central idea is to displace stacked containers, cutting through the interior and relieving saturated highways. Each freight train can replace more than a hundred trucks, reducing emissions and freeing up roads for lower-impact uses, while meeting the projected growth in demand.

The Inland Rail requires complex engineering interventions. Bridges have been rebuilt, tunnels adapted, and signaling modernized to allow the safe passage of double-stacked containers. In Broadford, an entire bridge was demolished and erected in forty hours, demonstrating the level of precision required to update inherited industrial infrastructure. The initial forecasted cost has risen to 31 billion dollars, with unstable soils, additional bridges, and global inflation explaining the increase, but the government maintains its commitment to priority sections. The Stockinbingal to Parkes segment records concrete advances, with ongoing tests, while the complete project still faces litigation with rural landowners and scope adjustments.

Australia sees this railway as an axis of continental competitiveness. The corridor integrates the inland agribusiness with the Pacific ports and reinforces the country's export capacity. While Australia advances in its territory, Turkey is building its own railway revolution. The Istanbul North Rail Crossing Project creates 127 kilometers of line that crosses the strait, using the Yavuz Sultan Selim Bridge. The 2 billion dollar investment, with World Bank financing, is expected to increase cargo transport capacity from 3 million to 50 million tons annually, representing a multiplication of over sixteen times.

The project diverts heavy traffic from the center of Istanbul, strengthening Turkey's position as a logistics hub on the modern Silk Road, connecting Europe, Central Asia, and the Middle East. Half of the route will be built in tunnels, protecting operations against extreme heat, floods, and strong winds, ensuring continuous 24-hour operation. The World Bank estimates the creation of 414,000 direct and indirect jobs, including technical training programs open to women, reinforcing the inclusive nature of the initiative.

Turkey seeks to reduce its traditional dependence on trucks. High-capacity electric trains promise to cut emissions and attract investments in logistics and manufacturing. The parallel between the two countries reveals a strategic lesson: governments that prioritize efficient logistics corridors integrate territories and eliminate bottlenecks that limit economic growth. Double-stacked freight trains double the capacity per composition. Combined with digital control and progressive electrification, they generate scale gains impossible in road transport.

These experiences resonate in Brazil, where the logistics matrix is still excessively road-based, with high costs and chronic highway saturation. Multimodal integration initiatives are gaining space on the federal agenda. The project that envisions a waterway along the São Francisco River, combined with railway sections, illustrates the effort towards modernization. Brazil's challenge remains in scale and continuity. While Australia and Turkey execute works with defined targets, Brazil needs to overcome institutional fragmentation and accelerate regulatory decisions.

A promising example in Brazil is the megaproject by the Petrocity group, which creates a railway complex linking the Brazilian Central-West to the port of São Mateus in Espírito Santo, crossing 41 municipalities in Minas Gerais. The project, which will take 10 years to complete, aims to serve mineral and steel production, as well as to transport grains and minerals destined for the international market. José Roberto Barbosa da Silva, president of Petrocity, informed that all costs are being covered by investors, without relying on public funds or BNDES.

Another large-scale project is the Longotoma Bioceanic Corridor, which seeks to connect Mendoza, Argentina, to the Chilean coast of Valparaíso. The consortium formed by the Chilean company Beler SA and the Singaporean company International Nusantara Investment plans an estimated investment of 9.6 billion dollars. The project includes the construction of a 54-kilometer railway tunnel, a 420-kilometer double-track electrified railway line, a multimodal cargo center in Longotoma, and a deep-water submarine port in the Chilean commune of La Ligua.

According to its creators, the integration of these technologies aims to consolidate a low-emission corridor that combines operational efficiency with environmental sustainability criteria. The goal is clear: to make Chile one of the main gateways to the Pacific Ocean for South American agricultural production destined for Asia. According to consortium estimates, Argentina and Brazil export more than 380 million tons of corn, soybeans, and wheat annually, of which approximately 79% are destined for China and other economies in the Asia-Pacific region.

Proponents of the project argue that the combination of a multimodal center, railway, and maritime terminal will reduce transit times, logistics costs, and diversify the export routes of South America to the Pacific. The use of a double-track electrified line aims to reduce dependence on fossil fuels and decrease the carbon footprint of cargo transport between the two countries.

In Brazil, where agribusiness still depends on trucks traveling thousands of kilometers, the message is clear. Without a dense and modern railway network, there is no sustainable competitiveness. The State must plan and execute these works as a national priority to break with dependent and inefficient patterns. The tracks represent a strategic choice for development. Countries that build them today define their position on the economic map of the next decades.

According to the agency <a href='https://www.o-cafezinho.com/'>O Cafezinho</a>, the future of global cargo is being built on tracks. Sensors, dispatch algorithms, and electrified systems transform the rail mode into the basis of a low-carbon economy. The energy efficiency of trains offers predictability that roads cannot replicate. Huge volumes circulate with reduced environmental impact, strengthening the logistical sovereignty of nations. Australia and Turkey demonstrate that investment in railway infrastructure is an investment in autonomy.
