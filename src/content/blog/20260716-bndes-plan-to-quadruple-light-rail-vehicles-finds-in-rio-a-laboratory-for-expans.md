The Rio de Janeiro VLT marks a decade as a technical and urbanistic reference, while the city government proposes a new line and the replacement of BRT corridors with rails, aligned with the national goal of 2,500 km of network.

Light rail vehicles (VLTs) are more efficient than trains for urban use, summarized Felipe Borim, Superintendent of the Infrastructure Area at BNDES, during the presentation of the National Urban Mobility Study. The document mapped 194 projects in 21 metropolitan regions and projects quadrupling the network of Light Rail Vehicles, BRTs and monorails in Brazil by 2054, with an estimated investment of R$ 500 billion.

The current network of these modes totals 631 kilometers and will need to reach 2,500 kilometers to meet future demand. The metro, which costs up to R$ 1 billion per kilometer, should increase from 376 to 699 kilometers. With a much lower implementation cost—about R$ 50 million per kilometer for a BRT—the combination of rail modes and segregated corridors emerges as the financially viable backbone of urban mobility.

Rio de Janeiro, which in June celebrated ten years of operation of the VLT carioca, emerges as a concrete laboratory of this national guideline. The system, operated by the concessionaire VLT Carioca, has 28 kilometers of track, four lines and 32 trains in daily operation from 5am to 11pm.

Despite the urban transformation in the city center, with Avenida Rio Branco dedicated to pedestrians and the mode, current demand of 95,000 passengers per working day is far from the original contractual projection of 243,777 users. The Covid-19 pandemic worsened this decline, but the inauguration of the Gentileza Intermodal Terminal, handling 78,000 people daily, and the creation of the Margaridas Single Ticket (BUM), integrating passengers from the Baixada Fluminense to the VLT, indicate recovery.

In this context, the Rio city government sent to the City Council, in August 2025, an ambitious bill to expand the service and change the level of medium-capacity transport in the city. The plan includes a new line connecting the Port Zone to São Cristóvão, connecting to the old Leopoldina Station and the metro and train stations of the imperial neighborhood.

The conceptual route covers points of interest such as the Luiz Gonzaga Center for Northeastern Traditions, the Quinta D'Or Hospital, Quinta da Boa Vista and the Biopark, as detailed by the bill submitted by Mayor Eduardo Paes. The São Cristóvão region gained strategic weight by integrating the urban operation that enabled the revitalization of the Port Zone and allows the use of Certificates of Potential Additional Construction (Cpacs) to leverage real estate investments.

The most structuring point of the proposal is the progressive conversion of the Transcarioca BRT corridors (Barra–Fundão) and Transoeste (Jardim Oceânico–Campo Grande and Santa Cruz) to the rail-based mode. The city government is evaluating both the traditional VLT, with overhead catenary and low floor, and the Light Vehicle on Tires (VLP), which also runs on electrified rails but has a lower implementation cost.

The change represents a relevant urbanistic course correction, recognized councilman Paulo Messina (PL), from the opposition bench, stating that the city "should have opted for rail-based transport" from the beginning. The Public-Private Partnership (PPP) model proposed for the new services will be in the sponsored concession modality, requiring public contributions both for works and for fare subsidy.

Financing the system remains a central challenge. The VLT carioca cost R$ 1.157 billion, of which R$ 532 million came from PAC Mobilidade and R$ 625 million were advanced by the PPP. Since the original contract, seven amendments have been signed to renegotiate obligations and cover imbalances, including the removal of a 2.6-kilometer stretch to the Novo Rio Bus Terminal, generating a loss of revenues of R$ 11 million.

In parallel, another project sent by Paes to the legislature requests authorization for a loan of R$ 882 million, part of it destined for drainage of the Acari River basin and the urbanization of favelas such as Complexo do Alemão and Rocinha. The articulation between mobility and sanitation indicates that the expansion of the VLT will not be an isolated work, but part of a broader urban restructuring, backed by revenues such as the Municipal Participation Fund (FPM) as a counter-guarantee.

Felipe Borim, from BNDES, warned at the "Caminhos do Brasil" meeting that the loss of 43% of public transport passengers between 2014 and 2023, largely migrated to app-based motorcycles and cars, deepens the financing crisis of the systems. Without good projects and perennial planning, the reduction in demand decreases fare collection, creating a vicious circle that only qualified investments can break.

The BNDES study, <a href='https://oglobo.globo.com/brasil/noticia/2025/08/04/estudo-do-bndes-aponta-que-brasil-precisa-dobrar-metro-e-quadruplicar-os-brts-e-vlts.ghtml' target='_blank'>as pointed out by a report from O Globo</a>, projects that the number of people served by high and medium capacity modes in Rio could jump from 1.6 million to 5.9 million — an increase of 270%.
