The math of Brazilian railways is still hostage to iron ore, but the new concessions plan and the partnership with China attempt to transform the rail network into the backbone of agribusiness, facing the billion-dollar cost of each kilometer.

<p>Every great city reveals its intelligence by how it moves people, but an entire country reveals itself by how it moves its freight. Brazil, which spent decades betting on trucks as its logistics vector, is now trying to rewrite its economic geography with rails, but stumbles upon an equation that stubbornly refuses to balance.</p>

<p>The National Railway Plan, in the final stages of adjustments under the Lula administration, foresees R$ 100 billion in investments and the expansion of 4,700 kilometers in the rail network, as detailed by Transport Minister Renan Filho during a presentation to the president. The package includes granting five major projects to the private sector, with the federal government committing to cover between 20% and 30% of the investments to ensure the economic viability of the projects.</p>

<p>The East-West Corridor is the centerpiece of this territorial engineering, with about 2,400 kilometers that will link the West-East Integration Railway (FIOL) and the Central-West Integration Railway (FICO), connecting Ilhéus in Bahia to Lucas do Rio Verde in Mato Grosso. The extension of the North-South Railway will add 477 kilometers between Açailândia in Maranhão and the port of Vila do Conde in Pará, creating an alternative route to the monopoly of the Carajás Railway, which belongs to Vale and operates with little idle capacity for grains.</p>

<p>The plan presented by Renan Filho to CNN Brasil also includes the controversial Ferrogrão project, with 933 kilometers between Sinop and Itaituba in Pará, which depends on a conciliation at the Supreme Federal Court due to impacts on conservation units. The Southeast Railway Ring, with approximately 300 kilometers linking Vitória to Itaboraí, and the completion of the Transnordestina, which has been dragging on since 2010 and is now scheduled for delivery in 2026 or 2027, complete the pipeline of structured projects.</p>

<p>The plan's ambition, however, stumbles upon a mineral reality that has shaped Brazil's railway geography for decades. In 2025, iron ore accounted for 73% of the 406.6 million tonnes transported on the country's rails, while agribusiness, despite being the engine of the trade balance, represented only 19% of the total volume, with 104.4 million tonnes of agricultural items.</p>

<p>Alone, Vale moved 301 million tonnes on its railways last year, operating in a vertically integrated model that combines mine, railway, and port, as explained by Solange Costa, a lawyer at Mello Torres law firm specialized in the sector. Outside mining, Rumo, a logistics company of the Cosan group, focuses on grain transport via the Norte Network, connecting Mato Grosso to the Port of Santos, but the Oeste Network, whose contract expires in June, has not carried freight since November 2025.</p>

<p>The average cost of railways in Brazil is R$ 27 million per kilometer, more than three times the value of a highway, estimated at R$ 8 million per kilometer, according to InvestNews. This relentless math explains why, even after the privatization of the state network in the 1990s and the creation of the railway authorization regime, the country never managed to consistently expand its railways, as observed by David Goldberg, managing partner at A&M Infra, highlighting the stagnation of the modal while highways were modernized.</p>

<p>To unlock investments, the government is betting on a special financing line at BNDES, with repayment terms extended up to 60 years and a grace period extended during the construction phase, when the railway is not yet generating revenue. Another path is the search for international capital, especially Chinese, since 70% of the soybeans and corn imported by China come from Brazil, which turns Brazilian logistics into a matter of food security for Xi Jinping's government.</p>

<p>The Brazil-China agreement signed on July 7, 2025, foresees studies for a transcontinental railway that would link Ilhéus to the port of Chancay in Peru, crossing South America from the Atlantic to the Pacific and reducing transport time to Asia from 40 to 28 days, according to a report by G1. The National Secretary of Railway Transport, Leonardo Ribeiro, described the initiative as a technical and diplomatic strategic move to bring continents closer and reduce distances, leveraging existing railway sections in Brazil.</p>

<p>The route would cross Bahia, Goiás, Mato Grosso, Rondônia, and Acre before crossing the Peruvian border, reaching the port of Chancay, inaugurated in 2024 with Chinese financing under the Belt and Road Initiative. Although Brazil has not formally joined the New Silk Road, the Lula administration's assessment is that Chinese investments are already significant and do not require institutional accession to the program, while five Chinese companies are already studying railway and highway projects in the country, according to the Transport Minister.</p>

<p>CRRC, the world's largest train manufacturer, has already established itself in Brazil as a partner of Grupo Comporte in the Intercity Train concession and is preparing a factory in Araraquara, in the interior of São Paulo, with an initial investment of R$ 50 million. CCCC, a Chinese state-owned infrastructure company, has put down roots in the country with a stake in the São Luís Port in Maranhão, and showed special interest in the FICO-FIOL corridor during meetings with the Ministry of Transport in 2025.</p>

<p>Chinese interest, however, has not yet translated into concrete proposals, and the first auction of this batch of eight projects is scheduled for September, but the tender notice has not yet been published. In the sector, the prevailing view is that unlocking these investments, with or without the Chinese, will remain a monumental challenge, as a source with market experience told InvestNews, who does not see 100% private demand and doubts viability without government subsidies.</p>

<p>The renegotiation of railway concessions, inherited from the Bolsonaro administration and renegotiated under the Lula government, is expected to secure public funds to cover the federal government's counterpart in new projects. Vale has agreed to pay up to R$ 17 billion for the extension of the Carajás and Vitória-Minas railway contracts, while agreements have already been closed with Rumo and MRS Logística, injecting billions into the federal coffers to fuel the National Railway Plan.</p>

<p>Brazil is trying, once again, to prove that rails can be more than ore corridors and become the backbone of an agricultural powerhouse that needs to efficiently move its production. The financial engineering is complex, costs are prohibitive, and dependence on mineral commodities still dictates the pace, but the bet on intermodality and the bi-oceanic connection with China suggests that, this time, the country might finally get the math right.</p>
