German railway engineering proves that a line's capacity lies not just in concrete, but in the intelligence of the software controlling the fleet.

The German capital is preparing one of the most profound operational transformations of its underground network without passengers noticing any service interruption. The project aims to modernize the signaling of lines U5 and U8, introducing the communication-based train control system known by the acronym CBTC. The transformation will allow trains to run at intervals of less than 100 seconds, increasing the line's capacity.

Siemens Mobility CEO Michael Peter precisely defined the magnitude of the technological leap by stating that investing in leading CBTC technology is excellent news for Berlin subway passengers. He highlighted that semi-automatic operation will allow trains to run every 100 seconds, increasing the line's capacity by up to 30%. The figure impresses because it translates into a gain equivalent to building a new branch line, but obtained exclusively through the logical migration of the control system.

The platform chosen for modernization is Trainguard MT, a CBTC solution already equipping 96 subway lines in 49 cities around the world. The system breaks with the outdated logic of fixed track circuits, which segment the railway into rigid blocks and limit the number of trains that can occupy the same section simultaneously. In place of this static architecture, Trainguard MT establishes continuous radio communication between each train and the control center, transmitting position, speed, and braking commands in real time.

Operation will be conducted at GoA2 automation level, known as semi-automatic, where the onboard driver supervises door closing, passenger boarding, and intervenes only in emergency situations. Acceleration, deceleration, cruise speed control, and even emergency braking are now managed by the onboard computer, which reacts in milliseconds to data received from the track. The practical result for the user is a smoother ride, less wear on mechanical components, and stopping accuracy at platforms that optimizes passenger flow.

Line U5, with its 10 stations, will receive the new signaling by 2029, while the extensive U8, which has 24 stops and operates east-west with a central deviation, will be fully converted by 2032. The project covers 40 kilometers of track and will be carried out with the network in commercial operation, a railway engineering challenge requiring modular commissioning and nighttime migration of field equipment. The complexity of conversion without service interruption is one of the technical differentiators that justify the large-scale investment and long deployment times.

The logic that Berlin is applying now is not conceptually far from what São Paulo has begun implementing on its oldest lines to combat peak-hour saturation. The CBTC system is being deployed on lines 1-Blue, 2-Green, and 3-Red of the São Paulo Metro, retiring the fixed signaling that limited train frequency. The parallel between the two megacities reveals that modernizing onboard electronics is the fastest and most rational way to squeeze additional performance from tunnels that cannot be physically widened under the consolidated urban fabric.

The relevant difference lies in the maturity stage of each project: while São Paulo is replacing the logical brain on lines that have been in operation for decades, Berlin is applying the technology to corridors that already have part of the infrastructure prepared to receive the digital leap. The German contract also includes long-term maintenance agreements, ensuring that the system's reliability curve will be monitored and adjusted throughout the entire life cycle of Trainguard MT. Predictive maintenance, fueled by the constant data flow generated by CBTC, reduces dependence on cyclical inspections and shifts maintenance engineering to the realm of algorithm analysis.

The political economy of mobility also transforms when a digital signaling system allows the same train fleet to transport 30% more passengers without adding a single extra car. The line capacity metric is no longer solely tied to heavy infrastructure investment but depends on the sophistication of the software governing the spacing between trains. Berlin demonstrates that the efficiency of mass transit in the 21st century will be measured less by kilometers of excavated tunnels and more by the intelligence of data packets traveling through the control center's servers.

Calibrating the headway to around 99 seconds implies that the safety distance between trains will be permanently recalculated based on actual speed and rail adhesion, rather than on a fixed block oversized for the worst theoretical scenario. The result is a more intense and intelligent use of the track channel, reducing waiting time on platforms and better distributing occupancy throughout the day. The 30% gain in line capacity becomes, in practice, a new weapon against the mobility collapse that threatens large metropolises when individual transport is still irrationally subsidized.

The Berlin case also exposes a characteristic often neglected in Brazilian public debate about urban railways, which tends to treat signaling modernization as an accessory item compared to inaugurations of new stations. The German experience demonstrates that installing CBTC is building capacity without moving earth, is expanding public rail transport supply using electronics as the main civil engineering tool. For a country like Brazil, where fiscal resources for large works are scarce and the mobility deficit is immense, Berlin's lesson should sound like a priority order.
