The first railway authorization to leave the drawing board and the new concessions reshape the Brazilian transport matrix with gauges, rails, and contracts targeting the future.

Every great city reveals its intelligence by the way it moves people. A nation's intelligence is measured, with the same candor, by the engineering with which it moves its cargo. Brazil is facing a historic window in which the response ceases to be rhetorical and takes the form of rails, sleepers, and long-term contracts. The National Railway Plan comes into play with a portfolio of approximately R$ 100 billion and 4,700 kilometers of new railway projects spanning from Bahia to Pará, from Mato Grosso to Espírito Santo.

The backbone of this turnaround is the combination of five major concessions — the East-West Corridor, the extension of the North-South Railway, the Southeast Railway Ring, Transnordestina, and Ferrogrão — with a new regulatory framework. This is already bringing projects out of the paper stage. The technical design is ambitious but grounded in contracts with investment commitments and in the railway authorization model that introduced the *open access* regime in the country, breaking the vertical monopoly of the large historical operators.

The most concrete symbol of this tectonic shift in national logistics is in Inocência, Mato Grosso do Sul. There, the National Land Transport Agency (ANTT) made possible the first railway authorization that actually left the drawing board. A 47-kilometer *short line* branch designed to connect the future Arauco factory — which will be the world's largest pulp plant — directly to the national railway network, as recorded by ANTT itself at the groundbreaking ceremony.

The completion of the works is scheduled for the second half of 2027. The 3.5 million annual tons of pulp will leave the highways to take the Rumo North Network toward the Port of Santos. The logic is cuttingly simple: fewer trucks, more safety, decreasing marginal cost, and international competitiveness supported by meter-gauge rails that connect with the penetration railways of the Central-West.

While the spearhead advances in the *short lines*, the heavy artillery of the National Railway Plan positions the structuring corridors. The East-West Corridor, with about 2,400 kilometers in length, stitches together the West-East Integration Railway (FIOL) and the Central-West Integration Railway (FICO). This connects Ilhéus, in Bahia, to Lucas do Rio Verde, in Mato Grosso, creating an intermodal logistics vector that challenges the dictatorship of the Southeast ports.

The extension of the North-South Railway adds 477 kilometers to the port of Vila do Conde, in Pará. It offers a real alternative for grain shipment that today heavily depends on the Carajás Railway, dominated by Vale's ore. Meanwhile, the Southeast Railway Ring projects 300 kilometers between Vitória (ES) and Itaboraí (RJ), connecting the Vitória-Minas Railway to the MRS Logística network. It resolves, on asphalt and on rail, the urban conflict that crosses the metropolitan region of São Paulo.

The financial engineering that supports this plan is as relevant as the civil engineering of the routes. The early renewals of concessions — valid until 2057 — unlocked R$ 17 billion in commitments from Vale, R$ 5 billion from Rumo, and another R$ 5 billion from MRS for the São Paulo railway ring. In addition to investments in own fleets, such as the R$ 700 million that VLI has just directed to eight new Progress Rail locomotives on the Central-Atlantic Railway, according to the plan presented to President Lula by Transport Minister Renan Filho.

The machines, custom-designed for Brazilian conditions, increase cargo capacity, reduce emissions, and enhance operational comfort. The most eloquent data point is that VLI has already injected R$ 17 billion between 2014 and 2024, generating eight thousand direct jobs. The cycle that begins is no longer one of promises or works halted for a decade, like Transnordestina — now with a 600-kilometer connection in Estreito (MA) and delivery forecast for 2026 or 2027.

There are, naturally, technical and political rough edges that no one underestimates. Ferrogrão, with its 933 kilometers between Sinop (MT) and Itaituba (PA), remains the most challenging project in the portfolio. It is stalled in conciliation at the Supreme Federal Court due to overlap with conservation units and environmental risks that require sophisticated mitigation modeling. The right of passage, which the new authorizations intend to universalize, still needs to be tested on a commercial scale to prove that the *open access* model operates with real efficiency, not just conceptual.

However, the movement is irreversible. The federal government calibrated a mechanism in which the Union takes on 20% to 30% of the investments to close the viability gap, using resources precisely from contractual renegotiations. This closes the virtuous circle between mature concession and network expansion. It is the first time in decades that the country looks at its transport matrix — still 65% road-based — and decides, with executive projects on the table, that freight can no longer be held hostage to diesel and asphalt.

The lesson that emerges from these rails is neither nostalgic nor romantic: it is thermodynamic and economic. Transporting a ton by rail consumes up to seven times less energy than by truck, reduces accidents, compresses carbon emissions, and above all, restores predictability to the flow of harvests and industrial inputs. The penetration railway advancing over the Cerrado is not a developmental whim; it is the only rational response for a country that produces in the center and exports through the coast, with continental distances that no road freight regulation can tame.

Brazil, which spent the 20th century watching its railway network shrink under state neglect, begins to understand that rail is not a vestige of a glorious past, but cutting-edge infrastructure and development technology. Every kilometer of welded steel advancing from the Central-West to the sea, every *short line* spur that frees a truck from the asphalt, every authorization contract that transforms paper into ballast and sleeper is a practical demonstration that development is written with mixed gauge and long-term investment.

And that is precisely what separates countries that merely produce commodities from those that control the logistics of their own growth: the density of steel per square kilometer of productive territory, the intelligence to move tons with the same precision that smart cities move people, and the sovereign decision not to leave the logistical future of an agricultural and industrial powerhouse to the exclusive whims of highways.
