With a record investment of R$5.4 billion, São Paulo's metro system combines intelligent systems, new trains, and a reassessment of the concessions model.

Every great city reveals its intelligence by how it moves people. In 2026, São Paulo, with a record budget of R$5.4 billion for the Metro, turned this premise into a real-scale engineering and management laboratory. The investment, the largest in the company's history, materializes in simultaneous work fronts that combine physical expansion, fleet renewal, and a profound technological mutation in operations.

The flagship of this transformation is the expansion of Line 2-Green, a project that adds 13.8 kilometers of new tracks and 13 stations starting from Vila Prudente. As detailed by <a href="https://www.ocafezinho.com/2026/06/08/expansao-do-metro-de-sao-paulo-reflete-necessidade-de-modernizacao-no-transporte-urbano-brasileiro/" target="_blank">O Cafezinho</a> in its analysis of the modernization cycle, the first phase has already surpassed 55% completion, with eight new stops emerging from the subsoil to relieve the overloaded east zone.

The central nerve of this new network is not only in the tunnels, but in intelligent systems like CBTC (Communication-Based Train Control). In practice, CBTC allows reducing the minimum interval between trains without compromising safety, increasing hourly transport capacity. This technology places São Paulo on the same operational level as London and Singapore, although it is still under testing on Line 17-Gold and partially implemented on Line 4.

Associated with this operational intelligence, the implementation of platform screen doors (PSD) at the new stations represents a silent revolution in safety and energy efficiency of the system. PSDs eliminate the risk of falls onto the tracks, stabilize boarding flows, and block the intrusion of hot air into the tunnels, drastically reducing the load on the gigantic air conditioning and ventilation systems.

An expansion project of this magnitude, however, would lose its meaning without a corresponding renewal of rolling stock. That is exactly what drives the order of 63 new trains for lines 1-Blue, 2-Green, 3-Red, and 15-Silver. Scale renewal is not an aesthetic luxury, but an essential technical condition so that the greater supply of tracks translates into a real reduction of intervals and a tangible increase in system reliability for passengers.

Line 15-Silver, one of the rare examples of high-capacity monorail in commercial operation in the world, receives R$1.03 billion to expand through the east zone. The monorail, which moves on an elevated concrete beam powered by the classic third rail, proves to be a faster and lower-cost construction solution in corridors where deep tunnels would be unfeasible, even if its per-train capacity is lower than that of a heavy metro. Currently, 15 of the 19 new trains have already been delivered.

Line 17-Gold, with a budget of R$836.3 million, bears the burden of being the redemption of a promise dating back to the 2014 World Cup. This section, which was supposed to connect Congonhas Airport to Morumbi, has been paralyzed since 2024 due to consortium bankruptcy and lack of bidding for resumption. Despite this, the project operates as a model of separation between public construction and future concession management, a contractual architecture that tends to multiply in state programs.

At the heart of this cycle, the inauguration of the first section of Line 6-Orange, with 11.7 kilometers and six stations in assisted operation this Thursday, symbolizes both the advances and the immense difficulties of the public-private partnership (PPP) model in Brazil. Eighteen years after being announced, the city's deepest line was only unlocked after successive corruption scandals, contract terminations, and complex economic-financial rebalancing negotiations that added R$3.69 billion to the original R$19 billion contract, according to a report by newspaper O Globo.

The delay was not only bureaucratic but geological, with soil variability during excavation generating unforeseen services, in addition to historic stoppages for preservation of archaeological sites such as Quilombo Saracura, discovered during works at Estação 14 Bis. The result, even with unfinished stations and reduced operating hours, is a line that cuts through the extreme northwest and serves a university corridor, projecting a future demand of 600,000 daily passengers when the 15 stops are complete.

Despite the unprecedented technical and financial volume, the 2026 cycle also exposes a shift in Governor Tarcísio de Freitas' strategy, who, witnessing the concentration of concessions in two major economic groups, signaled his inclination to keep lines 1, 2, 3, and 15 under the management of the public company Metrô de São Paulo. The São Paulo metro network thus ceases to be a field of ideological doctrine about privatization and becomes an environment of technical pragmatism, where the efficiency of the public sector competes and complements private capital.

The 2025 performance, with R$4.51 billion effectively applied — a budget execution rate of 89% — provides the credibility that the discourse lacked, proving that there is technical and administrative capacity to absorb high volumes of investment. It is not about solving decades of underinvestment in urban mobility in a single term, but about establishing, with robust technical and financial facts, that the rails are the backbone by which the city has chosen to grow, reduce its social friction, and raise its standard of urban intelligence.
