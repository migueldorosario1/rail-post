Brazil drafts a pharaonic letter of intentions for the rails while reality exposes a network the size of the Empire and no one to foot the bill.

When technique organizes space, society gains time to live. Brazil, however, has accumulated more than a century of lost time by confining its logistics matrix to asphalt, and now finds itself facing a paradox that mixes geopolitical ambition with budgetary asphyxiation.

The new National Railways Plan, a package of concessions that the federal government intends to launch in 2026, promises to break the inertia by adding 9,000 kilometers of rails at an estimated cost of R$ 140 billion. The project's architecture, however, reveals a critical dependence on foreign capital, especially Chinese, to unlock works in a territory that operates, in practice, with the same railway coverage as at the end of Dom Pedro II's reign in 1889.

The numbers lay bare the historical stagnation of the mode. Brazil's effective operating railway network stands at around 29,347 kilometers, with about 21,000 kilometers under active concession and 8,300 kilometers under federal administration. Despite this, a large part of the lines faces maintenance and economic viability challenges.

In 2025, this infrastructure moved 489.1 million tons of cargo, according to official ANTT data. Alone, Vale, controller of strategic assets like the Carajás Railway (EFC) and Vitória-Minas Railway (EFVM), transported 382.4 million tons, consolidating a verticalized model where mine, railway, and port operate in symbiosis to ensure the commodity's competitiveness abroad.

Faced with this mineral concentration scenario, the Union tries to reactivate the railway cycle by targeting agribusiness, a sector that represented only 19% of cargo transported in 2025. According to <a href='https://investnews.com.br/negocios/brasil-tenta-de-novo-expandir-as-ferrovias-mas-ainda-falta-combinar-com-os-chineses/amp/' target='_blank'>InvestNews</a> in its sectoral analysis, the vast soybean and corn producing belt of the Center-West serves as the economic justification for five structuring projects in the government's pipeline.

The plan's central and most controversial piece is the Ferrogrão, with 862 kilometers designed to connect Sinop (MT) to Itaituba (PA), creating a waterway export corridor through the Arco Norte. The route survives amidst a longstanding legal and environmental friction, which includes the extinction of the Jamanxim National Park conservation unit, consolidated in 2023 via Provisional Measure 1,167.

While the government tries to hold the reimbursement value at R$ 58.2 million, adjusted by the IPCA based on a 2016 ceiling, the company claims R$ 172.9 million alleging a decade of unpaid work, as detailed by <a href='https://www1.folha.uol.com.br/mercado/2026/07/ministerio-dos-transportes-barra-aumento-milionario-de-estudos-da-ferrograo.shtml' target='_blank'>Folha</a> when revealing the imbroglio at the Ministry of Transport. The pre-bid paralysis exposes the difficulty of carrying out large-scale engineering when the average cost per kilometer of a new railway, estimated at R$ 27 million, triples the amount needed to build a highway.

Beyond the Ferrogrão, the ambitious map includes the 2,400-kilometer East-West Corridor, joining the Fico and Fiol to connect the Center-West to the ports of Bahia, and the 477-kilometer Extension of the North-South Railway to the Port of Vila do Conde (PA). The economic modeling of these undertakings, detailed by <a href='https://www.cnnbrasil.com.br/economia/macroeconomia/com-investimento-de-r-100-bi-governo-lancara-plano-nacional-de-ferrovias/' target='_blank'>CNN Brasil</a>, indicates that the National Treasury will need to cover 20% to 30% of the total investment to make the bids attractive to the market, a heavy burden considering the country's chronic fiscal difficulties.

The solution found in Brasília inevitably involves the internationalization of financing with Chinese protagonism. The formal approach with the China Railway Economic and Planning Research Institute, signed in a memorandum of understanding to design a bi-oceanic railway that crosses Acre towards the port of Chancay in Peru, is the clearest sign of this strategy.

The commercial symbiosis is evident: with Xi Jinping's government importing 70% of the soybeans and corn consumed in the Asian giant from Brazil, ensuring logistical efficiency here becomes a matter of Chinese domestic food security. Therefore, negotiations at the BNDES aim to create a financing line with extended repayment terms, up to 60 years, to match the slow maturation of rails, a model that seeks to emulate the state investment structure that China State Railway used to expand its domestic network.

However, the promise of massive contributions from state-owned companies like CCCC and CRRC runs up against the uncertainty of the Brazilian regulatory environment, where the rules of the National Land Transport Agency (ANTT) oscillate with the political wind. The reality of the idle network proves that, without robust return guarantees, capital retreats, and Brazil continues to be a continental country that, tragically, finds road freight transport cheaper than the efficiency of a heavy train crossing the plateau.

The relentless math exposes the central challenge of shifting the national transport matrix: while a highway costs R$ 8 million per kilometer, the railway demands R$ 27 million without generating revenue throughout the long construction period. This cash flow reality turns the National Railways Plan into a kind of high-risk bet that, without Beijing's financial backing, threatens to perpetuate the logistical isolation of the productive interior and freeze Brazil in the asphalt era.
