The ground-level power supply system that preserved Rio's historic heritage now inspires São Paulo to modernize its mobility with silent and efficient trams.

The early hours of March 27, 2015, marked the beginning of the transformation of downtown Rio de Janeiro. The first 400 meters of track for the Rio de Janeiro Light Rail (VLT Carioca) were laid on General Luiz Mendes de Morais Street, in Santo Cristo. Each 18-meter rail was positioned by workers of the concessionaire, paving the way for a revolutionary mode of transport.

'It's a paradigm shift. Society has to adopt the new transport, which starts to coexist with streets, cars, people. It's a cultural change too,' argued engineer Tiago Augusto Alves, representative of the Companhia de Concessões Rodoviárias (CCR), responsible for the project. The statement, recorded at a debate at the Union of Engineers in the State of São Paulo (SEESP), captures the spirit of the VLT: a vehicle that not only moves passengers but also reshapes urban space and people's relationship with the city.

The major technical innovation of the Rio de Janeiro Light Rail is its Alimentation by the Ground (APS) system. This completely dispenses with the overhead catenary wires that tangle the skies of many cities with electric trams. Energy is captured by collector shoes installed on only one of the car modules, activating underground *powerboxes* only when the vehicle is exactly over the rail. Emergency battery banks ensure 400 meters of autonomy in case of power failure, keeping the air conditioning operational in Rio's tropical climate.

With a width of 2.65 meters and a height of 2.17 meters, the vehicle is larger than European models and carries up to 420 passengers per car at an average speed of 17 km/h. The complete project, budgeted at R$1.5 billion, includes 28 kilometers of track, 46 stops, and 32 cars. It included meticulous archaeological work that preserved original 19th-century paving stones, as detailed by the <a href='http://www.rio.rj.gov.br/web/guest/exibeconteudo?id=5267887' target='_blank'>city government of Rio</a> at the time. On Constituição Street, a 15-meter stretch of stones from the era of Emperor Dom Pedro I was kept intact under the tracks, requiring excavation of only half a meter deep.

The payment system adopts voluntary validation integrated with the Bilhete Único (Unified Ticket), with a total capacity of 300 thousand passengers per day when the lines are fully operational. Multimodal integration connects the Novo Rio Bus Station, Central do Brasil train station, the ferries, Santos Dumont Airport, BRTs, the cruise terminal, and even the Providência Hill Cable Car, stitching together the transportation network of the central and port region into a cohesive logistical hub.

While Rio consolidates its VLT, São Paulo advances with its own project, named Bonde São Paulo (São Paulo Tram) and focused on the revitalization of the historic downtown. SP Urbanismo concluded in February 2024 a comprehensive urban study that points to the transformation potential of degraded areas such as Luz, Bom Retiro, and the Historical Triangle, the <a href='https://prefeitura.sp.gov.br/web/sp_urbanismo/w/prefeitura-conclui-estudo-urban%C3%ADstico-e-avan%C3%A7a-com-projeto-de-ve%C3%ADculo-leve-sobre-trilhos-vlt-para-o-centro-de-s%C3%A3o-paulo' target='_blank'>São Paulo city government</a> reported in an official note.

The Bonde São Paulo plans two lines of about 6 kilometers each, totaling 12 kilometers in length and 27 stops, with the capacity to serve 134 thousand people per day. The modeling follows an Expression of Interest Procedure (PMI) conducted by the Executive Secretariat of Privatization and Partnerships, which is analyzing a Public-Private Partnership (PPP) to enable the implementation, operation, and maintenance of the system, replicating the institutional arrangement that worked in Rio.

Integration with nine metro stations, two CPTM (Companhia Paulista de Trens Metropolitanos) stations, and five bus terminals positions São Paulo's VLT as a 'last mile' solution downtown, connecting popular shopping areas such as 25 de Março and Santa Efigênia to museums, theaters, and parks. The CEO of SP Urbanismo, Pedro Martin, emphasized at an event of the Association of Engineers and Architects of the Metro (AEAMESP) that the mode is 'an urban regeneration project guided by the VLT,' also aiming at the future state government headquarters in Campos Elísios.

From an environmental standpoint, the electric traction technology with regenerative braking reduces greenhouse gas emissions and particulate matter, aligning with the capital's climate goals. The absence of overhead wires in the São Paulo project, although not yet confirmed as a definitive standard, would tend to follow Rio's philosophy of preserving the urban landscape without the tangle of wires characteristic of old trams, a fundamental contrast with electric buses that rely on intrusive overhead networks.

An alternative project drawn up by urban mobility experts also proposes a route connecting the Palmeiras-Barra Funda Station to the Mandaqui neighborhood, with 8 kilometers and 12 stations along Engenheiro Caetano Álvares Avenue, serving a region lacking rail transport. Signal priority would ensure speed faster than that of cars, and the possibility of real estate appreciation would add to the positive environmental impact with the renaturalization of the Mandaqui Stream, according to a report by <a href='https://mobilidade.estadao.com.br/inovacao/o-vlt-de-sao-paulo-vem-ai-confira-diferentes-projetos-para-o-meio-de-transporte/' target='_blank'>Estadão</a>.

The international experience of cities like Bordeaux, France, which also adopted APS to eliminate wires in its historic center, shows that Rio's model is aligned with the most advanced in the world on rails. Bordeaux opted for the system precisely to protect its UNESCO-listed architectural heritage, the same logic that guided the preservation of Rio's colonial paving and that could guide São Paulo in the treatment of the Historical Triangle and the Luz region.

Therefore, the Rio de Janeiro Light Rail is not just a utilitarian vehicle: it is a statement that mobility and urban memory can coexist elegantly. By swapping the chaos of wires for the discretion of buried *powerboxes*, Rio showed that the future of rails runs on the surface with lightness and respect for the built environment.

By looking to Rio's example, São Paulo can avoid past mistakes and implement a clean, efficient, and aesthetically integrated system in the expanded downtown. The success of the Bonde São Paulo will depend on the same technical daring that made the Rio de Janeiro Light Rail a reference case: the bet on cutting-edge technology combined with urban sensitivity.
