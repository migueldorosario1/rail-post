While the country struggles with freight railways, medium and large cities are structuring modern Light Rail Vehicle projects with technical support from Infra S.A., reuse of existing rail networks, and clean energy solutions

Bauru, with 380,000 inhabitants in the interior of São Paulo, will get the city's first Light Rail Vehicle, with 28.8 kilometers of extension distributed in three branches and more than thirty stations. The project, which utilizes the existing railway network and will be structured by the state-owned Infra S.A., reveals a new and strategic movement: Brazil is beginning to treat urban rail transport as a development policy, not as a piece of railway nostalgia.

The same technical drive is repeated in Campina Grande, in Paraíba, where studies for a VLT for 400,000 inhabitants are in the final consolidation phase. It is expected that, after the completion of the studies, the project will move forward to the bidding and construction phases, with future delivery of the system. And it gains scale in the city of São Paulo, which is planning a Public-Private Partnership for the Bonde São Paulo, 12 kilometers of VLT with an estimated investment of R$ 4 billion, boosted by the PPP of the new state administrative center, worth R$ 6 billion, won by the MEZ-RZK Novo Centro consortium.

When technique organizes space, society gains time to live. This is the most honest reading key to the current renaissance of the Brazilian VLT, which emerges as a concrete response to the chronic mobility crisis and the need to requalify degraded urban centers, many of them suffocated by viaducts and avenues that pushed life away from the tracks.

Infra S.A., a federal public company formed by the merger of the Empresa de Planejamento e Logística (EPL) and Valec, is at the core of this technical shift. Contracted by the municipality of Bauru to prepare the Technical, Economic, and Environmental Feasibility Study for the local VLT, the state-owned company replicates, in the urban environment, the same project structuring capacity that BNDES exercised in sanitation and highways, as pointed out by the <a href='https://abifer.org.br/projetos-de-vlts-no-brasil-ganham-impulso/' target='_blank'>Associação Brasileira da Indústria Ferroviária (ABIFER)</a> in its recent analysis of the VLT market.

The president of Infra S.A., Jorge Bastos, defined the moment as the consolidation of a new front of action for the company. According to him, the same expertise that designed major logistics corridors is being taken into the cities, with technical rigor and legal security for passenger rail transport projects.

The choice of VLT is neither accidental nor romantic. Unlike the metro, which requires colossal excavations and kilometer costs that can exceed R$ 500 million, the VLT operates on the surface, can share space with traffic, and uses electric traction, with much leaner implementation and operation costs. In Bauru and Campina Grande, for example, the projects take advantage of the capillarity of already existing and underutilized railway networks, drastically reducing the need for expropriations and earthwork expenses, while returning a structuring transport axis to the city.

This rationality of reusing old railway assets is a leap of intelligence in a country that, as data from the National Confederation of Industry shows, has kept the share of freight rail stagnant between 18% and 21% of the transport matrix for more than twenty years. While freight projects face complex modeling and investments of R$ 127 billion still on paper, the VLT advances precisely because it combines reverse engineering, lower cost, and strong urban appeal.

Brazil already operates two electric VLTs successfully: the VLT Carioca, in Rio de Janeiro, and the Baixada Santista system (EMTU), on the coast of São Paulo. Both function as national technical references and prove that Brazilian industry masters the implementation and operation chain of this mode. Vicente Abate, president of ABIFER, stated that manufacturers established in the country have the capacity to serve new projects with solutions associated with clean energy, including hybrid traction and even green hydrogen, a technological frontier that places the VLT at the center of the urban transport decarbonization agenda.

The São Paulo Bonde project, which foresees 12 kilometers of extension, illustrates how the VLT also serves the revitalization of historic centers that have lost population and economic activity to the road axes of peripheral expansion. The PPP of the new administrative center, won in February by the MEZ-RZK Novo Centro consortium, creates an institutional anchor and passenger demand that technically and financially justifies the implementation of the system, in a rare chain of integrated urban planning that, if well executed, could change the face of the central region.

At the national level, the entry of Infra S.A. into VLT projects in medium-sized cities signals a relevant governance shift: the federal government, which historically has been absent from urban rail mobility outside the capitals, now offers technical modeling capacity to municipalities that would never have been able to contract robust feasibility studies on their own. This is the same logic that allowed the multiplication of sanitation concessions after BNDES took over the structuring of projects.

The positive side effects are many: lower emission of local pollutants, reduction of pressure on the road system, induction of population densification along the served axes, and encouragement of intermodality with buses, bicycles, and pedestrians. In addition, the VLT acts as a trigger for urban requalification, raising land value and creating mobility corridors that sew the city more rationally than the valley-floor avenues or road tunnels that marked the highway-centered urbanism of the 20th century.

It is not, therefore, a mere romantic return to the trams of the past. The Brazilian VLT of this cycle is cutting-edge technology applied to intelligent urban designs, clean electric traction, and sophisticated financial modeling, with real potential to spread to dozens of medium-sized cities that have idle railway beds and a thirst for decent public transport. While the bullet train remains on the drawing board and mega freight projects depend on gigantic guarantee arrangements, the Light Rail Vehicle is silently proving that, in Brazil, the mobility of the future may be hidden on the tracks that are already on the ground.
