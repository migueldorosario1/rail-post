The pragmatism of urban rails versus the voluntarism of trains that never arrive.

When technique organizes space, society gains time to live. São Paulo has just turned this maxim into concrete, steel, and CBTC systems by finalizing a package of R$57 billion to expand its metro network by approximately 50 kilometers, with an additional R$14 billion allocated to 22 kilometers of CPTM tracks, totaling 72 additional kilometers of rail transport in the metropolitan region.

The São Paulo Metro budget for 2026 reached R$5.4 billion, the highest in the state-owned company's history, with a 12% increase over the R$4.8 billion executed in 2025. Eight lines are undergoing simultaneous construction, 63 new trains are in the procurement process, and control systems such as platform doors and intelligent signaling are moving from promise to schedule.

As reported by <a href='https://timesbrasil.com.br/brasil/sp-anuncia-mais-de-r-50-bi-para-expansao-do-metro/' target='_blank'>Times Brasil</a>, Governor Tarcísio de Freitas (Republicanos) enacted the law authorizing the state to borrow up to US$425 million from multilateral organizations and private banks, equivalent to about R$2.2 billion in additional credit. The expansion breaks the capital's limits for the first time in the system's history, taking Line 4-Yellow outside the municipality of São Paulo and extending Line 2-Green to Dutra/Guarulhos.

Line 2-Green concentrates the largest volume of resources in 2026, with R$2.59 billion reserved for the 13.8-kilometer extension between Vila Prudente and Dutra/Guarulhos, which will include eight new stations in the first phase and five in the second stage. The execution of the stretch to Penha has already surpassed 55% physical progress, while the extension to the neighboring municipality consolidates a metropolitan integration awaited for decades.

Line 15-Silver, a monorail system serving the East Zone, will receive R$1.03 billion in 2026 to extend its tracks from Ipiranga to Hospital Cidade Tiradentes, a 63% increase over the R$629.5 million foreseen in the previous year. Meanwhile, Line 17-Gold, with a budget of R$836.3 million, is moving toward the inauguration of the stretch between Congonhas Airport and Morumbi Station, ending a wait that dates back to the 2014 World Cup.

Line 6-Orange advances as an underground project of 15.3 kilometers and 15 stations under a Public-Private Partnership with concessionaire Linha Uni, from the Spanish group Acciona, with partial operation scheduled for October 2026 and full operation in 2027. The state government also maintains resources for the projects of Lines 19-Sky Blue, 20-Pink, and 22-Brown, which will connect São Paulo to Guarulhos, Osasco, the ABC region, and Cotia.

Technological modernization accompanies the physical expansion with the implementation of the CBTC system — Communication-Based Train Control — which allows greater precision in train control and reduces the interval between trains, as well as the installation of platform doors in stations. As detailed by <a href='https://exame.com/brasil/expansao-das-linhas-2-15-e-entrega-da-17-plano-de-r-54-bi-do-metro-de-sp/' target='_blank'>Exame</a>, of the 63 new trains ordered, 19 are destined for Line 15-Silver — of which 15 have already been delivered — and 44 will reinforce lines 1-Blue, 2-Green, and 3-Red with updated rolling stock.

While the São Paulo Metro records successive investment execution records — R$4.26 billion in 2024 and R$4.51 billion in 2025 —, the bullet train between São Paulo and Rio de Janeiro has accumulated 18 years of announcements, postponements, and zero kilometers of track built. The project, which was part of the Growth Acceleration Program during the government of President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva in 2007, reaches 2026 with construction transferred from 2027 to 2028 and commercial operation estimated only for 2032.

TAV Brasil, the company authorized by the National Land Transport Agency (ANTT) to build and operate the high-speed rail corridor for up to 99 years, projects an updated cost of R$60 billion for 417 kilometers in length with a maximum speed of 320 kilometers per hour. The promise of tickets between R$300 and R$500 coexists with the absence of the Environmental Impact Study and Report (EIA/Rima), demanded by the Brazilian Institute of Environment and Renewable Natural Resources (Ibama) in March 2024 and so far not presented, as recorded by <a href='https://www.gazetadopovo.com.br/brasil/promessa-do-pt-obras-adiadas-trem-bala-sao-paulo-rio-de-janeiro/' target='_blank'>Gazeta do Povo</a>.

The federal government, which in 2023 had signaled participation in financing through the Minister of Transport, Renan Filho (MDB), now states that the project 'is not the government's' and does not foresee direct Union contribution, delegating to BNDES the role of financial agent without commitment of public resources. Execution remains projected as 100% private, supported by the Railway Law (Law No. 14,273/2021), sanctioned during the administration of former President Jair Bolsonaro, which paved the way for railway authorizations without the need for traditional concession.

Economist Cláudio Frischtak, president of Inter.B Consultoria Internacional de Negócios, warns that the required technological leap is radical for a country whose railway engineering historically operates between 80 and 100 kilometers per hour. 'Our historical experience involves maximum speeds between 80 km/h and 100 km/h. A high-speed train operates above 300 km/h. The nature of the project, equipment, and components is completely different,' the specialist stated.

Only four countries dominate high-speed trains over large distances — China, France, Japan, and Spain —, the latter having expanded its network with significant transfers from the European Union over two decades, a fiscal context nonexistent in Brazil. The director-secretary of the Brazilian Association of Railway Preservation, Thomaz Schoeffel, reinforces that not even Brazilian subways are entirely national: 'At most, they are assembled in the country,' requiring massive importation of know-how for any project above 300 kilometers per hour.

The contrast between the two projects reveals two opposing visions of national railway policy — one anchored in the pragmatism of daily urban mobility, which delivers stations, trains, and control systems with measurable physical execution, and the other held hostage by the voluntarism of electoral campaigns, chasing a R$60 billion technological leap without environmental licensing, without defined subsidy, and with 18 years of accumulated delay. The engineering that advances is the one that recognizes its real capabilities and builds upon them, kilometer by kilometer, while the closest station of the bullet train remains that of a promise.
