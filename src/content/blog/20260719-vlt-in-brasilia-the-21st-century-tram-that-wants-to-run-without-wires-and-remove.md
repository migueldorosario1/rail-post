Brazil needs to quadruple its VLTs by 2054, and Brasília emerges as a test for an innovative solution, while the Rio VLT shows the operational challenges and post-pandemic recovery.

Modernity is not abstract. It has ballast, gauge, energy, and direction. In the case of the Rio VLT (Light Rail Vehicle), the initial ambition was clear: a 28 km system, 4 lines, and 32 articulated trains, capable of carrying between 400 and 560 passengers per trip. The project, inaugurated in 2016, promised modernity and efficiency. However, the operational reality offers valuable lessons.

The Rio VLT operates below projected demand, carrying 95,000 passengers per day, against the contractual target of 243,000. The pandemic, the economic crisis, and passenger evasion were factors that contributed to this scenario. Despite this, the recent fare integration with Rio’s Single Ticket (RioCard) brought optimism for the future.

The Rio VLT faces challenges such as the need to reduce speed in busy spots, due to distracted pedestrians, delivery workers, and tourists on electric scooters crossing the tracks. Additionally, train maintenance and fare enforcement are aspects that require constant attention.

Despite the challenges, the Rio VLT brought significant transformations to mobility and urban space in Rio’s downtown. Stretches of Avenida Rio Branco were dedicated only to pedestrians and the VLT, changing city traditions, such as the Bola Preta parades, which moved to Avenida Presidente Antônio Carlos. The growth outlook is driven by new urban developments, such as Reviver Centro, Rua da Cerveja, and the Maravalley tech hub, in addition to the expansion of the rail network, with studies to “VLT-ize” BRT corridors.

For Brazil, the success of the Rio VLT and future implementation in other cities, such as Brasília, are crucial to achieving public transportation expansion goals. According to a BNDES study, the country will need to invest R$ 500 billion to expand subways and BRTs by 2054, doubling the subway system and quadrupling BRTs and VLTs. The plan, which mapped 194 projects, foresees the expansion from 376 km of subway to 700 km, and from 631 km of BRTs and VLTs to 2,500 km, aiming to meet growing demand and recover the loss of 43% of public transport passengers between 2014 and 2023.

Brasília, although it does not have a VLT in operation, is already discussing innovative projects. An example is the proposal for a system without overhead wires, adopting the ground-level power supply (APS) system. This project, which has undergone several revisions since 2009, is now moving forward for validation at the local Court of Auditors, with a 30-year concession to the private sector. Management and maintenance would be the responsibility of the winning company.

The VLT, with its capacity for fare integration, low floor, and comfort, represents a modern and sustainable solution for urban mobility. While Rio seeks recovery and growth of its system, Brasília tests innovative technical solutions. Modernity, although promising, requires careful planning and rigorous execution to become a concrete and beneficial reality for the population.
