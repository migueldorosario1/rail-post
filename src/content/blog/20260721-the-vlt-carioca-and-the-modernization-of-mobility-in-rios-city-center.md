When technique organizes space, society gains time to live.

When technique organizes space, society gains time to live. In Rio de Janeiro's city center, a silver vehicle glides among historic houses without a single overhead wire crossing the landscape.

The VLT Carioca resolved the classic conflict between electric mobility and heritage preservation with an engineering solution that dispenses with overhead catenaries. The magic happens on the ground, where the ground-level power supply (APS) system delivers energy to the vehicle in a segmented and safe manner.

The technology works through underground power boxes distributed along the permanent way, communicating with collector shoes installed on only one of the car modules. The electrical contact is activated only when the vehicle is over the rail, eliminating any risk of shock to pedestrians or cars at intersections.

This intelligence allowed the <a href="https://www.kfw.de/stories/economy/mobility/tram-rio/" target="_blank">modern tram system to integrate</a> the financial center and the cultural corridor of the city without disturbing the listed landscape. Constitution Street, for example, revealed during excavations original paving from the city's inauguration and shards from the time of Dom Pedro I at just half a meter deep.

The implementation of the permanent way required archaeological precision and adaptation to the existing urban layout. Each rail bar is 18 meters long, a dimension that allows curves compatible with the organic design of Rio's city center without losing operational stability.

The sleepers and rails were laid in coordinated stages that at times mobilized more than 500 workers simultaneously. Subsequently, signaling equipment and power cables connecting to the underground power boxes were installed before the final concrete pouring and the urban covering layer.

Each VLT car transports up to 420 passengers and operates at an average speed of 17 km/h in an environment shared with pedestrians and vehicles. The planned fleet of 32 cars was dimensioned to serve a network of 28 kilometers in length, according to the original project data from the Rio de Janeiro City Hall.

A central concern in choosing the technology was air conditioning, considered indispensable for the city's climate. The recharging system is constant while the vehicle is on the rails, but onboard energy banks provide a strategic reserve that allows about 400 meters of travel in case of a power supply failure.

The project cost approximately R$ 3.8 billion, which included not only the construction of the track and acquisition of the vehicles but also traffic light adaptation, flower bed drainage, and all urban signage. The financing combined resources from BNDES, the German KfW bank, and Brazilian private banks, with a significant contribution made possible by the German federal government.

The climate impact was a declared focus of the international financing, with an estimate of avoiding at least 5.6 million car trips per year when the system operates at full capacity. Over a 25-year use cycle, the reduction in carbon dioxide emissions should exceed 300 thousand tons.

The VLT Carioca operation adopts a voluntary validation system integrated with the Bilhete Único (single ticket), a cultural change that requires trust from the operator in the citizen. The fare of R$ 3.80 allows connecting the Novo Rio Bus Station, Central do Brasil train station, the ferries, and Santos Dumont Airport in a single system that already transports about 65,000 people daily.

The urban requalification generated by the mode is visible in the port region, where the combination of electric mobility and the absence of overhead wires transformed degraded areas into valued circulation spaces. Companies began to set up near the stations, generating jobs and energizing the local real estate market.

The success in Rio inspires other Brazilian capitals, and São Paulo developed a project named <a href="https://gestaourbana.prefeitura.sp.gov.br/noticias/prefeitura-desenvolve-projeto-de-vlt-para-melhorar-mobilidade-e-integracao-no-centro-de-sao-paulo/" target="_blank">Bonde São Paulo</a>, with 12 kilometers of planned extension and an investment of R$ 1.4 billion via the Growth Acceleration Program. The ground-level power supply technology is becoming established as the technical choice for historic centers that cannot give up sustainable mobility nor landscape preservation.

Engineer Tiago Augusto Alves, from CCR, describes the process as a paradigm shift in the coexistence between transport, streets, cars, and people. Brazilian urban mobility finds in the APS system a model that respects the past while building a future of low carbon and high efficiency.
