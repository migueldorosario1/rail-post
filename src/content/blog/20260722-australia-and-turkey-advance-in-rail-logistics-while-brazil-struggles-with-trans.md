Freight rail corridors show that development is built on rails, but Brazil still hesitates.

Each freight train can replace over a hundred trucks, reducing emissions and freeing up roadways for uses with lower economic impact. This simple yet powerful logistical equation is being applied with utmost determination in countries that have decided to bet on rails as the backbone of development.

In Australia, the Inland Rail megaproject consists of a 1,600-kilometer corridor designed to connect Melbourne to Brisbane. With an estimated budget of 31 billion Australian dollars, analysts compare the initiative to a Suez Canal on rails, as published in an analysis by <a href='https://www.ocafezinho.com/2026/04/21/trens-de-carga-redefinem-a-logistica-global-com-megaprojetos-na-australia-e-na-turquia/' target='_blank'>O Cafezinho</a>.

Each composition crossing the Australian outback can eliminate up to one hundred trucks from the congested Hume Highway, relieving highways and cutting diesel consumption. The engineering required to adapt infrastructure inherited from the last century included the demolition and complete reconstruction of the Broadford bridge in just forty hours, a display of surgical precision that avoided prolonged disruptions.

The total cost of Inland Rail rose from initial projections due to unstable soils, additional bridges, and global inflation. However, the Australian government maintains its commitment to priority sections. Tests are already underway on the most advanced segments, while the project faces litigation with rural landowners and scope adjustments that are part of the complexity of works of this magnitude.

In Turkey, the bet is equally robust with the Marmaray rail line, which crosses the Bosphorus Strait via the Yavuz Sultan Selim Bridge. Funded with 2 billion dollars from the World Bank, the rail line has been operating since 2013, integrating the Turkish rail network and improving passenger and freight transport capacity.

Half the route is built in tunnels, protecting operations from extreme heat, floods, and strong winds. The World Bank estimates the creation of 414,000 direct and indirect jobs, including technical training programs open to women, reinforcing the inclusive and modernizing character of the initiative.

Brazil watches these advances while dragging an emblematic rail project for almost two decades. The Transnordestina, which began to take shape in 2006, has been halted since 2023, with no segment in commercial operation or active testing. The 1,200-kilometer railway aims to connect Eliseu Martins in Piauí to the Port of Pecém in Ceará, promising to reduce logistics costs for agribusiness and mining in the Matopiba region.

While Tijuca Alimentos moves corn, sorghum, and soybeans along the rails, the Pernambuco branch to the Port of Suape remains stopped, pending approval from the Federal Court of Accounts. The promise of regional integration and reduced logistical costs remains distant, reflecting Brazil's hesitation in prioritizing investments in rail infrastructure.

The experience of other countries shows that building freight rail corridors not only improves logistical efficiency but also creates jobs, reduces emissions, and drives economic development. While Australia and Turkey move forward, Brazil needs to accelerate its projects to avoid falling behind.
