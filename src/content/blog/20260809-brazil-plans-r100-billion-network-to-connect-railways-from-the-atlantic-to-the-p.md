The National Railway Plan structures a pipeline of megaprojects to break road dependency and enable intermodal freight corridors with deep logistical impact.

Every great city reveals its intelligence by how it moves people. The maxim applies even more harshly to moving an entire country. Brazil seems to have awakened to this logistical truth.

The National Railway Plan, presented to President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva by Transport Minister Renan Filho, materializes this shift. Five major railway projects will be granted to the private sector. They total nearly five thousand kilometers of new railways and an estimated investment of approximately R$100 billion, as detailed by CNN Brasil in exclusive coverage of the announcement.

To ensure the economic viability of the ventures, the federal government will cover part of the contributions, assuming between 20% and 30% of the investments, depending on the project. The model addresses the high cost of rail infrastructure, which generates no revenue for the concessionaire until the tracks are operational. This often made previous auctions unfeasible.

The East-West Corridor serves as the backbone of the plan. It links the West-East Integration Railway (Fiol) and the Midwest Integration Railway (Fico), totaling about 2,400 kilometers in length. Fiol departs from Ilhéus (BA) and Fico is being executed by Vale as compensation for the extension of its contracts. The project now gains a much larger continental dimension.

The new transcontinental railway, which would connect the Bahia port to the Chancay port in Peru, would reduce the average cargo transit time to Asia from 40 to 28 days, according to projections by the Peruvian government in a memorandum of understanding signed with Infra S.A. and the China Railway Economic and Planning Research Institute. The National Secretary of Rail Transport, Leonardo Ribeiro, described the initiative as a strategic step that resumes 2015 studies with greater technical maturity and available infrastructure.

The route would cross Bahia, Goiás, Mato Grosso, Rondônia, and Acre before crossing the border into Peru, leveraging existing railway sections in Brazil. The proposal integrates into the so-called New Silk Road, China's global infrastructure investment program. Although Brazil has not formally joined the initiative, considering that Chinese investments in the country already bypass the need for institutional affiliation.

Beyond the transoceanic scale, the plan tackles historic domestic bottlenecks. The North-South Railway Extension, for example, covers 477 kilometers from Açailândia (MA) to the port of Vila do Conde (PA). The extension is crucial because the Carajás Railway, primarily operated for ore, offers little spare capacity for grain transport from MATOPIBA and the Midwest.

The Southeast Railway Ring, approximately 300 kilometers long, will connect Vitória (ES) to Itaboraí (RJ). It will integrate the Vitória-Minas Railway network with MRS Logística's grid. The project approved by ANTT in December 2025 was one of the first decided since 2021, signaling a resumption of the regulatory agency's decision-making capacity.

Ferrogrão, with 933 kilometers between Sinop (MT) and Itaituba (PA), remains the most challenging project in the portfolio. It faces environmental and engineering risks that led to a conciliation at the Supreme Federal Court. An update of the studies was carried out at the Court's request, as part of the route crossed Jamanxim National Park, requiring new technical resolution.

The Transnordestina, a project promised since 2010 and stalled for years, now has a delivery forecast for 2026 or 2027. Under the plan, it gains a commitment to connect with the North-South Railway in Estreito (MA), adding another 600 kilometers of track. The rebidding of the South Network, with a contract expiring in February 2027, was divided into three projects to ensure greater attractiveness: Paraná-SC Corridor, Rio Grande, and Mercosur.

The federal government has planned eight rail auctions in 2026. The first project, the Minas Rio Corridor, is already delayed, according to a CNN Brasil report that interviewed ANTT's director-general, Guilherme Sampaio. For small networks, called shortlines, the approach will be through public calls, a mechanism similar to that used in the port sector, with a bidding notice and reference project.

The strategy combines early renewals, concessions, rebidding, and authorizations in an effort to reorganize the existing network, unlock investments, and address both freight capacity and passenger infrastructure. Industry insiders admit that achieving two effective auctions on B3 as early as 2026 would already represent a significant operational turnaround after years of regulatory paralysis.

Intermodality as a structuring principle finally replaces the mindset of isolated corridors with an integrated system of railways, waterways, and ports. It expands outflow capacity and reduces dependence on road transport. What the National Railway Plan reveals is not merely a pipeline of auctions, but a new logistical intelligence. Railway engineering ceases to be treated as a vestige of the past and begins to operate as concrete technology of national sovereignty.
