From Rio to Salvador, rail-based transit re-emerges as a vector of urban efficiency, but exposes tensions among engineering, budget, and the right to the city.

Building a fixed track and putting wheeled transport on it is a contradiction. Fifteen years later, the city government proves me right. The statement by Rio councilman Paulo Messina, retrieved amid the processing of Rio de Janeiro's most ambitious urban mobility project, sums up a historic shift that is beginning to pull Brazil out of its road-centric inertia.

The 'VLTization', as the plan approved by the City Council and sent for Mayor Eduardo Paes' sanction was named, foresees the conversion of the Transcarioca and Transoeste corridors — which together span more than 80 kilometers — into Light Rail Vehicle (VLT) or Light Rail on Tires (VLP) lines. The initiative, detailed in an extensive report by the <a href="https://g1.globo.com/rj/rio-de-janeiro/noticia/2025/10/25/vltzacao-entenda-o-plano-da-prefeitura-do-rio-para-substituir-o-brt-por-vlt-ou-vlp.ghtml" target="_blank">G1 portal</a>, estimates an investment between R$ 12 and R$ 16 billion, structured through a Public-Private Partnership (PPP).

The technical rationality behind the project is crystal clear: a marriage between operational efficiency and asset longevity. Each line will have a capacity to move 16,000 passengers per hour per direction, a figure equivalent to the current BRT system, but with significantly lower operating costs in the long run.

The useful life of an electric train, as recalled by the municipal Secretary of Transport, Maína Celidonio, is far superior to that of a diesel articulated bus. The reduction in noise, pollutants, and permanent way maintenance costs makes rail a development technology, not just a means of displacement.

The debate over choosing between the traditional VLT and the VLP — which runs on tires with automatic guidance and electric traction — is a portrait of the financial engineering that permeates Brazilian mobility. The system imported from China may lower immediate deployment costs, but it carries the paradox of replicating, to some extent, the dependence on pneumatic friction that doomed the original BRT.

While Rio advances the plan to serve 1.7 million residents and transport up to 580,000 daily passengers, Salvador is already experiencing the materialization of transformation. Construction of the VLT in the Bahia capital and metropolitan region received the first 27,500 meters of rail that began arriving through the city's Port, a logistical milestone that accelerates the schedule for Section 2 between Paripe and Águas Claras.

The Bahia government announced that Section 1, linking Calçada to Ilha de São João in Simões Filho, has already reached 33.79% physical progress, as reported by <a href="https://g1.globo.com/ba/bahia/noticia/2025/10/02/obras-do-vlt.ghtml" target="_blank">G1 Bahia</a>. The expectation is that the first train, manufactured in Hortolândia (SP) and visited by Governor Jerônimo Rodrigues alongside community leaders, will arrive in December 2025 to begin dynamic testing in early 2026.

The 40-kilometer system with 42 stops, budgeted at R$ 5.4 billion, will not be limited to moving people. It incorporates a layer of artificial intelligence in automatic turnstiles, security cameras integrated via fiber optics with the subway, and real-time digital panels, proving that rail-based transit is the vector of digitalization of public space.

However, the pouring of concrete foundations exposes the social fissure that accompanies major urban interventions. In the Plataforma neighborhood, residents who inherited houses from former railway workers were surprised with the notification of expropriation of 14 properties on Almeida Brandão Street for the construction of a rectifier substation and a police station.

Receptionist Gabriele Santos described the anguish of having only one month to leave if she accepts the compensation, while the Bahia Transport Company (CTB) maintains that negotiations are individualized and respect non-negotiable technical criteria. The tension between the right to housing and the need for the system's energy infrastructure is a drama that will repeat itself in other capitals.

In parallel, a new expansion front is consolidating in medium-sized cities thanks to the expertise of the state-owned Valec (EPE), which took on the elaboration of the Technical, Economic and Environmental Feasibility Study (EVTEA) of the VLT in Bauru, in the interior of São Paulo. Leveraging the existing railway network, the project foresees 28.8 kilometers of extension, three branches, and over 30 stations, as reported by the <a href="https://abifer.org.br/projetos-de-vlts-no-brasil-ganham-impulso/" target="_blank">Brazilian Railway Industry Association (ABIFER)</a>.

The CEO of Valec (EPE), Jorge Bastos, defined the initiative as the consolidation of a new operational front for the company, bringing to the urban environment the same technical capacity that structured the great national logistics corridors. The state-owned company also conducts advanced studies for the VLT in Campina Grande, Paraíba, signaling that rail policy is no longer an exclusive privilege of coastal metropolises.

In downtown São Paulo, the 'Bonde São Paulo' project still seeks institutional support and resources to get off the drawing board. Although there is interest in revitalizing the new state administrative center, the project faces planning and financing challenges. It is evident that rail mobility intertwines with urban requalification, returning density and value to the city's fabric.

Switching rubber for steel, diesel for electric traction, is a civilizing course correction that shakes up the consolidated interests of the road industry. While councilman Pedro Duarte questions the nearly R$ 9 billion already spent on recovering the Rio BRT, the technical truth echoes Messina's initial statement: there is no greater contradiction than paving a fixed track and condemning it to the eternal wear of rubber.

Brazil at last seems willing to overcome the myopia of deployment cost and focus on the lifecycle cost. The return of VLTs is not a nostalgia for old streetcars, but the application of clean, predictable, and enduring financial engineering against the urban chaos that stifles the productivity of large cities.

It remains to be seen whether the pace of works, justice in compensation, and the serenity of PPP contracts will manage to keep rail as rail, and not as a new promise of modernity buried by improvisation. The historic window is open.
