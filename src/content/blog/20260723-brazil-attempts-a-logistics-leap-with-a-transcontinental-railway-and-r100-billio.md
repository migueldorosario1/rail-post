Rail integration with the Pacific can shorten continental distances and redefine the competitiveness of national agribusiness.

When technique organizes space, society gains time to live. The phrase, so often forgotten in the corridors of Brasília, now finds a concrete echo on the drawing boards that design the new Brazilian railway map.

The memorandum of understanding signed between the state-owned company Infra S.A. and the China Railway Institute of Research and Economic Planning in early July reignited the ambition to connect the port of Ilhéus, in Bahia, to the Peruvian terminal of Chancay, crossing the South American continent from east to west by rail. The project, still in the technical study phase, projects an impressive compression in the transit time of Brazilian cargoes bound for Asia.

This gain is not only about energy efficiency or emission reductions by replacing long road stretches with rail and sea transport. It is a brutal shortening in the capital cycle, accelerating the turnover of agribusiness and mining exports—sectors that support Brazil's trade surplus with China, the destination of almost a third of the country's foreign sales. The port of Chancay, inaugurated in 2023 with Chinese financing under the New Silk Road, would offer a direct channel to the Pacific, although Brazil's cargo bound for Asia still requires long transpacific navigation.

As detailed by the Ministry of Transport, the proposal envisions crossing Bahia, Goiás, Mato Grosso, Rondônia, and Acre, taking advantage of sections of already existing railways or those being structured on the Brazilian side. The cooperation agreement, with an initial five-year duration, focuses on intermodality and the economic, social, and environmental sustainability of the corridor, although there is still no definitive route, cost estimate, or start date for works.

The centerpiece of this puzzle is the Leste-Oeste Corridor, the junction of the Ferrovia de Integração Oeste-Leste (Fiol) with the Ferrovia de Integração do Centro-Oeste (Fico), totaling approximately 2,400 kilometers in length. Fiol departs from Ilhéus and already has one section granted and another under construction as a public work. Fico, in turn, is a Union project, with funds from the General Budget of the Union and financing from BNDES, without involvement of Vale. Once completed, the corridor will consolidate a broad-gauge backbone capable of channeling production from the Center-West directly to the Atlantic and, in the transcontinental horizon, to the Pacific.

This movement is not isolated. The National Railway Plan, about to be launched by the federal government after final adjustments with the Casa Civil and the Ministry of Finance, foresees the expansion of 4,700 kilometers of network with an investment of R$100 billion. Among the definite projects, besides the Leste-Oeste Corridor, are the extension of the Ferrovia Norte-Sul by 477 kilometers from Açailândia (MA) to the port of Vila do Conde (PA), the Southeast Railway Ring linking Vitória (ES) to Itaboraí (RJ) over about 300 kilometers, the concession of the Transnordestina and its connection with the Norte-Sul in Estreito (MA), and the controversial Ferrogrão.

The Ferrogrão, with 933 kilometers of planned tracks between Sinop (MT) and Miritituba (PA), on the banks of the Tapajós River, represents one of the most challenging projects in the portfolio. The railway would create a new export corridor through the ports of the Northern Arc, but faces an environmental impasse in the Federal Supreme Court (STF) for crossing the Jamanxim National Park. While the Court has not concluded its analysis, the government finds itself entangled in another dispute: the amount of reimbursement for technical studies prepared by Estação da Luz Participações (EDLP) over more than a decade.

The Ministry of Transport and the Advocate General of the Union (AGU) rejected the adjustment request that would have raised the bill from R$58.2 million, already inflation-adjusted, to R$172.9 million. According to the ministry, the 2016 decision set a clear cap for the indemnity, updated by the IPCA, and subsequent revisions would not justify additional remuneration. The president of EDLP, Guilherme Quintella, disputes the decision and states that the work accumulated over 575,000 man-hours by engineers, economists, and biologists, and that the project 'is not just the idea of a railway, but a deep study.'

In total, the 2026 agenda projects eight railway auctions, including shortline concessions — short-distance railways operated by public call —, early renewals of contracts such as that of the Ferrovia Centro-Atlântica (FCA), and re-tenders of returned networks, as in the cases of Malha Oeste and Malha Sul. The plan combines different instruments to unlock investments and reorganize a network that, in the words of the director-general of the National Land Transport Agency (ANTT), Guilherme Sampaio, will be mapped by the Inter-American Development Bank (BID) to identify cargo and passenger corridors with bidding potential.

Behind the scenes of the sector, however, expectations are much more modest than the official schedule. Sources heard by the press admit that they would be satisfied if two projects actually reach B3 by year-end. The first planned project, the Minas Rio Corridor, is already delayed, with the bidding notice expected for January now due to be published only in May and the auction pushed to August.

Despite the difficulties, the mere existence of a pipeline that links Fiol, Fico, Ferrogrão, the Norte-Sul extension, and the Southeast Railway Ring signals an operational turnaround not seen since the Fiol 1 auction in 2021. The outflow capacity these railways promise to add can transform Brazilian logistics geography, reducing dependence on Southeast ports and bringing agricultural and mineral production closer to Asian markets with an efficiency that road transport will never be able to offer. China has already put its engineers and its capital on the table; it is up to Brazil to decide whether it truly wants to shorten the path between Mato Grosso soy and the Asian factory floor.
