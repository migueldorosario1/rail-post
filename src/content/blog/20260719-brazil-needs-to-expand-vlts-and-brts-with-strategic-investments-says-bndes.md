Study by the development bank highlights the importance of expanding the network of medium-capacity systems to reverse the decline in passenger numbers, prioritizing cheaper and more efficient solutions.

<p>Every major city reveals its intelligence by how it moves people. Brazil has received a clear diagnosis about the stagnation of urban mobility. A comprehensive study by the Brazilian Development Bank (BNDES) mapped 194 projects, highlighting the need to expand the network of VLTs, BRTs, and monorails.</p>
<p>The survey, entitled National Urban Mobility Study, indicates that the country needs to invest R$ 500 billion by 2054. With the current metro network at 376 kilometers, the necessary addition is 323 kilometers. The expansion of medium-capacity modes, which must leap from the current 631 kilometers to 2,500 kilometers over the next three decades, is fundamental.</p>
<p>The logic defended by BNDES Infrastructure Superintendent Felipe Borim is clear. The average cost of R$ 50 million per kilometer for a BRT is much lower compared to the fiscal chasm of up to R$ 1 billion per kilometer for subways. In a country with scarce resources, the rationality of the VLT with its tracks embedded in the urban landscape proves more viable.</p>
<p>The loss of 43% of system demand between 2014 and 2023 exposes the urgency of this shift. The Minister of Cities, Jader Barbalho Filho, acknowledged the gravity, promising R$ 42 billion over four years. Previously, there was no consistent federal funding for the sector.</p>
<p>The advocacy for the VLT emerges as a technical response to a country exhausted by tariff mismanagement and modal disintegration. Borim summarized the scenario, stating that, given the difficulty of implementing pharaonic projects, cheaper and more suitable solutions, such as the Light Rail Vehicle, are essential.</p>
<p>The contrast between the potential and reality of Rio's VLT exemplifies national challenges. The 28-kilometer system with 32 trains transports 95,000 passengers per day, far below the contractual target of 243,000. Despite this, the technology eliminates the expensive overhead catenary in the central section, ensuring agility and silence amidst historic buildings.</p>
<p>The modernization of rail mobility faces the absence of full integration. The inauguration of the Gentileza Terminal in 2024 and the creation of the Margaridas Single Ticket begin to integrate the VLT network with BRTs, metro, and ferries. Silvia Bressan, unit director of VLT Carioca, bets on growth sustained by urban densification and real estate projects in the Center.</p>
<p>The technical flexibility of the mode allows it to penetrate pedestrian walkways and exclusive lanes without segregating pedestrians, operating with low-floor and level boarding. The energy efficiency of the VLT, less polluting than diesel buses, resonates with the climate emergency and decarbonization goals.</p>
<p>The criticism by urban planners of the dependence on overhead catenary systems collides with fiscal reality. Adapting the entire route to ground-level power would demand investments in supercapacitors that, for now, are only justified in corridors of greater visual density. Clarisse Cunha Linke, executive director of ITDP Brazil, points out that each model has its advantages and that large cities will depend on BRT and metro complementing each other with smart fare integration.</p>
<p>The optimistic scenario outlined by BNDES projects an explosive growth potential in user numbers. Fortaleza leads with a 501% jump, and Rio de Janeiro advances 270% if car restriction policies are adopted. Public-Private Partnerships (PPPs) are crucial, but they demand rigorous public scrutiny.</p>
<p>The social tension of a mode that aspires to be modern but operates in an unequal society, where enforcement falls more heavily on the poorest, is evident. BNDES, conditioning the transformative scenario on limiting fare expenditure to 6% of the average worker's income, lays bare that rail technology requires fare justice and permanent state subsidy.</p>
<p>The prospect of the VLT replacing saturated bus corridors, replicating the experience of European cities, is the great window of opportunity for Brazil to abandon road-based improvisation. While the freight railway plan advances, medium-capacity rail transport establishes itself as the most realistic and civilizing frontier for the daily lives of millions of Brazilians in the coming decades.</p>
