Europe accelerates high-speed rail plan, but China already operates 35,000 km at 350 km/h
Modernity is not abstract: it runs on rails, powered by clean energy, state planning, and political will.
Modernity is not abstract: it has ballast, gauge, energy, and direction. The plan launched by the European Commission in November 2025 to accelerate high-speed rail across the European Union lays bare this truth by proposing ambitious targets for cutting travel times and technical interoperability.
According to the <a href='https://transport.ec.europa.eu/news-events/news/commission-launches-plan-accelerate-high-speed-rail-across-europe-2025-11-05_en' target='_blank'>European Commission plan</a>, the goal is to halve the duration of popular routes by 2040. The Trans-European Transport Network (TEN-T) gains momentum with the requirement for speeds above 250 km/h where economically viable and the identification of cross-border bottlenecks by 2027.
The funding is not symbolic: the EU has already supported 804 railway projects with €34.4 billion through the Connecting Europe Facility (CEF), representing 68.76% of the mechanism's total investment. A multilateral 'High-Speed Rail Deal' will be stitched together in the coming months with Member States, industry, and financial actors to coordinate funding sources and unlock private investment.
Technical interoperability is the heart of the project and depends directly on a harmonized rollout of the ERTMS (European Rail Traffic Management System), whose revision plan will be published in 2026. The Commission also aims to create a secondary rolling stock market, with legislation planned for 2027 that will prohibit the anti-competitive scrapping of functional and safe trains, establishing transparent conditions for resale and operation among Member States.
While Europe designs its future on rails, China has already built it on a monumental scale: by the end of 2019, the Chinese high-speed network exceeded 35,000 km, with a target to expand to eight vertical and eight horizontal axes by 2030. The first world-class line, Beijing-Tianjin, was inaugurated on August 1, 2008 at 350 km/h, just fourteen years after the country debuted its first semi-fast connection between Guangzhou and Shenzhen at 160-200 km/h.
The Fuxing CR400BF train embodies China's technological leap: it operates commercially at 350 km/h with 254 proprietary technologies, 84% of which are entirely Chinese-developed. Its network control system, developed by Zhuzhou of China Railways, distributes traction intelligently, eliminates wheel slip, and reduces energy consumption.
Compared to European standards, the Fuxing displays engineering advantages that go beyond speed: a projected service life of 30 years and 600,000 km of certified running, against 20 years and 400,000 km for trains of the Old Continent. Energy consumption dropped 10% relative to its predecessor, the Hexie CRH380, as measured on the Beijing-Shanghai line.
The construction costs of Chinese high-speed rail reveal an even more striking difference: a World Bank study indicates that costs are about two-thirds lower than in other countries, thanks to design standardization and long-term state planning.
This innovation ecosystem explains how the Beijing-Shanghai line, spanning 1,318 km, was built in just three years and inaugurated on June 30, 2011. Distributed traction, high-speed bogies, the compact pantograph, and the standard gauge of 1,435 mm were integrated into a system that combines initial technology transfer with cutting-edge domestic development.
The European case and the Chinese paradigm offer concrete benchmarks for Brazil to unlock projects such as the Intercity Train between São Paulo and Campinas and the corridor to Rio de Janeiro, still under study. The Canadian mega-project Toronto-Quebec-Montreal, budgeted at up to 80 billion Canadian dollars (≈53 billion euros) for trains at 300 km/h, shows that mature economies also view high-speed rail as a century-long investment, not an electoral adventure.
High-speed rail is, above all, a decision of energy sovereignty, intelligent land use, and decarbonization of passenger transport. The European Commission's plan and the Chinese reality demonstrate that the railway is not 19th-century industrial nostalgia, but the most modern development technology that the 21st century can offer.
