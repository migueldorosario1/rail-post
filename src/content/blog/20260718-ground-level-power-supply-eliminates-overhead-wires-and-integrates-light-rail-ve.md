From Constitution Street in Rio to the Pilot Plan in Brasília, APS technology proves it is possible to modernize mobility without mutilating the landscape.
The maxim "speed is also a form of civilization" applies precisely to Rio de Janeiro. The Carioca light rail vehicle (VLT) demonstrates that it is possible to unite the colonial past with 21st-century engineering. The project, a Public-Private Partnership with a total investment of R$1.5 billion, foresees 28 kilometers of track and a fleet of 32 articulated vehicles. Currently, 14 kilometers are already in operation, carrying silent crowds through the Center and the Port Region.
Its main feat, however, is hidden beneath the historic cobblestones of Constitution Street. Instead of overhead catenary wires that disfigure the horizon, the Carioca VLT was the first in Brazil to adopt ground-level power supply technology, the APS system. Energy is supplied by underground power boxes installed along the permanent way. Collection shoes in a single module of the vehicle absorb the electric current when the train is over the rail, with no risk of shock to pedestrians or automobiles crossing the track.
Each 18-meter rail section was laid starting in 2015, as detailed by the Rio de Janeiro City Hall. Nighttime operations fixed sleepers, poured concrete, and laid a covering layer, always respecting the urbanization of each stretch along the modal’s route.
The result is a low-floor vehicle without rigid divisions between cars, slightly larger than its European counterparts, with 2.65 meters in width and 2.17 meters in height. Its modest average speed of around 17 kilometers per hour belies the efficiency of carrying up to 420 passengers per car.
Voluntary fare validation, integrated with the Bilhete Único (single ticket system), eliminates turnstiles and speeds up passenger flow at the 46 stations and stops planned for the full project. When all lines are operational, the system’s daily capacity will jump to around 300,000 passengers.
The model is not a luxury but a necessity in cities that dare to modernize without losing their character, as argued by engineer Tiago Augusto Alves of concessionaire CCR during a debate at SEESP. There, it became clear that the choice of APS was imposed by the landscape and the tropical climate.
Jurandir Fernandes, coordinator of the Advisory Council, emphasized that rail-based modals have specific niches, provided they are well planned. The Carioca VLT also represents a cultural paradigm shift, for society needs to learn to coexist with a means of transport that shares surface space with cars and pedestrians.
1,200 kilometers away, Brasília is preparing to replicate the recipe in an even more delicate setting. The future capital’s VLT, planned with 39 trains of 45 meters each and a capacity of 400 to 560 passengers, faces the federal heritage listing of the Pilot Plan by the National Historic and Artistic Heritage Institute (Iphan). Overhead catenary wires are prohibited by Iphan in the W3 region. As reported by O Cafezinho, the APS system emerges as the sole solution for the 24-station, 16-kilometer train to run without harming the capital’s monumental views.
The Rio experience teaches that APS requires a dedicated engineering ecosystem, including emergency power banks capable of moving the train for about 400 meters without external power supply. Constant air-conditioning, a non-negotiable item in the humid coastal heat, demands permanent recharging from the power boxes.
The billion-real investment in Rio covered not only tracks and vehicles but also the reurbanization of planters, traffic lights, signage, and drainage, as well as an archaeological survey. An original section of cobblestones from the First Reign now rests beneath the rails of Constitution Street, preserved during excavation of just half a meter deep.
The first stretch of the Carioca VLT connected the Novo Rio Bus Station to Santos Dumont Airport, passing through the Railway Tunnel under Providence Hill. The Integrated Operations and Maintenance Center, built in Gamboa, coordinates the logistics of a system connected to the metro, trains, ferries, BRT lines, and cruise terminals.
Brasília, in turn, fits into the New PAC effort, which prioritizes rail transport as a sustainability driver. The planned 30-year concession aims to attract private capital and guarantee the financial viability of the operation over the coming decades.
Both projects show that the civilization mentioned at the outset is not an abstraction but a steel rail fed by discreet power boxes. It is speed just enough to reorder urban space without wounding its memory.
While the Carioca VLT consolidates its 14 operational kilometers and awaits full network expansion, Brasília advances toward environmental licensing and detailed design. Brazil is re-embracing urban rail not out of nostalgia but as a development policy that respects heritage and incorporates the most advanced electric power supply engineering.
