Modernity is not abstract. It has ballast, gauge, energy, and direction.
Few infrastructures symbolize the marriage between heavy civil engineering and the digital revolution as well as the underground galleries of the Lisbon Metro. The LUMIRing project (Lisbon Underground Multicore Fiber Ring) was officially inaugurated, transforming the Blue Line into a 26-kilometer living laboratory for next-generation optical communications.
This is the largest multicore optical fiber testbed ever installed in the world, an infrastructure that places Portugal at the forefront of global research in data transmission. The underground network connecting Odivelas to Rato has been equipped with over 1,900 kilometers of multicore fiber, of which an impressive 728 kilometers are of the same type, ensuring technical uniformity for the tests.
According to the <a href="https://www.metrolisboa.pt/institucional/2025/11/03/metropolitano-de-lisboa-e-iscte-inauguram-maior-banco-de-testes-de-fibra-otica-multinucleo-do-mundo-3/" target="_blank">official press release from the Lisbon Metro</a>, the project's architecture uses fibers with four and seven cores, radically breaking with the paradigm of conventional single-mode fiber. In practice, this means multiplying the data traffic capacity in cables with the same external thickness, a feat that solves the physical bottleneck of current telecommunications networks without requiring new urban excavations.
The initiative is the result of a robust partnership between ISCTE – University Institute of Lisbon, the German Heraeus Covantics, and the Italian Tratos Cavi, combining academic excellence and cutting-edge industrial capacity. The project is not a mere sealed laboratory setup: it operates in a real urban environment, with train vibrations, temperature variations, controlled humidity, and all the electromagnetic challenges of a metropolitan railway in full operation.
The central concept is to validate multicore fiber as the ultimate medium for the data tsunami approaching with the expansion of artificial intelligence, tactile internet, and urban digital twins. The vice-rector of ISCTE for Research and Technological Modernization, Jorge Costa, highlighted that the testbed will transform Portugal into a global center for optical telecommunications, attracting investments and highly qualified jobs.
The statement is not empty rhetoric. The project has already attracted the interest of global operators such as AT&T, Deutsche Telekom, and Vodafone, giants that daily face the challenge of scaling the capacity of their backbone networks. In addition, a strategic collaboration has been agreed with the National Institute of Information and Communication Technology (NICT), the Japanese public agency that is an absolute benchmark in cutting-edge research in the sector.
The operational horizon for long-duration tests is set for 2026, marking the definitive entry of the Lisbon metro into the era of extreme digitalization. The scientist responsible for the project, Adolfo Cartaxo, confirmed that the large-scale tests with the Japanese will take place precisely in this realistic underground environment, validating the robustness of the technology under conditions that simulate the stress of a modern metropolis.
For the acting president of the Lisbon Metro, Maria Helena Campos, the initiative is a decisive step in the development of cutting-edge solutions still little tested on a global scale, with potential for direct application in the operator's own control and safety systems. The digitalization of infrastructure is not an academic luxury, but an operational necessity for trains running at ever shorter intervals and requiring instantaneous and unbreakable communication with control centers.
The logic behind LUMIRing echoes a global trend of transforming mobility tunnels into connectivity backbones. While Berlin is modernizing its U5 and U8 lines with state-of-the-art digital CBTC systems to reduce headway to less than 100 seconds, Lisbon bets on the deepest physical layer: the glass of the optical fiber itself.
With this move, the metro ceases to be just a carrier of passengers to become a carrier of data packets on the scale of terabits per second. Railway infrastructure, historically associated with steel and concrete, also becomes the fastest vein of the knowledge economy, housing the central nervous system of smart cities.
In the Brazilian context, the Lisbon experience lights a bright warning about the idle potential of the extensive underground networks of São Paulo and Rio de Janeiro. The installation of multicore fiber testbeds along São Paulo's metro lines could catapult data transmission capacity between data centers and 5G antennas, creating a competitive advantage without the need to dig new trenches in the already saturated asphalt.
The Portuguese project also demonstrates that public investment in mobility can generate parallel assets of extremely high technological value, monetizing each kilometer of tunnel as sovereign digital heritage. It is not a futuristic daydream, but applied engineering that already connects the Cidade Universitária station to the major research centers of Europe and Asia.
The inauguration ceremony of LUMIRing, attended by ministers Miguel Pinto Luz and Castro Almeida, sealed a paradigm shift in the perception of public transport. The Lisbon underground consolidates itself as a strategic asset of technological sovereignty, where each optical fiber core represents an invisible highway for the continent's data traffic.
By placing technology at the service of mobility and urban progress, the Lisbon Metro reinforces its structuring role in building resilient cities. After all, modernity is not a conceptual abstraction; it runs on rails, wrapped in laser light beams, encoding the future in the glass cores that now wind beneath the vibrant Portuguese capital.
