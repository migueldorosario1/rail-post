The VLTization approved in the Rio City Council exposes the multi-billion cost of historical hesitation and reopens the essential debate on railway technology and technical sovereignty in corridors that were born ready for the train.

"Building a fixed gutter and putting transport on wheels is a contradiction. Fifteen years later, the city hall proves me right." The phrase by councilman Paulo Messina, spoken during the vote on the VLTization project in the Rio City Council, is not just political irony. It is the perfect technical summary of an entire generation of urban planning that built permanent infrastructure to operate provisional vehicles, as if the city were afraid of its own rails.

The numbers are brutal and known by heart by those who follow mobility: the Transcarioca corridor is 35.6 kilometers long with 45 stations and terminals, linking Alvorada to Fundão. The Transoeste totals 44.6 kilometers and 41 stations and terminals, connecting Santa Cruz to Jardim Oceânico. Both were designed as typical BRTs, with dedicated at-grade lanes, without railway infrastructure.

The Rio de Janeiro City Hall has now approved the logical leap: replacing the BRT with Light Rail Vehicle (VLT) or similar systems, with an estimated cost between R$12 billion and R$16 billion via Public-Private Partnership, as detailed by the <a href="https://g1.globo.com/rj/rio-de-janeiro/noticia/2025/10/25/vltzacao-entenda-o-plano-da-prefeitura-do-rio-para-substituir-o-brt-por-vlt-ou-vlp.ghtml" target="_blank">G1 report</a> on the project. The construction period is up to 45 months, with partial operation at half that time, and demand projection reaches 580,000 passengers per day over 18 hours of operation. The planned capacity of 16,000 passengers per hour per direction equals the current BRT, but with lower operating cost and much longer lifespan.

The technical decision that now imposes itself carries a fascinating bifurcation for engineers and economists: steel rail or imported alternative technologies. The traditional VLT runs on metal rails with railway trucks, predictable wear and a rolling dynamic that favors energy efficiency and load capacity. Alternatives, such as the *Translohr* (French, obsolete) or *Bombardier GLT*, use rubber tires with automatic guidance — a sort of light metro on tires, electric, that eliminates the cost of installing rail but introduces technological dependency on external suppliers.

The difference is not cosmetic. The steel rail transfers loads with minimal contact area, reduces rolling resistance, and allows longer trains with lower installed power. The tire, although it offers better grip on slopes and less noise at low speed, multiplies energy consumption per ton transported and requires periodic replacements that burden maintenance contracts. Every kilometer of corridor that Rio paved was designed for buses, but the discussion on the feasibility of rails is crucial.

The municipal secretary of Transportation, Maína Celidonio, argued that "investments are higher, but the quality is greater and lasts longer", citing the superior lifespan of a train compared to a bus. The annual reduction of R$19.8 million in emission costs reinforces the environmental argument, but the heart of the matter is structural: Brazil spends fortunes building fixed gutters only to later save precisely on the system that runs inside them.

The Rio paradox echoes on a national scale. While Rio debates whether to put steel or rubber on its concrete, the federal government has structured a R$100 billion National Railway Plan, with 4.7 thousand kilometers of new tracks in projects such as Ferrogrão and the Southeast Rail Ring, <a href="https://www.cnnbrasil.com.br/economia/macroeconomia/com-investimento-de-r-100-bi-governo-lancara-plano-nacional-de-ferrovias/" target="_blank">according to a CNN Brasil report</a>. The country is learning — albeit slowly — that the rail mode is not railway nostalgia, but contemporary technology for logistical productivity and decarbonization.

The Ferrogrão, which will connect Sinop to Miritituba over 933 kilometers, illustrates the same dilemma of technical maturity: its feasibility studies began in 2014, were updated for over a decade, and even now the responsible company is in disputes with the Ministry of Transport over the reimbursement amount, as reported by <a href="https://www1.folha.uol.com.br/mercado/2026/07/ministerio-dos-transportes-barra-aumento-milionario-de-estudos-da-ferrograo.shtml" target="_blank">Folha de S.Paulo</a>. There are 575,000 hours of engineers, economists, and biologists invested in a project paralyzed by judicial and environmental disputes, while the grain harvest continues to be transported by trucks on saturated roads.

The Rio VLT dialogues with this same long timeframe of Brazilian infrastructure: it is thought of in 2010, the skeleton is built, it operates with makeshift solutions for fifteen years and only then is it admitted that the rail was the answer from the first stroke. Councilman Pedro Duarte questioned the transparency of the costs and recalled that the city has already spent almost R$9 billion on the recovery of the BRT system in the last four years, with the purchase of buses and renovation of stations. Public money used to patch up a transport mode that the municipality itself now declares obsolete.

The choice between VLT and alternative technologies transcends engineering and touches upon the geopolitics of technology. Chinese technologies arrive packaged with competitive financing and a promise of accelerated deployment, but tie the operation to a supply chain that does not transfer to the national industry. The rail-based VLT allows manufacturers installed in Brazil — with accumulated know-how in projects such as the Carioca VLT in the port region — to participate in the expansion with local content and the creation of qualified jobs.

Rio's own geography imposes constraints that favor the rail: extreme heat degrades tires more quickly, tropical rains require drainage systems that the wheel-rail contact solves with mechanical simplicity. A three-module light train on rails can operate with only four motor trucks, while the equivalent on tires multiplies components, sensors, and electronic failure points that depend on proprietary software updates.

The international connection reinforces the diagnosis. Brazil signed an agreement with China to study a transcontinental railway linking the Atlantic to the Pacific, from the port of Ilhéus to the port of Chancay in Peru, <a href="https://g1.globo.com/economia/noticia/2025/07/08/ferrovia-para-ligar-o-atlantico-ao-pacifico-veja-o-que-se-sabe-ate-agora-do-projeto-do-brasil-com-a-china.ghtml" target="_blank">according to a G1 report</a>. The same country that dreams of crossing the Andes Mountains on rails cannot hesitate to put steel on the corridors that already cross its second largest metropolis.

The Rio VLTization will benefit 1.7 million residents and may expand to São Cristóvão, Ilha do Governador, and Botafogo, creating an electrified surface network that dialogues with the metro and ferries. The cost of R$12 to R$16 billion, diluted in a PPP with a term of decades, is high but predictable — unlike the diffuse cost of maintaining a bus fleet that consumes diesel, breaks down frequently, and requires increasing subsidies with each contract renewal cycle.

The concrete gutter is there, waiting. It was dimensioned for axle loads that buses don't even scratch, and drained for a system that never came to be installed. Rio has before it the rare opportunity to correct a design error without needing to demolish anything, merely laying upon the existing base the rolling stock that always fit there. The technical discussion between steel and tire is legitimate, but the fundamental thing is that the rail — physical or metaphorical — finally defeats the asphalt that unduly occupied its place for fifteen years.
