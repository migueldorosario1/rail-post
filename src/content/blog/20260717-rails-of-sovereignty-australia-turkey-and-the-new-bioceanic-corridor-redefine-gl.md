Rails are becoming the material geometry of the future, redefining global logistics and economic sovereignty. Countries like Australia, Turkey and Chile invest in bioceanic and double-stack corridors, while Brazil needs to accelerate its projects to avoid falling behind.

Every great city reveals its intelligence by the way it moves people. This maxim, however, can be extended to freight transport, fundamental for the competitiveness and logistical sovereignty of nations. In the era of globalization, railway megaprojects are emerging as strategic instruments, transforming the way goods are moved and integrated into international markets.

Australia, for example, is betting heavily on the Inland Rail, a 1,600 km corridor between Melbourne and Brisbane, with capacity for double-stack trains. The project, valued at around 31 billion Australian dollars, aims to replace more than a hundred trucks by train, reducing emissions and freeing up congested highways. This investment is compared to a Suez Canal on rails, integrating the agribusiness from the interior to the Pacific ports and reinforcing the country's export capacity.

In Turkey, the Istanbul North Rail Crossing Project creates 127 km of line crossing the strait using the Yavuz Sultan Selim Bridge. With financing from the World Bank, the project promises to increase freight transport capacity from 3 million to 50 million tons per year, multiplying it sixteenfold. Half of the route will be built in tunnels, protecting the operation from adverse weather conditions and ensuring continuous operation. The project also includes technical training programs, open to women, reinforcing the inclusive nature of the initiative.

In the Southern Cone, the Longotoma Bioceanic Corridor, a consortium formed by the Chilean company Beler SA and Singapore's International Nusantara Investment, foresees an estimated investment of 9.6 billion dollars. The project will connect Mendoza, Argentina, to the Chilean coast of Valparaíso, through a modern freight and passenger railway network. The plan includes the construction of a 54 km trans-Andean railway tunnel, a 420 km double-track electrified railway line, a multimodal freight center in Longotoma, and a deep-water submarine port in La Ligua.

These megaprojects are not just ambitious engineering works; they represent strategic development choices. Australia, Turkey and the Southern Cone are prioritizing energy efficiency and scale, eliminating bottlenecks that limit economic growth. Double-stack freight trains double the capacity per train, combined with digital control and progressive electrification, generating scale gains impossible in road transport.

Brazil, despite having an excessively road-based logistics matrix, is beginning to realize the importance of these investments. The Petrocity project, which plans to connect the Center-West to Espírito Santo, is an example of an initiative that seeks to modernize the country's railway infrastructure. The railway complex, which includes the Juscelino Kubitschek Railway (EF 033) and the Minas Espírito Santo Railway (EFMES), aims to serve mineral and steel production, as well as facilitate the outflow of grains and ores destined for the international market.

According to Petrocity's president, José Roberto Barbosa da Silva, the project has national and foreign investors and will not require public funds. The forecast is to complete all environmental licensing and executive projects of the railway megaproject within five years, to speed up construction works. The return on investment, according to commissioned studies, will occur within 10 years, with amounts to be charged for transporting different types of cargo, such as grains, fertilizers, ore, wood and ornamental rocks.

The lesson that these countries offer Brazil is clear: governments that prioritize efficient logistics corridors integrate territories and eliminate bottlenecks that limit economic growth. In Brazil, where agribusiness still depends on trucks traveling thousands of kilometers, the message is direct: without a dense and modern railway network, there is no sustainable competitiveness. The State must plan and execute these works as a national priority, breaking with dependent and inefficient patterns.

Moreover, technology is being incorporated in an innovative way. In the United Kingdom, for example, the IntelliPan Network, funded with 5 million pounds by the British government, uses AI to detect catenary faults, reducing interruptions and increasing safety. Projects like this demonstrate the potential for innovation and efficiency that the combination of rails and advanced technology can provide.

Rails, therefore, represent a strategic development choice. Countries that build them today define their position on the economic map of the coming decades. Australia, Turkey and the Southern Cone are already charting their path. Brazil, with its natural wealth and logistical potential, cannot fall behind. It is time to prioritize the modernization of railway infrastructure and consolidate logistical sovereignty.
