The country structures a billion-dollar railway plan for grains and ore, but solemnly ignores the high-speed train technology that China itself is preparing to manufacture on São Paulo soil.

Rails are the material geometry of the future, but in Brazil they largely trace the extractive past of the economy. While the federal government prepares to launch a National Railway Plan with around R$ 100 billion in investments and 4.7 thousand kilometers of new tracks, there is not a single high-speed passenger line scheduled on the Brazilian horizon, a silence that contrasts with the growing presence of Chinese technology in the country.

According to <a href="https://www.cnnbrasil.com.br/economia/macroeconomia/com-investimento-de-r-100-bi-governo-lancara-plano-nacional-de-ferrovias/" target="_blank">CNN Brasil</a>, the package includes projects such as the East-West Corridor, the extension of the North-South Railway, the Southeast Railway Ring and the controversial Ferrogrão, all designed to shorten the distance between commodities from the interior and export ports.

The logic is relentless and reveals the DNA of the Brazilian network: in 2025, national railways transported 406.6 million tons of iron ore, equivalent to 73% of all cargo moved on rails, while agricultural products totaled only 104.4 million tons.

The average cost of deploying a railway in the country is around R$ 27 million per kilometer, more than three times the cost of a highway, which makes the concession model a perennial financial challenge. To make the auctions viable, the federal government intends to fund 20% to 30% of the contributions for each project, using resources obtained from renegotiating old contracts with giants such as Vale, Rumo and MRS Logística.

The Minister of Transport, Renan Filho, described the plan as a pipeline of projects with a defined auction schedule, but the math remains harsh for passengers: Brazil maintains only two interstate passenger lines, the Vitória-Minas Railway and the Carajás Railway, both operated by Vale under contractual obligation and without any vocation for high commercial speed.

While the government concentrates its ambition on broad-gauge logistics corridors for grains and ore, the world's largest manufacturer of high-speed trains is preparing its industrial landing in the interior of São Paulo. The Chinese state-owned CRRC is building a factory in Araraquara, with an initial investment of R$ 50 million, to produce rolling stock for the São Paulo Metro and the Intercity Train that will connect the capital to Campinas.

The Intercity Train project, budgeted at R$ 14 billion and operated by Grupo Comporte in partnership with CRRC itself, represents the only relevant passenger rail transport project on the Brazilian radar, but its projected commercial speed classifies it as medium speed, far from the high-performance Asian and European standards.

China's presence in the sector is not limited to train manufacturing: CCCC, China's infrastructure state-owned company, has already planted its flag at Porto São Luís and expressed formal interest in the Fico-Fiol corridor, while Xi Jinping's government sees Brazilian logistics as a piece of food security, as 70% of the soybeans and corn imported by China come from Brazil.

In July 2025, Brazil and China signed a memorandum of understanding to study a transcontinental railway linking the port of Ilhéus, in Bahia, to the port of Chancay, in Peru, crossing five Brazilian states and shortening cargo transportation time to Asia from 40 to 28 days, according to <a href="https://g1.globo.com/economia/noticia/2025/07/08/ferrovia-para-ligar-o-atlantico-ao-pacifico-veja-o-que-se-sabe-ate-agora-do-projeto-do-brasil-com-a-china.ghtml" target="_blank">a report by G1</a>.

The national secretary of Railway Transport, Leonardo Ribeiro, classified the initiative as a strategic step to bring continents closer, but the project still lacks a definitive route, cost estimate and construction schedule, repeating the cycle of studies that has characterized Brazilian railway planning for decades. Ferrogrão, another emblematic project, has been slipping since 2014 in legal and environmental disputes, with the company responsible for technical studies charging R$ 172.9 million for over a decade of work, while the government insists on reimbursing only R$ 58.2 million, as detailed by <a href="https://www1.folha.uol.com.br/mercado/2026/07/ministerio-dos-transportes-barra-aumento-milionario-de-estudos-da-ferrograo.shtml" target="_blank">Folha de S.Paulo</a>.

Intermodality, a technical concept that combines railways, waterways and highways to optimize logistics costs, emerges as a mantra in official speeches, but the operational reality reveals a country that still depends on trucks for most of its agricultural cargo. Brazilian road transport is artificially cheap due to decades of public investment concentrated on roads, while railways remained stagnant and hostage to iron ore.

The National Railway Plan tries to correct this historical distortion with new logistics corridors geared toward agribusiness, but the absence of high-speed passenger projects suggests that Brazil continues to treat rails as a mere appendage of the extractive economy, and not as the backbone of a modern mobility system.

The geopolitical irony is evident: China, which has developed the largest high-speed train network on the planet with over 40,000 kilometers of lines, is willing to manufacture trains, finance railways and even operate concessions in Brazil, but Brazilian interest is limited to using this partnership to transport soybeans and ore. CRRC masters the technology of trains operating at 350 kilometers per hour with state-of-the-art catenaries, digital signaling systems and international standard gauge, but its know-how in national territory will be employed to produce metro trainsets and regional trains of moderate speed.

As pointed out by <a href="https://investnews.com.br/negocios/brasil-tenta-de-novo-expandir-as-ferrovias-mas-ainda-falta-combinar-com-os-chineses/" target="_blank">InvestNews</a>, industry sources assess that unlocking investments in railways remains a formidable challenge, with or without Chinese participation, and that purely private demand for new projects has not yet proven viable without robust state subsidies.

The contrast between the gigantism of investments in commodity corridors and the complete absence of a national high-speed plan reveals a deliberate political choice, not a technical limitation. Brazil dominates the engineering of heavy rail construction, has potential passenger demand on the São Paulo-Rio-Belo Horizonte axis comparable to densely operated European routes, and has at its disposal the world's leading manufacturer of high-speed trains setting up on its territory.

What is missing is the strategic decision to treat rails as a vector of human development and territorial integration, and not just as a conveyor belt of raw materials to the ports of the Atlantic and the Pacific.

Rails are the material geometry of the future, but the future that Brazil designs with its R$ 100 billion is that of a country content to be the breadbasket and the mine of the world, transporting raw wealth on rails while its citizens remain stuck to the asphalt. CRRC's factory in Araraquara will be ready to produce cutting-edge technology from 2026, but the question echoing on Brazilian sleepers is whether there will ever be tracks equal to this industrial capacity.
