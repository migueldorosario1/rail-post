The technology of rechargeable batteries at stations eliminated the overhead wires from Rio's downtown and became a reference for São Paulo to revive the tram as a vector for urban requalification.

In the early hours of March 27, 2015, workers from the VLT Carioca concessionaire laid the first 400 meters of track on General Luiz Mendes de Morais Street, in Santo Cristo, the port area of Rio de Janeiro. Each steel rail measured exactly 18 meters in length, flexible enough to bend and follow the winding layout of a heritage-listed historic center, as detailed by the Rio City Hall at the time.

The gesture inaugurated much more than a roadwork: it paved the way for an engineering solution that would eliminate overhead wires from the landscape once and for all. The rechargeable battery system at stations turned the VLT Carioca into a visually clean and technically robust mode, with enough power to keep the air conditioning running uninterrupted in the city's relentless climate.

Unlike the old trams that relied on suspended wires and metal poles, the system operates with underground batteries installed along the permanent way. These batteries are recharged at the stations, ensuring autonomy for the journey between them. A collector shoe positioned on just one of the car's modules draws the electric current exactly when the train covers that segment, eliminating the risk of shocks for pedestrians, cyclists, or cars crossing the route.

Safety is intrinsic to the design: if there is no vehicle on the track, there is no exposed energy on the surface. This intelligence allowed the VLT to run through narrow streets and colonial pavements without the need for poles or overhead networks, preserving the visual integrity of areas such as Rio Branco Avenue and the surroundings of Morro da Providência.

In addition to the wayside batteries, each VLT Carioca car carries onboard energy banks that act as an immediate backup in case of a main supply failure. In operational tests, the vehicle was able to travel about 400 meters solely on stored charge, more than enough distance to reach the next station or a safe stopping point.

As explained by engineer Tiago Augusto Alves, from CCR, during a debate promoted by SEESP, the choice was for a constant recharge technology due to the indispensable use of air conditioning in Rio de Janeiro's climate. The backup is only for emergencies, ensuring that passengers' thermal comfort is never sacrificed even on the hottest days of the Rio summer.

The concept of continuous power supply solves a classic problem of light rail systems that operate with catenaries in historic centers: visual pollution and architectural interference. In Rio's case, the port area was undergoing a broad requalification process within the Porto Maravilha Urban Operation, and the absence of overhead wires was essential for the new mode to dialog with the built heritage instead of clashing with it.

The 18-meter rails were fixed on sleepers and received layers of concreting and final surfacing, always respecting the specific urbanization of each stretch. On Constituição Street, for instance, workers found original pavements from the time of Dom Pedro I only half a meter deep, requiring a delicate archaeological and preservation operation that allowed the VLT to cross the historic stones without damaging them.

With a width of 2.65 meters and a height of 2.170 meters, each VLT Carioca car transports up to 420 passengers at an average speed of 17 km/h, a figure that may seem modest but reflects the ideal cadence for a vehicle sharing urban space with pedestrians, cars, and bicycles. The operation adopts a voluntary validation system, integrated with the Bilhete Único (universal ticket), relying on spot checks and the population's adherence to a more civilized model of public transport usage.

The total investment in the VLT Carioca reached R$ 1.5 billion, covering not only the rolling stock and batteries but all the adaptation of the urban environment, including smart traffic lights, drainage of central reservations, traffic signaling, and the construction of the Integrated Operation and Maintenance Center in Gamboa. The planned network is 28 kilometers, of which 14 kilometers are already in operation, connecting the Novo Rio Bus Terminal to Santos Dumont Airport and linking subway, ferry, BRT, and conventional bus terminals into an integrated network.

Now, São Paulo is preparing to replicate the model with the Bonde São Paulo project, which has received a release signal of R$ 1.4 billion from the federal government's Growth Acceleration Program. The project foresees a 12-kilometer network, with two lines that will connect five bus terminals, nine subway stations, and two CPTM stations, totaling 27 boarding and alighting stations in the heart of São Paulo.

Here, the rechargeable battery system at stations again emerges as an unavoidable technical solution, because São Paulo's central region also demands respect for architectural heritage and the pedestrian scale. The future Bonde São Paulo line will pass by icons such as the Theatro Municipal, Sala São Paulo, the Municipal Market, and the Anhangabaú Valley, where the absence of catenaries will be as important to the urban experience as the efficiency of the transport itself.

The Rio experience demonstrates that the battery system is not just an aesthetic sophistication but a concrete enabler of mobility in areas where burying high-voltage underground networks would be unfeasible and where every centimeter of landscape matters. Engineering solved the problem with a sectioning system so fast that the transition between energized segments occurs imperceptibly for the passenger, while the driver simply follows the rhythm of the permanent way.

The sleepers, the concreting, and the 18-meter rails of the VLT Carioca taught that it is possible to return the tram to large Brazilian cities without the stigma of tangled wires and outdated looks. The Bonde São Paulo will be proof that that early morning of 2015 in Santo Cristo was not just the start of a construction work, but the birth certificate of a new standard of light transport for Brazil.

When fully operational, the VLT Carioca will have a capacity of 300 thousand passengers per day, while the Bonde São Paulo will absorb flows coming from the East, South, North, and West zones through the Dom Pedro II, Bandeira, and Princesa Isabel terminals. Both projects share the same logic: to requalify the center through mobility, and not to shrink mobility to fit into a congested center.

The constant recharge technology solved the equation that seemed unsolvable: providing thermal comfort without sacrificing the landscape, power without visual pollution, safety without fences or heavy-handed isolation. The rechargeable batteries at stations turned the rail into an intelligent conductor of energy, and turned the VLT into a mode that finally deserves the adjective contemporary.
