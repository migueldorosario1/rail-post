A study by the development bank mapped projects in several metropolitan regions and projects a massive investment to save medium-capacity public transport, which lost 43% of passengers in the last decade.

Modernity is not abstract. It has ballast, gauge, energy, and direction; and Brazil, despite the road-building nightmares of the past, is beginning to measure the exact extent of its backwardness and the investment needed to overcome it.

A BNDES study revealed that the country needs to significantly expand the BRT and VLT network by 2054. The current VLT and BRT network in Brazil has about 450 km, and the metro, a high-capacity system, has approximately 480 km. The goal is to expand these networks to better meet growing urban demands.

The cost of this urban redesign, which mapped several projects in various metropolitan regions, is breathtaking and requires an investment of R$ 500 billion over the next three decades. The superintendent of the Infrastructure Area of BNDES, Felipe Borim, when presenting the data, was clear in demystifying the war between modes and pointing out the harsh costs of Brazilian engineering.

“The need to mobilize resources is enormous. We will face resource scarcity and difficulty in implementing these projects in the future,” Borim warned, highlighting that the average implementation cost is a decisive factor in choosing the technology. While a kilometer of VLT or BRT costs around R$ 50 million, a kilometer of metro can reach the stratosphere of R$ 1 billion, justifying the adoption of surface solutions whenever population density allows abandoning the underground.

The financial dilemma, however, is only one layer of the crisis laid bare by the study. According to the <a href="https://oglobo.globo.com/brasil/noticia/2025/08/04/estudo-do-bndes-aponta-que-brasil-precisa-dobrar-metro-e-quadruplicar-os-brts-e-vlts.ghtml" target="_blank">BNDES survey</a>, Brazilian public transport suffered a hemorrhage of 43% of passengers between 2014 and 2023, a brutal emptying that undermines the fare-based sustainability of the system itself.

It is not enough, therefore, to spend billions on new rails and segregated corridors if a mass of users has permanently migrated to private cars and app-based motorcycles in search of comfort and predictability. “This is a consequence of a service that is not of good quality due to lack of planning and good projects. At the same time, the loss of passengers deepens the crisis, because you reduce demand in a system that already has financing problems,” diagnosed Borim.

Rescuing the lost passenger requires smart fare engineering and multimodal integration that Brazilian state competence has rarely delivered. The optimistic scenario projected by the development bank, which foresees an increase of up to 501% in the number of people served in cities like Fortaleza — jumping from 213,000 to 1.2 million users —, depends on complex social and political premises. It is necessary to ensure that the fare does not compromise more than 6% of the average worker's income, integrate different modes under a physically seamless single ticket, and, above all, discourage car use with unpopular measures such as parking space restrictions. The executive director of ITDP Brazil, Clarisse Cunha Linke, reinforced this mandatory symbiosis between modes: “All large cities will have to have BRT and metro complementing each other, well integrated. Each model has its advantages.”

The VLT, in this context, resurfaces not as a nostalgic tram from Rio's Lapa, but as a technological vector for densification and urban requalification. Its ability to overcome steep gradients, the energy efficiency of electric motors, and the comfort of running on rubber-tired rails position it as the guiding thread of the compact city of the future.

It is the perfect alternative for corridors where a monorail would be an elevated white elephant and the BRT, even with bi-articulated buses, already operates at the limit of operational saturation. Unlike the chaos of buses competing for space in congested lanes, the VLT commands respect from traffic with its signal priority and rigid pavement, creating an immutable axis of real estate development.

However, implementation requires a rigor that Brazilian construction culture often disregards. Building thousands of kilometers of medium-capacity infrastructure is not just pouring concrete; it is integrating intelligent traffic control systems, ensuring predictive maintenance of trains, and, above all, shielding operation contracts from the political capture that historically turns terminals into electoral fiefdoms.

The intra-urban medium-capacity railway, like the National Railway Plan expected to inject R$ 100 billion into 4.7 thousand kilometers of freight tracks, forms the backbone of national logistics, but its social function is even more sensitive. While the freight train silently moves soybeans from the Midwest to the ports, the VLT moves the citizen, and it is in this daily movement that the quality of public management is measured.

The Minister of Cities, Jader Barbalho Filho, promised R$ 42 billion over four years for mobility, but admitted the need for “continuity in these actions,” recognizing the historical funding vacuum. The true track to be built is that of administrative continuity, without which the R$ 500 billion will be just a large number in a PDF, and not the ballast of modernity that Brazilian cities cry out for.
