Rio's VLT completed a decade with numbers below what was promised, but with sufficient technical grounding to sustain the most ambitious plan in Brazilian urban mobility.

Modernity is not abstract. It has ballast, gauge, energy, and direction.
The Light Rail Vehicle (VLT) of Rio de Janeiro has just completed ten years of operation, offering the country the most concrete benchmark to measure the achievements, gaps, and ambitions of Brazilian urban transportation. Inaugurated in June 2016, Rio's VLT transformed the landscape of the city center with 28 kilometers of extension, four lines, and 32 trains in daily operation.
As reported by the <a href='https://oglobo.globo.com/rio/noticia/2026/06/07/uma-decada-de-vlt-transporte-mudou-a-paisagem-enfrentou-o-centro-esvaziado-e-projeta-recuperacao.ghtml' target='_blank'>O Globo newspaper</a> in a recent review, the system operates from 5 a.m. to 11 p.m. and currently transports 95,000 passengers per business day. The contractual target, however, was 243,777 daily users, a figure calculated in feasibility studies available on the website of the Carioca Partnerships and Investments Company, CCPar.
The unit director of VLT Carioca, Silvia Bressan, explains that the contractual reference value was modeled in a different context for the city and Brazil, before the economic crisis and the Covid-19 pandemic emptied the city center. Bressan prefers to look at the recent recovery, driven by fare integration and new connections.
Fare integration was still the critical node of this ecosystem until, in early May, the Rio City Hall created the Margaridas Single Ticket, BUM, connecting passengers from the Baixada Fluminense to the VLT from the Margaridas Terminal. The operator views the novelty with optimism, although there is still no consolidated data on the impact on demand. What is already measured with precision is the change in the urban landscape: Rio Branco Avenue became exclusive territory for pedestrians and rails, while Praça Quinze was reborn after the demolition of the Perimetral viaduct.
The total cost of the work reached R$ 1.157 billion, with R$ 532 million in federal funds from the PAC Mobility and R$ 625 million advanced by the public-private partnership. Over ten years, seven contractual amendments were signed, including one right in 2016 that removed from the original scope the 2.6-kilometer stretch to Praia Formosa, a vital connection with the Novo Rio Bus Station. The suspension of municipal contributions during the financial crisis of Marcelo Crivella's administration led the concessionaire to threaten to hand over the operation in 2019, when the debt already reached R$ 150 million.
Despite the bumps, the turnstile-free mode has consolidated itself as the backbone of mobility in the city center, with the Cristiano Ottoni stop next to Central do Brasil, Candelária, and Pereira Reis ranking among the busiest. Fare evasion enforcement occurs continuously, with a fine of R$ 170 for those who do not validate the ticket, and the recent implementation of Pix in validators promises additional traceability. The user profile is mostly workers who abandoned buses to circulate through the center, although there are complaints about unlit destination signs and trains wrapped in advertising that make it difficult to see the landscape.
Now, Rio de Janeiro is preparing for the next leap, transforming the VLT from a central system into a metropolitan structuring axis. The VLTization plan, under discussion in the City Council, foresees converting 80.2 kilometers of BRT corridors into Light Rail or Light Rail on Tires lines.
According to a report by the <a href='https://g1.globo.com/rj/rio-de-janeiro/noticia/2025/10/25/vltzacao-entenda-o-plano-da-prefeitura-do-rio-para-substituir-o-brt-por-vlt-ou-vlp.ghtml' target='_blank'>G1 portal</a>, the Transcarioca with its 35.6 kilometers and 45 stations between Alvorada and Fundão, and the Transoeste with 44.6 kilometers and 41 stations between Santa Cruz and Jardim Oceânico would be completely converted. The estimated investment is between R$ 12 billion and R$ 16 billion, with works scheduled to last up to 45 months and partial operation halfway through the period.
The modeling foresees a Public-Private Partnership with the division of contributions between the private sector and the public authorities, aiming for a capacity of 16,000 passengers per hour per direction and serving 1.7 million residents, with the potential for 580,000 daily passengers in 18-hour operation. The city's technical study, prepared with support from BNDES, projects an annual reduction of R$ 19.8 million in emissions, noise reduction, thermal comfort, and economic activation in neighborhoods adjacent to the corridors.
The VLP, an alternative with technology imported from China that runs on tires with automatic guidance and electric propulsion, emerges as a cheaper option than the traditional VLT, dispensing with the overhead catenary and reducing infrastructure costs. The debate among council members exposed the underlying tension: the city has already spent almost R$ 9 billion on recovering the BRT system over the last four years with bus purchases, station renovations, and terminal construction, and now may abandon part of this structure.
Councilman Pedro Duarte questioned the transparency of costs and the schedule, while Paulo Messina argued that rail transport should have been adopted since 2010, when the corridors were built. 'Building a fixed channel and putting wheeled transport on it is a contradiction,' Messina stated, echoing a technical argument that fifteen years later gains strength: the service life of a train is much longer than that of an articulated bus.
What the tenth anniversary of Rio's VLT reveals is not only the resilience of a system that survived crises, a pandemic, and fare evasion, but also the accumulated technical maturity to plan the largest expansion of light rail transport in the country. Each stop, each integration, each real estate development attracted by the rails are living data that feed the VLTization project with a grounding in reality. Brazil, which still stumbles with railway works dragged down by legal disputes and billion-dollar readjustments in technical studies, as demonstrated by the Ferrogrão imbroglio, has in Rio the evidence that well-managed urban rails deliver measurable transformation.
Modernity is not abstract — but neither is it automatic. Rio's VLTization carries the responsibility of proving that technical boldness can overcome political inertia and passenger distrust, exactly as the city center's VLT proved over a decade that rails, when well grounded, change entire cities.
