The first section of Line 6-Orange arrives with assisted operation, geotechnical challenges, and a quilombola archaeological site on the way, while the governor reconsiders his plan to hand over the network to the private sector.

The ability to change one's mind is fundamental. The phrase, spoken by São Paulo governor Tarcísio de Freitas on the eve of the inauguration of the first section of Metro Line 6-Orange, reflects a profound shift in politics and underground works.

After 18 years of announcements, corruption scandals and stoppages, the city's deepest subway line finally opens its doors. The section, linking João Paulo I station in Freguesia do Ó to Perdizes, begins assisted operation with reduced hours and no fare charged, as detailed by the newspaper <a href="https://oglobo.globo.com/brasil/sao-paulo/noticia/2026/07/01/sp-inaugura-nova-linha-do-metro-mas-segue-com-rede-menor-que-a-de-outras-metropoles-e-muda-plano-de-concessoes.ghtml" target="_blank">O Globo</a>.

This feat, however, is just the beginning of a complex project involving engineering, archaeology and finance. Originally designed by former governor Geraldo Alckmin to operate in 2011, the line faced the dissolution of consortia in the Lava-Jato scandal and geotechnical challenges that prevented any linear timeline.

Lúcio Matteucci, director of Acciona, the Spanish concessionaire responsible for the Public-Private Partnership (PPP), explained that the differences encountered during excavation generated unforeseen additional services. This geotechnical event resulted in a contractual addendum of R$ 3.69 billion, raising the total investment to nearly R$ 22.6 billion.

The technical density of the work contrasts with the archaeological lightness that emerged from the subsoil. In the excavations of the 14 Bis station, in the Bela Vista neighborhood, the soil revealed a site linked to the Quilombo Saracura, a landmark in the history of the black population in central São Paulo.

The stoppages, ordered between 2023 and 2025 by the Institute of National Historical and Artistic Heritage (Iphan), not only delayed the machines but rewrote the subway's toponymy. Social pressure led the government and concessionaire to rename the stop 14 Bis-Saracura, integrating quilombola memory into the transportation infrastructure.

Despite the recently inaugurated 11.7 kilometers, São Paulo's subway network still lags behind compared to other global metropolises. While Beijing boasts over 800 kilometers of underground tracks, São Paulo, with the new additions, reaches an extension that still does not rival the systems of Santiago or Mexico City in Latin America.

With full completion expected next year, when the 15 planned stations are expected to absorb around 600 thousand daily passengers, Line 6-Orange consolidates itself as a vital axis. It will connect the Brasilândia periphery to the city's hospital and university heart, drastically reducing travel time in a region choked by road dependence.

The quietest transformation, however, lies in the political strategy of the Palácio dos Bandeirantes. A historical advocate of injecting private capital into the rails, Tarcísio signaled that he no longer intends to grant lines operated by the public company, such as the Blue and Red lines, to the private sector.

The argument used was the risk of market concentration in a few economic groups, a move that seeks to preserve state governance over a transport mode that, according to the governor, operates efficiently. The new stance reopens the debate on the economic-financial rebalancing of current contracts, using the owed resources in direct investments rather than transferring management.

Line 6-Orange, ironically built under the PPP regime with Linha Uni, thus becomes a hybrid management laboratory. It demonstrates that private investment is capable of drilling through the complex Paulista soil, while the public power signals a clear limit for the alienation of its assets.

The branch that cuts through the subsoil will not just transport passengers from Freguesia do Ó to Perdizes. It carries with it the weight of almost two decades of waiting, the resilience of an archaeological past that refuses to be buried, and proof that development on rails demands, above all, the political capacity to recalculate the route.
