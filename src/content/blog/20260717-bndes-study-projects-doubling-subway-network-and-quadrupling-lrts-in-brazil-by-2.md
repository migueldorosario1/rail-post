The monumental cost of R$500 billion challenges the country to overcome the passenger hemorrhage and consolidate a civilizational leap on rails, while Chinese industry establishes its base in Araraquara.

Tracks are the material geometry of the future, a steel-and-concrete equation that separates urban stagnation from civilizational development. A monumental study by the National Bank for Economic and Social Development (BNDES) has just mapped the geography of this equation for Brazil, revealing a scenario of extreme ambition and colossal costs that will require the reinvention of mobility financing over the next three decades.

The survey, named the National Urban Mobility Study, X-rayed 194 projects in 21 metropolitan regions and arrived at a relentless diagnosis: the country needs to double its subway network, jumping from the current 376 kilometers to around 700 kilometers by 2054. In parallel, medium-capacity systems — BRTs and Light Rail Vehicles (LRTs) — will have to be quadrupled, expanding from 631 kilometers to an impressive 2.5 thousand kilometers, according to data compiled by the state bank.

The total estimated cost for this transformation — which also encompasses urban trains and bus corridors — could reach R$500 billion, a volume of capital that defies the historical logic of chronic underinvestment in the Brazilian public sector. The cost engineering mathematics is rigorous and exposes the abyssal difference between modes: while each kilometer of subway can consume up to R$1 billion, a quality BRT kilometer can be built for about R$50 million, a disparity that forces managers into a permanent dilemma between capacity and fiscal viability.

The context of this demand is paradoxical and reveals the inner workings of a crisis that goes far beyond the lack of new stations. Data from the BNDES itself, presented by the Infrastructure Area superintendent, Felipe Borim, show that Brazilian public transportation suffered a 43% hemorrhage in passenger numbers between 2014 and 2023, a mass exodus to cars and app-based motorcycles that tears apart the fare-based financial balance.

The phenomenon is not just a pandemic hangover, but the aggravation of a destructive trend of collective abandonment that was already detected at an annual drop rate of 15% before the health crisis. The Minister of Cities, Jader Barbalho Filho, defended the continuity of actions in response to historical paralysis, and recalled that over the last four years of the current administration, planned investment amounts to R$42 billion, a sharp break from a previous period when federal financing for the sector simply did not exist.

While the Brazilian state rehearses its long-term planning, Chinese industry moves with the operational speed of a power that integrates engineering, logistics, and geoeconomic interests in one fell swoop. The giant CRRC, the world's largest rolling stock manufacturer, is building a factory in Araraquara, in the interior of São Paulo, with an initial investment of R$50 million, as the InvestNews portal pointed out when reporting the railway offensive of Asian capital in the country.

The São Paulo plant has a surgical and non-speculative objective: to start producing trains from 2026 to supply the demands of the São Paulo Metro and the ambitious Intercity Train, which will connect the capital to Campinas through a consortium in which CRRC itself is a partner. The industrial location, far from being a random fact, inserts Brazil directly into the route of cutting-edge railway technology, breaking a historical dependence on sporadic imports and creating a local ecosystem for maintenance and development of highly complex train compositions.

The presence of the Chinese state-owned company on the Brazilian map tackles one of the most problematic knots in rail expansion in the developing world: the unavailability of private financing for projects that, by their capital-intensive nature, take decades to generate returns and mature only with some form of state subsidy. In the BNDES plan, this financial architecture is the core of the difficulty, and the public bank emerges as the natural guarantor agent to lend very long-term resources, with grace periods during the construction phase and debt profiles that can stretch up to 60 years.

The technical diversity of the mapped solutions reveals a strategic maturity that escapes the pharaonic passions of the past, recognizing that the rail system — subway, monorail, or LRT — and the segregated BRT pavement need to operate in a complementary and integrated manner in physically coordinated networks. The project that provides for Line 3 of the Rio de Janeiro Metro, connecting Niterói to São Gonçalo with 22 kilometers of extension and capacity to serve over 400 thousand passengers per day, exemplifies the type of ultra-high-density intervention that becomes financially unfeasible if thought of only from the perspective of the fare paid at the turnstile.

The transformative power of rails translates into concrete numbers when the BNDES study models optimistic scenarios of population service, conditioning success on a nonnegotiable policy of maximum fare limited to 6% of the average worker's income. Under this arrangement, the city of Fortaleza — which today serves a modest 213,000 people on its high- and medium-capacity modes — could leap to a coverage of 1.2 million users, an exponential variation of 501% that would change the very metropolitan structure of the Ceará capital.

The bet on high-capacity mobility signals that the country is willing to pay the price of urban density, assuming that the cost of an underground kilometer of R$1 billion is paid not in fares, but in real estate revaluation, in reducing the commuting time of the workforce, and in the definitive replacement of diesel burned in congestion. The BNDES, by mapping 194 projects and filtering those with technical maturity and effective social impact, moved the discussion from the realm of election promises to the domain of financial engineering and concrete implementation logistics.

The deafening silence that precedes the announcement of the final list of priority projects, expected in the coming months by the bank, is filled by the noise of the Chinese drilling machines being installed in the interior of São Paulo, a reminder that while national financing fails to gain traction, the geopolitics of rails is already unfolding in Araraquara. The material geometry of the future, after all, does not wait for those who hesitate before the civilizational price of moving on steel.
