The maturity of the Rio system and the ambition of Bahia show that rail transportation has gone from being a promise to becoming state policy in Brazil, reorganizing urban space and the economy of cities.

When technique organizes space, society gains time to live. This maxim, which echoes in any major metropolis that has replaced asphalt chaos with the precision of rails, has never been as tangible in Brazil as it is now.

Rio de Janeiro has just celebrated a decade of operation of its Light Rail Vehicle (VLT), a system that has redefined the landscape of the city center and the port area, while Salvador is making rapid strides to implement an even more ambitious and integrated plan.

The Carioca VLT, which winds through 28 kilometers with its four lines and 32 low-floor trains with modern silhouettes, was born from an investment of R$ 1.157 billion. The funding was a financial engineering feat that combined R$ 532 million from the PAC Mobilidade with R$ 625 million advanced through a Public-Private Partnership (PPP), as detailed by the concessionaire in a recent balance sheet.

Today, carrying 95,000 passengers per day, the system still operates below the contractual target of 243,000, a deficit that did not materialize due to the brutal impact of the Covid-19 pandemic and the economic crisis that emptied the city center. The unit’s director, Silvia Bressan, sees recovery underway, sustained by new attraction hubs, such as the Gentileza Intermodal Terminal.

The inauguration of this hub, through which 78,000 people circulate daily, connected the VLT to 16 bus lines and seven BRT lines, injecting new life into the system. The engine gained even more strength last May with the creation of the Bilhete Único Margaridas (BUM), which allows passengers from the Baixada Fluminense to integrate with the VLT from the Margaridas Terminal, a tariff integration feat that dissolves the physical boundaries of the city.

The dynamics of the modal, which operates without turnstiles, relying on electronic validation, reflect a city in transformation. Stops like Pereira Reis, in Santo Cristo, have exploded in movement after real estate launches, proving that the rail is a powerful inducer of urban development, not just a means of transport.

If the daily operation faces the distraction of pedestrians absorbed in cell phones and the challenge of fare evasion, the legacy of a decade is solid enough to project a leap in scale. The municipality has already approved in the City Council a radical plan, nicknamed 'VLTzação', to convert structuring BRT axes into VLT or VLP (Light Vehicle on Tires) lines, with an estimated budget between an impressive R$ 12 and R$ 16 billion.

The focus is on the Transcarioca (35.6 km) and Transoeste (44.6 km) corridors, which would be converted to serve up to 580,000 daily passengers. <a href="https://g1.globo.com/rj/rio-de-janeiro/noticia/2025/10/25/vltzacao-entenda-o-plano-da-prefeitura-do-rio-para-substituir-o-brt-por-vlt-ou-vlp.ghtml" target="_blank">The Rio City Hall project</a> is seriously evaluating VLP technology, an electrically guided vehicle automatically and without the need for overhead wires, imported from China, which offers a modern and lower-cost alternative to conventional rail, reusing the existing segregated channel.

While Rio plans its metamorphosis for the next decade, Salvador already sees the construction of its VLT gaining concreteness, with the unloading of the first 3,000 meters of rails at the Port. The material, which will total 27,500 meters, will be used on Section 2, between Paripe and Águas Claras, with installation of the permanent way scheduled for November 2025.

The Bahian project, budgeted at R$ 5.4 billion, will be 40 kilometers long with 42 stops, with Section 1 (Calçada to Simões Filho) already showing a physical progress of 33.79%. The endeavor is not without social tensions, such as the justified complaints from residents of Plataforma who feel surprised by expropriations for the construction of a rectifier substation, a vital component for the system’s energy supply, but whose implementation requires a deep and respectful dialogue with the pre-existing urban fabric.

The Bahia Transport Company assures that each case is treated individually, while the trainsets, with their modern Wi-Fi systems, artificial intelligence on the bogies, and digital panels, promise to revolutionize the Subúrbio Ferroviário. In essence, what is seen from Rio to Salvador is no longer a nostalgic experiment, but the mature and technically rigorous adoption of rail as the backbone of mobility and urban development in Brazil, proving that when engineering organizes territory, the city reconciles with the future.
