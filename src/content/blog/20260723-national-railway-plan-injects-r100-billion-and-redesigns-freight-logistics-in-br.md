The government bets on five mega railway projects to break bottlenecks in grain and ore outflow, integrating the country from end to end.

Speed is also a form of civilization, and Brazil seems to have finally understood the urgency of applying it to freight transport. The National Railway Plan, presented by the federal government, projects R$100 billion in investments and more than 4,700 kilometers of new tracks to unlock national logistics.

The Minister of Transportation, Renan Filho, detailed the pipeline of projects to President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva and prepares the official launch of the initiative. The heart of the plan are five railway concessions that promise to integrate strategic corridors such as FIOL, FICO, and the North-South Railway.

The East-West Corridor will bring together about 2,400 kilometers of tracks, connecting Ilhéus in Bahia to Lucas do Rio Verde in Mato Grosso. This axis unifies the West-East Integration Railway (FIOL) and the Center-West Integration Railway (FICO), creating a direct route for the outflow of commodities from the Center-West through the Atlantic.

The extension of the North-South Railway to the port of Vila do Conde in Pará adds 477 kilometers and offers an alternative to the saturated Port of São Luís. The new connection breaks the dependence on the Carajás Railway, currently consumed primarily by Vale's ore transportation.

The Southeast Rail Ring designs 300 kilometers between Vitória and Itaboraí, connecting the Vitória-Minas Railway (EFVM) to the MRS Logística network. The interconnection strengthens intermodality in the country's most industrialized region, creating additional routes for cargo that currently suffocate on highways.

Transnordestina reappears in the plan with the promise of expansion. However, there is no technical design, feasibility study, or federal decree that envisions a physical connection with the North-South Railway at Estreito (MA). The work, which has been dragging on for more than a decade, is scheduled for delivery between 2026 and 2027, according to the government.

The most sensitive project is Ferrogrão, with 933 kilometers between Sinop (MT) and Itaituba (PA), aimed at shipping grains through the Northern Arc. The environmental license was suspended by IBAMA in 2023, and the judicial process is in the Federal Regional Court of the 1st Region (TRF-1), with an injunction from the federal judge that suspended the preliminary license. No settlement was brought to the Supreme Federal Court (STF) in 2026.

The financial modeling of the plan foresees that the Union will assume 20% to 30% of the contributions, using resources from the renegotiation of concessions with Rumo, MRS, and Vale. The logic is clear: without government subsidy, the high cost of implementing railways and the only deferred return make the projects unattractive to private investors.

Ferrogrão, in particular, has accumulated ten years of technical studies that consumed more than 575,000 man-hours of engineers, economists, and biologists. The impasse over the reimbursement amount to the company EDLP exposes the complexity of environmental licensing and contractual modeling, as reported by <a href='https://www1.folha.uol.com.br/mercado/2026/07/ministerio-dos-transportes-barra-aumento-milionario-de-estudos-da-ferrograo.shtml' target='_blank'>Folha</a>.

While Ferrogrão awaits a decision, the government moves forward with a pipeline of projects that prioritizes concessions to the private sector. The forecast is that auctions will occur in a staggered manner, with the first still this semester, as advanced by Minister Renan Filho to <a href='https://www.cnnbrasil.com.br/economia/macroeconomia/com-investimento-de-r-100-bi-governo-lancara-plano-nacional-de-ferrovias/' target='_blank'>CNN</a>.

Although there is speculation about the possibility of a bi-oceanic railway that would connect Bahia to the Peruvian port of Chancay on the Pacific, there is no official record at Itamaraty (the Ministry of Foreign Affairs), the Ministry of Transport, or reliable diplomatic sources. This supposed route, which would reduce travel time between continents from 40 to 28 days, still lacks concrete studies and formal agreements.

From a technical point of view, the national plan explores broad and mixed gauges to make existing sections compatible with new constructions. The standardization of gauges is crucial to avoid cargo breaks and enable the intermodality that the country so pursues.

The density of railway investment projects a silent revolution in Brazilian logistics. The speed of rails, after all, is not just a civilizational metaphor, but the gear that can shorten distances and increase the competitiveness of agribusiness and mining.

The plan's success, however, will depend on overcoming environmental bottlenecks, legal certainty, and execution capacity. If Brazil really manages to transform these 4,700 kilometers into steel and sleepers, it will have made the most significant leap in transportation infrastructure in recent decades.
