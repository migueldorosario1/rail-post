The federal state-owned company takes on the technical modeling of light rail systems, replicating the rigor it applied to freight railways, while São Paulo prepares a project for its historic center.

Every great city reveals its intelligence by how it moves people. Brazil is beginning to craft a more consistent response to this challenge by unlocking Light Rail Vehicle (VLT) projects structured directly by a federal state-owned company with recognized technical strength.

Infra S.A., a public company linked to the Ministry of Transport and resulting from the merger of the Logistics and Planning Company (EPL) with Valec, has taken on the modeling of light rail systems in medium-sized cities. This move marks a strategic shift: the Brazilian government is now treating urban passenger transport with the same technical rigor, legal certainty, and long-term vision it applied to structuring major freight logistics corridors.

The anchor project of this new phase is in Bauru, a São Paulo state municipality with 380,000 inhabitants. Infra S.A. was hired by the city government to prepare the Technical, Economic, and Environmental Feasibility Study (EVTEA) for a light rail system, as detailed by the Brazilian Railway Industry Association (ABIFER) when announcing the recent boost in the segment.

The route fully utilizes the existing railway network in the city, a factor that drastically reduces implementation costs and land impact. Instead of opening new greenfield corridors—a term that designates projects built from scratch and that usually require massive expropriations and high public investments—the Bauru light rail system transforms underused tracks into a vector of urban renewal, connecting neighborhoods to the center and relieving pressure on the road system.

Infra S.A.’s CEO, Jorge Bastos, described the operation as the consolidation of a new business front for the company. The logic is the same as the BNDES employed in sanitation and highways: the state technical arm organizes the project, mitigates risks, and delivers a mature asset for bidding, generally under a Public-Private Partnership (PPP) model.

This is not an isolated case. Infra S.A. is also working on the EVTEA for the Campina Grande light rail system, the second-largest city in Paraíba, with over 400,000 inhabitants, where studies are in the final phase of data collection and technical consolidation, with delivery expected in the first half of 2026.

The choice of Bauru and Campina Grande as pioneers is no accident. Both have existing railway networks, sufficient population to generate captive demand, and urban centers in need of renewal. ABIFER president Vicente Abate stressed that Infra S.A. possesses tremendous technical capacity to develop projects in this segment, precisely because it brings together Valec’s railway expertise and EPL’s logistics planning competence.

While medium-sized cities move forward with federal modeling, the São Paulo state capital is preparing its own system. The São Paulo Tram, a light rail system in the historic center, is in the planning phase and will be structured as a municipal PPP, leveraging the political and urban momentum generated by the state government’s new administrative center.

The national industry is watching the movement with appetite. Brazil already operates two electric-powered light rail systems—in Rio de Janeiro and Santos—and has the capacity to provide solutions that go beyond conventional propulsion, including hybrid systems and trains powered by green hydrogen, as Abate pointed out when describing the new frontier of sustainable transport.

The emergence of Infra S.A. as a structuring agent for light rail systems changes the geography of Brazilian urban transport. For decades, the country concentrated passenger rail investments on extremely costly per-kilometer subways or saturated metropolitan trains, leaving medium-sized cities hostage to diesel buses and road-based expansion.

Now, the equation is partially reversed: a light rail system on existing track costs a fraction of an underground metro, consumes clean energy, organizes urban growth along the rails, and can be replicated in dozens of Brazilian municipalities with railway infrastructure inherited from the last century. The connection to Brazil is explicit and central: the report deals entirely with the country, featuring concrete projects in three states and significant investments.

The technical rigor that Infra S.A. applies to the EVTEAs—with economic modeling, environmental analysis, and standardized operational design—is precisely what was lacking in previous cycles, when light rail projects appeared and disappeared at the whim of electoral agreements without engineering backing. What is underway in Bauru, Campina Grande, and São Paulo suggests an inflection point, where rail-based transport finally solidifies as a state policy for urban mobility, and not just a campaign promise.
