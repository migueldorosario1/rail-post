The GINT project tests ultra-fast internet on trains and anticipates the backbone of railway communication in Europe.

Every great city reveals its intelligence by the way it moves people. The maxim, which could adorn any urban planning manual, gains new meaning when displacement exceeds 200 km/h on rails.

Germany has just inaugurated an open-air laboratory that projects this intelligence into the 5G era. In the heart of Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania, the 10-kilometer stretch between Karow and Malchow has become the first test track for ultra-high-speed internet for passenger trains.

Named Gigabit Innovation Track (GINT), the project received 6.4 million euros from the German Federal Ministry for Digital and Transport. According to technical details disclosed by the <a href='https://www.umweltdialog.de/de/wirtschaft/Innovation-Forschung/2024/Teststrecke-fuer-High-Speed-Internet-im-Zug-eroeffnet.php' target='_blank'>Umweltdialog portal</a>, the goal is to achieve 5 gigabits per second per train by 2030.

What draws attention is the speed of installation: 13 15-meter masts were erected in just one month. The foundation is steel driven into the ground, completely dispensing with concrete and reducing CO₂ emissions.

This structural innovation is not a mere aesthetic detail, but a logistical revolution for the future FRMCS (Future Rail Mobile Communication System). FRMCS will replace the old GSM-R and will require a network of antennas capable of supporting massive data on the move.

In the 3.5 GHz band, the GINT's 5G operates with innovative radio combiners and a pre-configured radio access network (RAN). The 'Advanced TrainLab' laboratory train, a former ICE transformed into a testing platform, runs along the track to validate coverage and performance.

The alliance brings together Deutsche Bahn, Ericsson, O2 Telefónica, and Vantage Towers, which bet on shared-use masts. The idea is for railway and mobile telephony infrastructure to coexist on the same tower, accelerating the digitization of corridors and saving resources.

Minister Volker Wissing emphasized that the project went from paper to reality in eight months — 'the pace we need for network expansion.' DB executive Daniela Gerd tom Markotten reinforced that the goal is to make passengers feel at home, with stable calls and uninterrupted streaming.

For Brazil, which is slowly advancing projects like the Intercity Train and the modernization of the São Paulo metro, GINT signals a path for onboard connectivity. Incorporating steel masts without concrete and 5G cells in the 3.5 GHz band could reduce costs and accelerate the deployment of networks in tunnels and expressways.

The director of O2 Telefónica, Valentina Daiber, classified the test as a 'paradigm' that brings mobile infrastructure closer to the tracks. Meanwhile, Daniel Leimbach from Ericsson revealed that the network core was pre-assembled at the Aachen laboratory, drastically cutting delivery times.

The responsible engineers highlight that the absence of concrete eliminates curing time and allows for nighttime installation without interrupting rail traffic. This is vital for dense corridors like Berlin–Hamburg, where every minute of stoppage costs thousands of euros.

The European plan for high-speed trains, detailed in a <a href='https://www.s-ge.com/export/fr/articles/analysis/europe-railway-upgrade' target='_blank'>recent analysis</a>, projects 345 billion euros in investments by 2040. In this context, GINT ceases to be an isolated experiment and becomes a key piece for the continent's digital interoperability.

Sustainability also benefits from the elimination of cement, which is responsible for about 8% of global CO₂ emissions. The bolted steel masts can be disassembled and reused, aligning gigabit connectivity with the circular economy.

Christian Hillabrant, CEO of Vantage Towers, stated that the model 'proves that gigabit on railways can become a reality in months.' The company, which operates more than 80,000 towers in Europe, sees infrastructure sharing as a fast track to eliminating digital shadow zones.

To achieve 5 Gbps, GINT combines carrier aggregation and massive MIMO, techniques that multiply channel capacity. The train receives multiple data beams simultaneously, as if each window had a dedicated antenna.

The transition from GSM-R to FRMCS is crucial because the current system does not support the data demand of modern passengers. Operators need a unified architecture that caters to both critical signaling and onboard entertainment.

GINT tests directional antennas that focus the signal on the train, minimizing interference. This allows multiple operators to use the same spectrum band without degrading the user experience.

In Brazil, the National Telecommunications Agency (Anatel) is already discussing the auction of frequencies for 5G in mobility areas. The German experience could guide pole-sharing clauses along railways such as São Paulo's CPTM network.

Deutsche Bahn plans to replicate the model across the entire ICE network after final validation. The Advanced TrainLab results will be compiled by the end of the year, feeding into the FRMCS standards.

Germany's speed in deploying 5G on rails shows that the railway can be the digital backbone of the 21st century. Just as 'every great city reveals its intelligence by the way it moves people,' the European bloc reveals its own by transforming the train car into a high-speed office.
