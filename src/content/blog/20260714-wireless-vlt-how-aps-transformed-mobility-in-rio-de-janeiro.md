The APS system dispenses with overhead cables, combines operational safety with visual preservation, and pushes Rio's mobility into the 21st century.

Tracks are the material geometry of the future, and Rio de Janeiro’s Light Rail Vehicle (VLT) proves that this geometry can be elegant, silent, and perfectly integrated into the urban fabric. Inaugurated in 2016 and still expanding, the Carioca VLT crosses the Center and the Port Region without a single overhead cable, using an engineering solution that redefines the coexistence between mass transit and historical heritage.

Ground-level power supply (APS, from the French Alimentation par le Sol) is the soul of this visual silence: energy reaches the cars through shoes that touch segmented rails, fed by underground power boxes that energize only the exact segment under the vehicle. According to official data from the <a href="http://www.rio.rj.gov.br/web/guest/exibeconteudo?id=5267887" target="_blank">Municipality of Rio</a>, the system eliminates accidental shocks and discharges, allowing pedestrians and vehicles to cross the tracks without risk of electrocution.

Each Carioca VLT car, 2.65 meters wide and 2.17 meters high, carries up to 420 passengers at maximum capacity—a number comparable to heavy rail systems. The average speed of 17 km/h may seem modest, but it is perfectly adjusted to the pace of a dense urban center, where regularity and predictability are worth more than excessive haste.

In case of main power failure, onboard energy banks ensure the vehicle travels up to 400 meters, enough to reach the next stop or exit a critical intersection without blocking traffic. According to <a href="https://www.seesp.org.br/site/comunicacao/noticias/item/15341-vlt-modal-utilit-rio-tecnol-gico-e-n-o-agressivo-ao-ambiente" target="_blank">SEESP records</a>, engineers from the concessionaire CCR confirmed that these banks were sized exclusively for emergencies, while constant recharging ensures uninterrupted use of air conditioning, an indispensable item in Rio’s climate.

The total project foresees 28 kilometers of permanent way, 46 stations and stops, and a fleet of 32 cars, interconnecting the Novo Rio bus terminal, the Central do Brasil train station, ferries, Santos Dumont Airport, as well as BRTs, the Providência Hill cable car, and conventional bus lines. The first phase, covering 14 kilometers, already moves about 8,000 passengers per hour, with a fare of R$3.80 integrated into the Bilhete Único and voluntary validation—a gesture of operational trust in the citizen rarely seen in Brazilian public transport.

Made possible by a Public-Private Partnership (PPP) signed with the Municipality of Rio, the total investment reached R$1.5 billion, covering not only tracks and rolling stock but also the urban renewal of the Port Region, including the preservation of historic paving and the construction of the Integrated Operations and Maintenance Center in Gamboa. The result is a clean, silent mode that acts as a vector of requalification, returning previously degraded areas to the city and connecting the financial center to the cultural corridor.

While cities like Austin in the United States still debate the implementation of their first light rail systems with environmental hurdles and billion-dollar budgets—as per <a href="https://www.kut.org/transportation/2026-01-16/austins-light-rail-plans-get-environmental-clearance-from-trump-administration" target="_blank">recent planning</a>—Rio de Janeiro demonstrates in practice how the VLT can be a tool for urban transformation without harming the landscape. The Carioca experience, which dispensed with catenaries and adopted APS, is a lesson in engineering and political courage for other Brazilian capitals that still struggle with rail mobility projects.

The choice of APS was not a mere aesthetic whim: it was a technical decision that allowed the coexistence of the modern with centuries-old townhouses, of conductive shoes with the cobblestones of Old Rio. Tracks are, indeed, the material geometry of the future—and the Carioca VLT, with its discreet ground-level power supply, writes this geometry without harming the past, sewing together the financial center, the cultural corridor, and the port zone into a single network of efficiency and civility.
