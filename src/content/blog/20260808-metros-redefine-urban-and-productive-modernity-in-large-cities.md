Rails are the backbone of smart and productive cities.

<p>Rails are the material geometry of the future. They organize the subsoil, reorder the surface and dictate the rhythm of metropolitan productivity with a precision that asphalt has never been able to imitate.</p>
<p>The metro is not just a means of transport — it is the infrastructure that stitches knowledge, work, and innovation into a single pulsating system. When a city decides to dig tunnels and launch trains onto rails, it is making a civilizational decision about its own urban metabolism.</p>
<p>The Portuguese capital has just taken a step that illustrates exactly this logic: transforming the metro network into a living laboratory of cutting-edge technology. Iscte – University Institute of Lisbon leads a project that converts the Lisbon Metro into an underground platform for experimentation with next-generation optical fiber.</p>
<p>As detailed by the <a href="https://www.ccdr-lvt.pt/2025/11/iscte-transforma-o-metro-de-lisboa-num-laboratorio-vivo-de-inovacao-urbana/" target="_blank">portal of the Regional Coordination and Development Commission of Lisbon and the Tagus Valley</a>, the initiative has an investment of 1.4 million euros and around 588 thousand euros from the ERDF fund. The goal is to test fibers capable of multiplying the current data transmission capacity up to seven times, using the tunnels of the yellow line as a real validation environment.</p>
<p>The vice-rector of Iscte, Jorge Rodrigues da Costa, explained the project's origin with an almost poetic observation: the university campus is situated exactly above a metro line. This geographical coincidence became the trigger to create an underground ring of innovation that positions Lisbon as a global technological reference in optical communications.</p>
<p>Rector Maria de Lurdes Rodrigues emphasized that it is a unique laboratory in Europe, with a broad spectrum of economic impact. The project brings together academia, the public sector, and industry in a collaborative ecosystem that projects the Portuguese capital far beyond its physical borders.</p>
<p>What stands out in this case is not just the innovation in optical fiber, but the recognition that metro tunnels are the backbone upon which the smart city can be built. Urban rail infrastructure functions as a channel for much more than passengers — it carries data, monitoring, security, and efficient resource management.</p>
<p>This understanding is redefining mobility policies in the world's major capitals. In 2026, cities like Los Angeles, New York, Paris, Seoul, and Dubai are accelerating metro projects that combine physical expansion with digital integration.</p>
<p>Los Angeles is preparing for global events with the introduction of contactless payments, a new mobility app, and the rail link to the international airport. The Sepulveda Transit Corridor project moves forward with the option for a heavy underground tunnel that will connect the San Fernando Valley to the Westside, drilling through one of the most congested corridors in the country.</p>
<p>New York has resumed work on Phase 2 of the Second Avenue, adding three fully accessible underground stations in East Harlem. The expansion is expected to reduce travel time between Harlem, Midtown, and Brooklyn by up to 20 minutes, serving hundreds of thousands of passengers daily.</p>
<p>Paris advances with the Grand Paris Express, a ring of new automatic lines that avoids forced passage through traditional transfer centers. Line 16 is due to open the first segment still in 2026, while Line 15 will have its southern section inaugurated in 2027, creating faster orbital journeys between suburbs and business hubs.</p>
<p>Seoul prepares the opening of the Wirye Line for September 2026, a light metro with a dozen stations that will integrate new residential neighborhoods into the existing heavy rail network. Dubai has announced the Gold Line, with over 40 kilometers in length and up to 18 stations, designed to serve areas of rapid real estate growth.</p>
<p>All these projects share a common principle: the rail is the technology that organizes urban modernity. It is not about railway nostalgia, but about contemporary engineering applied to productivity, sustainability, and quality of life.</p>
<p>Brazil, unfortunately, still oscillates between flashes of strategic vision and chronic slowness that postpones essential works for decades. But there are tangible signs that rails are beginning to be treated as development infrastructure, and not as current expenditure.</p>
<p>In São Paulo, the Metrô Linha 2 Consortium prepares the arrival of the largest tunnel boring machine ever used in the country in diameter. Manufactured by Germany's Herrenknecht AG, the Earth Pressure Balanced machine will have 11.6 meters in diameter and will be used in the expansion of Line 2-Green towards Guarulhos.</p>
<p>Engineer Edson Peev, from Herrenknecht in Brazil, explained that the equipment features an independent bearing articulation system, allowing more precise control of ground pressure. This innovation extends the life of the cutters and improves excavation safety in complex terrains.</p>
<p>Another relevant technical novelty is the use of reinforced concrete rings in the 7+0 format, with seven segments of identical dimensions. This modern design simplifies ring assembly and ensures better distribution of excavation forces, accelerating the TBM advance cycle.</p>
<p>The regional director of Grupo Galvão, Flavio Henrique Cunha Lobato, guaranteed that the machine offers sufficient performance to meet contractual deadlines. The expansion of Line 2-Green will add 14.3 kilometers of tracks and thirteen new stations up to the region of Guarulhos, integrating with CPTM lines.</p>
<p>When completed, the line will become the longest stretch in the São Paulo metro network, with 23 total kilometers and capacity to transport over 500 thousand people per day. Metro project manager Eduardo Maggi highlighted that the expansion will bring measurable social benefits, such as reduced emissions and traffic accidents.</p>
<p>In Rio de Janeiro, the state government announced an even more ambitious plan: 31 new stations, 44 kilometers of additional tracks, and a tunnel under Guanabara Bay. The estimated investment is 28.8 billion reais, made possible through a Public-Private Partnership.</p>
<p>State Secretary of Transport Washington Reis classified the project as a turning point in Rio's mobility. Line 3 will connect Praça 15, in downtown Rio, to Guaxindiba, in São Gonçalo, with a stop in Niterói and a three-kilometer underwater tunnel.</p>
<p>The stretch between Estácio and Praça 15 will eliminate the operational crossings that currently strangle the capacity of Lines 1 and 2. With the separation of flows, intervals between trains will be reduced, increasing system frequency and reliability.</p>
<p>The extension of Line 4 to Recreio dos Bandeirantes, in turn, will transform the Alvorada Terminal into a strategic multimodal integration point. The project provides that the first bids occur in early 2026 and that the first sections be inaugurated in 2031.</p>
<p>Minister of Cities Jader Filho stated that the union of efforts between the Union, state, and municipalities is what shortens distances for citizens who depend on public transport. Mayor Eduardo Paes reinforced that metropolitan integration is the way to leverage resources and investments.</p>
<p>The Gávea station, whose works were halted for almost a decade, now has a delivery forecast for the end of 2026, according to Secretary Washington Reis. The resumption of this project, which has already consumed over 10 billion reais, is symbolic of the need to finish what is started.</p>
<p>What unites Lisbon, Los Angeles, São Paulo, and Rio de Janeiro is the understanding that the metro is not an expense, but an investment with economic, social, and technological return. Tunnels that transport people can also transport data, innovation, and competitiveness.</p>
<p>The Portuguese experience demonstrates that metro infrastructure is a multi-layered asset: on the same rails circulate trains, optical fibers, monitoring sensors, and the economic life of the city. This integrated vision is what Brazil urgently needs to absorb.</p>
<p>Treating the metro as development infrastructure means planning stations as poles of urban densification, connecting airport terminals, eliminating operational crossings, and opening tunnels for technological experimentation. It also means accelerating bids, unlocking financing, and demanding contractual performance.</p>
<p>The material geometry of the future is not an abstraction — it is steel, concrete, optical fiber, and steel wheels on rails. Cities that understand this equation will be better prepared for the productive, environmental, and social challenges of the coming decades.</p>
<p>Lisbon understood and created an underground laboratory that attracts talent and international investment. Brazil has the geographical, demographic, and technical conditions to do the same — it only requires that strategic vision prevails over the bureaucratic paralysis that consumes projects and hopes.</p>
