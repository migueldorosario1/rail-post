Rail corridors in Australia, Turkey, India and the United States reshape the freight transport map and expose Brazil's structural backwardness, still hostage to road-centric policies.

Every great city reveals its intelligence by the way it moves people, and the same logic applies to the logistical intelligence of an entire nation when it comes to cargo. The efficient flow of containers, grain and ores defines who leads and who skids in 21st-century global trade.

While countries like Australia, Turkey and India are executing railway megaprojects with ambitious goals and concrete deadlines, Brazil still carries the legacy of an excessively road-dependent matrix that bleeds competitiveness with every kilometer of congested highway. The difference between these two worlds lies not only in the numbers, but in the vision of the State that each adopts to plan its territory.

In India, the Eastern Dedicated Freight Corridor (EDFC) built 1,200 kilometers of tracks exclusively for freight transport between 2011 and 2026, quadrupling daily freight train capacity from 32 to more than 130 pairs. This leap, financed with support from the World Bank, reduced emissions by 55,000 tons of CO₂ equivalent in 2024 alone and cut logistics costs by approximately 58 million dollars, according to the <a href="https://www.worldbank.org/en/results/2025/08/28/transforming-logistics-the-power-of-rail-investment" target="_blank">World Bank report</a> on the impact of railway investments.

Each one-kilometer-long train running on the EDFC dedicated tracks replaces around 72 trucks on Indian highways, easing road congestion and reducing accidents. Projections indicate that by 2042, the corridor will have avoided 13.19 million tons of cumulative emissions, a 57% reduction compared to pre-project levels.

In Australia, the Inland Rail follows the same playbook of technical boldness and geo-economic vision. The 1,600-kilometer corridor will link Melbourne to Brisbane, with capacity for double-stacked trains that stack containers and multiply the efficiency of each consist.

The estimated cost of the Australian project is around 31 billion Australian dollars, and analysts compare it to a Suez Canal on rails crossing the continent's interior. Each freight train that will run on the Inland Rail could replace more than a hundred trucks, cutting emissions and freeing up highways for uses with less economic impact.

Turkey drives its logistical stake into the railway route that crosses the strait using the Yavuz Sultan Selim Bridge. Half of the route is built in tunnels, protecting operations against extreme heat, floods and strong winds, ensuring continuous 24-hour operation.

With a 2 billion dollar investment financed by the World Bank, the Turkish project multiplies freight transport capacity by more than sixteen times, jumping from 3 million to 50 million tons annually. The work strengthens Istanbul as a logistics hub of the modern Silk Road, connecting Europe, Central Asia and the Middle East with high-capacity electric trains.

In the United States, the Pier B Rail Program at the Port of Long Beach received 283.4 million dollars from the federal government to expand rail yards and build new receiving and departure tracks. The project's goal, which is part of the Mega and INFRA programs of the U.S. Department of Transportation, is to have 20% of the port's throughput moved exclusively by rail.

At the same time, railway integration projects in South America are emerging as promising initiatives. Although still in the planning phase, these projects aim to transform the region into a strategic logistics hub, connecting emerging markets and consolidating low-emission routes.

Brazil, despite being one of the world's largest commodity exporters, still faces institutional fragmentation and excessive road dependence that drive up freight costs and stifle competitiveness. Initiatives such as the Petrocity group's megaproject, which plans to connect the Center-West region to the port of São Mateus in Espírito Santo, crossing 41 municipalities in Minas Gerais, represent attempts to break through this structural bottleneck.

Petrocity's president, José Roberto Barbosa da Silva, stated that all costs of the undertaking are being borne by private investors, with no reliance on public funds or BNDES. The project will serve mineral, steel and grain production destined for export, with a ten-year deadline for completion.

The lesson that emerges from Australia, Turkey, India and the United States is crystal clear: governments that prioritize efficient logistics corridors integrate territories and eliminate the knots that limit economic growth. Double-stack freight trains, combined with digital control and progressive electrification, generate economies of scale impossible with road transport.

The dedicated freight corridor concept applied in India and the receiving and departure rail yards expanding in Long Beach show that efficiency does not come only from the network's length but from infrastructure specialization. Positive Train Control signaling systems and dispatch algorithms transform railways into the backbone of the low-carbon economy.

Without a dense and modern railway network, Brazil will continue to drain competitiveness with every ton transported on wheels. The choice for rails is not merely technical, but a strategic development decision that will define the country's position on the economic map for the coming decades.
