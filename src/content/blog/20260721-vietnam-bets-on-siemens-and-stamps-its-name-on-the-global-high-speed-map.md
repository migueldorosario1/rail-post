While Brazil struggles between the Intercity Train and FIOL, an emerging Southeast Asian country leaps directly to the gold standard of 320 km/h with the Velaro EVO and automatic signaling.

<p>When technology organizes space, society gains time to live. In April 2026, Vietnam turned this maxim into steel ballast and high-voltage cables by launching the construction of its first high-speed railway, connecting Hanoi to Halong, in Quảng Ninh province.</p>
<p>This is not an incremental project or a modernization of a single track. The country led by Prime Minister Lê Minh Hưng decided to skip stages and directly join the club of trains that challenge regional aviation on medium-distance corridors.</p>
<p>The Hanoi–Quảng Ninh line will be 120.2 kilometers long, built entirely in standard gauge of 1,435 mm with double electrified track and a maximum design speed of 320 km/h, a technological leap that will reduce travel time between the two cities from over two hours to just 23 minutes. The project, budgeted at 147 trillion Vietnamese dong — approximately 5.2 billion euros —, is the responsibility of VinSpeed, a subsidiary of the Vingroup conglomerate, with expropriation costs of 10.27 trillion dong covered by the state budget.</p>
<p>The technological backbone of the project bears the signature of Siemens Mobility, which signed an agreement in December 2025 to supply the Velaro EVO trains and signaling systems. The Velaro EVO is currently one of the most efficient very high-speed platforms on the planet: it offers 10% more capacity than competing models and consumes 30% less energy per passenger transported.</p>
<p>During the launch ceremony, Siemens Mobility CEO Michael Peter defined the project as “the best high-speed train technology”, promising know-how transfer, local assembly, and maintenance to create a cutting-edge railway ecosystem in Vietnam. The signaling system adopted will be ETCS Level 2 with ATO (Automatic Train Operation), the same standard that controls European high-speed routes, enabling semi-autonomous operation with second-precision train spacing.</p>
<p>This Vietnamese leap highlights the multiple geopolitical approaches currently competing in the global high-speed infrastructure market. On the Mumbai-Ahmedabad corridor, India advances with Japanese technical and financial assistance, using the renowned Shinkansen system and mountain tunnels already nearing completion, such as the recently bored one in Dahanu taluka, Palghar district, as reported by the Indian newspaper <a href="https://timesofindia.indiatimes.com/business/infrastructure/mumbai-ahmedabad-bullet-train-project-achieves-major-milestone-as-mountain-tunnel-breakthrough-completed/articleshow/131458512.cms" target="_blank">The Times of India</a>.</p>
<p>India's Railways Minister, Ashwini Vaishnaw, celebrated the achievement as a milestone in the construction of the country's first high-speed corridor, financed 81% by Japan at 0.1% annual interest with a 50-year repayment term. Meanwhile, China, which operates the world's largest high-speed network, continues expanding, but there are no confirmed projects for an underwater tunnel in the Bohai Strait.</p>
<p>To complete the very high-speed chessboard, Japan has just given the definitive green light for the Chūō Shinkansen, the maglev line that will connect Tokyo to Osaka in about one hour, floating 10 centimeters above the tracks by magnetic levitation at up to 500 km/h, as reported by the German outlet <a href="https://www.focus.de/finanzen/500-km-h-zug-fuer-60-milliarden-euro-japan-gibt-gruenes-licht-fuer-neue-magnetschwebebahn-strecke_4037b8b2-83ed-4a03-84c4-aee639bcf3f3.html" target="_blank">FOCUS online</a>. The estimated budget is 60 billion euros, with 25-kilometer tunnels under the Japanese Alps and full operation expected only in 2045.</p>
<p>Each of these projects reveals a distinct civilizational choice. India bets on the transfer of Japanese wheel-on-rail and overhead electrification technology, with the goal of nationalizing the production of 320 km/h trains by 2027. China pursues the narrative of the geographically impossible, drilling the seabed with tripartite tunnels to prove that not even the ocean can interrupt the integration of its territory.</p>
<p>Japan, for its part, invests in maglev as an object of national prestige and a direct response to the Chinese, who are already testing 600 km/h models developed with the cooperation of German engineers. Vietnam, without a high-performance railway tradition, went straight to the point: it contracted the most mature and austere platform in energy consumption from the Western market, the Velaro EVO, to inaugurate its high-speed era with European control and operation standards.</p>
<p>The most thought-provoking fact for Brazil lies precisely in this Vietnamese financing and execution architecture. The Hanoi–Halong line does not operate on mixed gauge, is not a regional train disguised as an express, and does not depend exclusively on non-repayable public funds: the Vingroup group provided private capital and Siemens the cutting-edge technology, while the state covered the expropriations.</p>
<p>While the national debate on the Intercity Train in São Paulo struggles between track restrictions and the eternal ghost of the inherited narrow gauge, and FIOL advances at a slow pace as a freight railway in western Bahia, Vietnam shows that an emerging country can stamp 320 km/h on the map without having been a railway power before. Standard gauge of 1,435 mm, double electrified track, and ETCS Level 2 with ATO are not luxuries of rich countries: they are technical prerequisites for reducing distances and giving time back to society.</p>
<p>The stopped clock of the empty station is not the inevitable fate of a developing nation. It is the result of choices that still treat high speed as a luxury item, while Southeast Asia transforms it into infrastructure for economic convergence and territorial cohesion.</p>
