The pharaonic project that was once a banner of the PAC, a World Cup promise, and a campaign piece has been accumulating postponements since 2007, without an environmental license, without national technological control, and with the federal government now out of the financing.

Every great city reveals its intelligence by how it moves people. In the axis that concentrates the highest economic and population density in Brazil, intelligence has still not managed to lay a single high-speed rail.

The High Speed Train (TAV) between São Paulo and Rio de Janeiro has just accumulated another postponement: the works, which had been announced for 2027, have been transferred to 2028. It has been eighteen years of official narrative, four PT presidential terms, successive failed auctions, and not a meter of permanent track built.

The current project foresees 417 kilometers in length, a maximum speed of 320 km/h, and a travel time of less than two hours between the two capitals. The cost estimate hovers around R$ 60 billion, with four planned stations: São Paulo, Rio de Janeiro, São José dos Campos, and Volta Redonda. The concession was designed for 99 years, entirely private, with no direct federal government participation in the financing.

As pointed out by the <a href="https://www.gazetadopovo.com.br/brasil/promessa-do-pt-obras-adiadas-trem-bala-sao-paulo-rio-de-janeiro/" target="_blank">Gazeta do Povo</a> in a recent survey, the TAV project faces numerous difficulties. The National Land Transport Agency (ANTT) has never granted or initiated an authorization process for the SP-Rio high-speed train. The agency only regulates conventional freight and passenger railways, not TAVs, which require a specific law and shared competency with the Ministry of Transport and Ibama.

Environmental licensing remains a primary knot. The Brazilian Institute of Environment and Renewable Natural Resources (Ibama) demanded, in March 2024, the preparation of the Environmental Impact Study and Report (EIA/Rima). The document has not yet been submitted. Without it, any works schedule remains in the realm of institutional fiction.

From an engineering standpoint, the undertaking faces geological and urban barriers that cannot be circumvented with rhetoric. Crossing the Serra do Mar requires long tunnels, high viaducts, and geotechnical stabilization in highly fractured terrain. Urban interferences in the metropolitan regions of São Paulo and Rio de Janeiro add complexity comparable to that of major international metro projects.

The director-secretary of the Brazilian Association for Railway Preservation (ABPF), Thomaz Schoeffel, warned that Brazil has lost the capacity for works of this magnitude and would need to import teams and know-how, raising implantation and maintenance costs. Economist Cláudio Frischtak, president of Inter.B International Business Consulting, reinforces that Brazilian railway engineering operates on another level, focused on freight trains that rarely exceed 100 km/h.

The dependence on foreign technology is structural and unavoidable. Frischtak recalls that only China, France, Japan, and Spain dominate large-scale high-speed technology. Brazil does not have an industrial base, supplier chain, or trained human resources to design, manufacture, and maintain trains that operate above 300 km/h.

The economic equation is equally relentless. TAV Brasil projects tickets between R$ 300 and R$ 500, but Frischtak maintains that viability is very difficult to justify without significant public subsidies. He recalls that Spain expanded its network with massive transfers from the European Union, a scenario that does not exist in Brazil, which faces a permanent fiscal crisis and severe budgetary restrictions.

A more rational alternative, discarded by former president Dilma Rousseff during her term, envisioned a medium-speed train at about 150 km/h during the day and logistical use at night. The model would allow two concessionaires and leverage existing infrastructure, drastically reducing implantation costs. The PT government opted for the radical technological leap and has collected failures ever since.

The history is relentless with official promises. In 2007, the government of President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva announced the TAV under the Growth Acceleration Program, with operation planned for the 2014 World Cup. Auctions were launched and canceled due to lack of interested parties, a state-owned company was created and renamed, and the project migrated from a strategic priority to an urban legend of national planning.

The Railways Law (Law No. 14.273/2021), enacted under the government of Jair Bolsonaro, altered the regulatory framework by allowing private authorizations without traditional bidding. The rule unlocked dozens of freight projects but did not solve the underlying problems: the absence of national engineering, the geological resistance of the Serra do Mar, and the lack of fiscal backing to guarantee the operation.

While the bullet train stalls, the country advances in more concrete rail projects, such as the National Railway Plan with an estimated investment of R$ 100 billion. The demand for railway infrastructure is real and urgent, but concentrated on freight transport, not high-speed passenger service.

The Rio-São Paulo axis deserves a modern, fast, and reliable rail connection. Above all, it deserves an honest technical debate about what is possible to build with the engineering, budget, and regulatory framework the country actually has. Persistence with the most expensive model, without an industrial base, without EIA/Rima, without a defined subsidy, and with a works date that keeps sliding into the future, is not ambition — it is postponement treated as planning.
