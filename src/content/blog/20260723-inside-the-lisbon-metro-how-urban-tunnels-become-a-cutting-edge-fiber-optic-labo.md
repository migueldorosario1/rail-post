Green Line tunnels serve as an underground ring to test technology that could redefine global optical communications and position the Portuguese capital at the forefront of urban innovation.

Modernity is not abstract. It has ballast, gauge, energy, and direction.
Few infrastructures embody this idea with as much precision as a metro network. And Lisbon has just revealed a new layer of utility for its tunnels.
The Lisbon Metro has been converted into the world’s most ambitious living laboratory for optical communications. The initiative is led by ISCTE – University Institute of Lisbon, which saw in the rails and underground galleries a testing platform impossible to replicate in a bench environment.
With a total investment of 1.4 million euros, of which 588,000 come from the ERDF fund, the project installs next-generation optical fibers along the Green Line. The main objective is to validate, under absolutely real conditions, cables capable of multiplying the data transmission capacity of the current network by up to seven times.
The generation of optical fiber that today carries almost all of the world's data traffic is approaching the physical limit of its bandwidth. Saturation of light beams within the glass cores is no longer a distant possibility, but a concrete and economically threatening horizon.
This invisible bottleneck was the warning that mobilized ISCTE to take up the challenge from a German optical equipment manufacturer. The industrial partner's proposal was as bold as it was logical: to test a new multi-channel fiber design in a harsh and vibrant environment, such as the interior of an active metro tunnel.
The choice of the Green Line was not random. ISCTE’s main campus is located in Carvalheira, close to the line, enabling an underground experimentation ring that connects the university to the operational bowels of the city. It is a real route, with moving trains, mechanical vibrations, thermal variations, and humidity – variables that no laboratory can faithfully simulate.
Installing next-generation optical fiber in such an environment means exposing the prototypes to the most severe stress test. What is learned inside will determine whether the next wave of global connectivity can indeed avoid a capacity collapse.
The concept is known as spatial multiplexing: instead of a single light beam traveling through the core, multiple beams occupy differentiated paths within the same glass filament. It is the difference between a single-lane road and a highway with several parallel corridors, both built within the same physical width.
The vice-rector of ISCTE, Jorge Rodrigues da Costa, explains that the German manufacturer was precisely seeking this type of field validation. The Lisbon metro offered the ideal combination of academic proximity and operational complexity, something that turned the project into a test bed with global scaling potential.
For the rector of ISCTE, Maria de Lurdes Rodrigues, the investment positions the Portuguese capital as a geographical and technological reference point in optical communications. She emphasizes that it is a unique laboratory not only in Portugal but in the entire European space.
The cables installed along the track serve as permanent monitoring sensors. They capture vibrations, detect intrusions, and measure the structural integrity of the tunnels with a precision that traditional electromechanical systems could never achieve.
The collected data travels through the same wires that carry the passengers' internet, in a virtuous cycle where connectivity serves both the citizen and cutting-edge engineering. It is the first time that a metro underground ring operates simultaneously as a mass transit channel and as a fundamental research platform.
The <a href="https://lisboa.portugal2030.pt/2025/11/04/iscte-transforma-o-metro-de-lisboa-num-laboratorio-vivo-de-inovacao-urbana/" target="_blank">Lisbon 2030</a> program, which finances the initiative through ERDF, frames the project within European goals for digital sovereignty and critical infrastructure. The medium-term idea is that the results obtained on the Green Line can be replicated in the transport networks of other anchor cities on the continent.
The economic implications of this prominence are hard to exaggerate. A city that serves as a test bench for the new generation of optical fibers attracts highly specialized talent, industrial investment, and scientific visibility in a sector worth tens of billions of euros per year.
The project also highlights a strategic shift in how academia and the public sector can collaborate. Instead of isolated islands of knowledge, universities like ISCTE are now using the urban fabric itself as an extension of their laboratories, dramatically shortening the cycle between research and practical application.
For Brazil, the lesson is direct and far from abstract. The metro networks of São Paulo and Rio de Janeiro total dozens of kilometers of tunnels that could host similar technological validation projects, combining the interests of the national telecommunications industry with the unique capillarity of rail transport.
A partnership with international manufacturers could transform sections of lines 1, 2, or 4 of the São Paulo Metro into living connectivity laboratories. It would be a way to add non-fare revenue to the system and, at the same time, accelerate the arrival of the next generation of ultra-fast internet into the Brazilian urban environment.
The Lisbon case shows that mobility infrastructure can be much more than a means of transport. It can function as a matrix of innovation, a support of digital sovereignty, and an international showcase for cities that dare to think of their tunnels as strategic development assets.
As the trains of the Green Line run along the Cidade Universitária–Rato axis, they carry ordinary passengers and, at the same time, fuel one of the most sophisticated experiments in European photonics. It is from this silent overlapping of functions that the densest and most interesting future emerges.
Modernity is not abstract, indeed. It is already installed beneath the feet of Lisbon, a few meters deep, pulsing light through fibers that may one day connect the world at speeds we can barely imagine today.
