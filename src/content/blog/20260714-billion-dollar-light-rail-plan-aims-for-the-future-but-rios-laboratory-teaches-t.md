As BNDES plans to quadruple the national network with R$500 billion by 2054, the decade-long experience of Rio's light rail exposes the chasm between financial modeling and the city's actual behavior.

Modernity is not abstract. It has ballast, gauge, energy, and direction.

As the federal government prepares a plan to quadruple the Light Rail Vehicle (VLT) network in Brazil, Rio's laboratory completes ten years teaching that cutting-edge technology needs a living city to avoid running empty. The study by the National Bank for Economic and Social Development (BNDES) mapped 194 mobility projects and pointed to the need to jump from the current 631 kilometers of medium-capacity corridors to 2,500 kilometers by 2054.

The figure is astronomical: R$500 billion in total investments, also encompassing subways and BRTs. The great advantage of the VLT in this financial equation is the deployment cost, estimated at R$50 million per kilometer, <a href="https://oglobo.globo.com/brasil/noticia/2025/08/04/estudo-do-bndes-aponta-que-brasil-precisa-dobrar-metro-e-quadruplicar-os-brts-e-vlts.ghtml" target="_blank">as detailed by the BNDES survey itself</a>, a bargain compared to the subway, which easily devours R$1 billion per excavated kilometer.

This brutal difference in scale makes the VLT the rational choice to stitch together central regions and saturated corridors with energy efficiency and zero local pollutant emissions. However, looking at the Rio de Janeiro showcase, reality imposes a sobriety that office projections rarely capture. The Rio system, operated through a Public-Private Partnership (PPP) under the supervision of the Carioca Partnership and Investment Company (CCPar), inaugurated its first line in 2016 and now boasts 28 kilometers in length and 32 trains in operation.

The original feasibility studies promised 243,777 passengers per working day when the last line was activated — which only happened in April 2024. In the real world, post-economic crisis and pandemic, the mode suffers a demand of 95 thousand daily users, less than 40% of the contractual reference target.

The damage to Brazilian public transport was systemic, with a hemorrhage of 43% of passengers between 2014 and 2023, migrating en masse to app-based motorcycles and private cars. But the VLT, precisely because it is a public investment with high fixed costs, suffers intensely from the underutilization of assets. Director Silvia Bressan, from the concessionaire VLT Carioca, credits part of the recent recovery to the Gentileza Intermodal Terminal, inaugurated in 2024, which injects 78 thousand people per day into the complex, although only 11.4 thousand of them actually board the silver trains.

The big leap to cover the demand gap came with the entry into operation of the Margaridas Single Ticket (BUM), in May 2026. The city allowed fare integration connecting passengers from the Baixada Fluminense who disembark at the Margaridas Terminal directly to the VLT network, breaking the economic barrier that isolated the mode from a mass of workers.

It is empirical proof that it's not enough to lay tracks and import technology: the system's survival depends on an aggressive fare policy and capillarity with regional mass transport.

Another layer of complexity that the national plan needs to absorb from Rio's experience is the unpredictable urban behavior. Without fixed turnstiles, the system faces the eternal war against fare evasion, with human inspectors trying to deter fare-dodging in an operation where the passenger profile has radically changed over a decade. The multiplication of app-based delivery workers crossing the permanent way, electric scooter tourists, and pedestrians immersed in headphones forced drivers to slow down at critical points, eroding one of the Light Rail Vehicle's main assets: commercial speed.

The original project also suffered painful mutations, such as the removal of the 2.6-kilometer stretch to the Novo Rio Bus Station, causing revenue loss and breaking the logic of metropolitan integration. The way out now seems to lie less in immediate physical expansion and more in the urban densification of the Center, driven by projects like Reviver Centro and the Maravalley tech hub.

The lesson that the VLT Carioca offers to the R$500 billion national plan is crystal clear: the rail is the soul of the system, but fare integration is the blood and the dense urban surroundings are the body that sustains it. Quadrupling the network is useless if Brazil insists on treating VLT as a landscaping item for revitalized grand avenues, instead of inserting it as the backbone of metropolitan capillarity.

The Rio model teaches that post-pandemic recovery is possible, but slow and totally dependent on physically integrating the extremes of the social pyramid. Unless the federal government and the municipalities understand that the financial modeling of a mobility PPP fails without an aggressive policy of reoccupation of city centers, we will repeat the most painful of contrasts: state-of-the-art trains silently gliding through urban landscapes while the passenger remains stuck in traffic, alongside, on an app-based motorcycle.
