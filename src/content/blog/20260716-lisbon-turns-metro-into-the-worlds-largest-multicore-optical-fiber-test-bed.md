The LUMIRing project occupies 26 kilometers of Yellow Line tunnels with over 1,900 km of revolutionary cables, anticipating the era of post-5G communications
Modernity is not abstract. It has ballast, gauge, energy, and direction — and in Lisbon, that direction points to the subsoil of the Yellow Line, where railway tunnels have become the most ambitious optical communications laboratory ever conceived in a real urban environment.
The Lisbon Metro and ISCTE – University Institute of Lisbon inaugurated the <a href='https://www.metrolisboa.pt/institucional/2025/11/03/metropolitano-de-lisboa-e-iscte-inauguram-maior-banco-de-testes-de-fibra-otica-multinucleo-do-mundo-3/' target='_blank'>LUMIRing (Lisbon Underground Multicore Fiber Ring)</a>, a 26-kilometer underground ring extending between Odivelas and Rato stations. The system contains over 1,900 kilometers of multicore optical fiber, of which 728 kilometers are of the same type — enough to make it the largest test bed of its kind on the entire planet.
This is not a facility for metro passengers. What runs alongside the overhead catenaries and tunnel walls of the old Iberian gauge is an infrastructure designed to break through the physical limits of data transmission that are being exhausted in conventional silicon.
Each traditional optical fiber has a single core through which light travels carrying information. The fibers installed in the LUMIRing have four and seven cores, multiplying transmission capacity by up to seven times without changing the cable diameter — a quantum leap for the networks that sustain the global internet.
The project is the result of a partnership between the public transport operator, academia, and two European industrial giants: Germany’s Heraeus Covantics, which developed the experimental fibers, and Italy’s Tratos Cavi, responsible for manufacturing the special cables. The total investment amounts to 1.4 million euros, with 588,000 euros channeled through the European Regional Development Fund (ERDF) via the <a href='https://lisboa.portugal2030.pt/2025/11/04/iscte-transforma-o-metro-de-lisboa-num-laboratorio-vivo-de-inovacao-urbana/' target='_blank'>Lisboa 2030</a> program.
The choice of the Lisbon Metro as a test platform was not random. The underground environment offers thermal stability, protection from external interference, and a winding route that forces the fibers to work under real stresses of bending, vibration, and expansion — conditions impossible to simulate in a laboratory with the same fidelity.
The project’s scientific leader, Adolfo Cartaxo, confirmed that NICT — the Japanese public research agency for information and communications technology — has already partnered to conduct large-scale demonstrations of multicore fiber systems throughout 2026. The expectation is that the tests in the Lisbon underground ring will provide the definitive parameters for global operators to migrate their networks to the new optical architecture.
ISCTE’s Vice-Rector for Research and Technological Modernization, Jorge Costa, revealed that market interest has already materialized in concrete contacts. The US-based AT&T, Germany’s Deutsche Telekom, and the UK’s Vodafone are closely monitoring the results, assessing how to integrate multicore technology into their continental and transoceanic infrastructures.
The acting president of the Lisbon Metro, Maria Helena Campos, described the initiative as an opportunity to validate next-generation technologies at an operational scale, something still little tested worldwide. For her, the partnership highlights the public company’s ability to attract projects of high scientific value and project its image onto the international stage.
What is at stake is not just the speed of next-generation broadband. The saturation of current single-core fibers is an imminent bottleneck for the expansion of artificial intelligence, real-time telemedicine, autonomous vehicles, and machine-to-machine communication — everything depends on physical layers capable of handling exponential data volumes without collapsing.
LUMIRing embodies a rare concept in telecommunications engineering: a living laboratory where fundamental science and urban operation coexist in the same three-dimensional space. Yellow Line trains continue transporting passengers while, millimeters above or alongside, light beams travel through multiple cores in parallel, testing the limits of applied physics.
ISCTE’s Rector, Maria de Lurdes Rodrigues, classified the laboratory as unique in Europe, underlining that its economic impact will be broad-spectrum. The facility already attracts researchers from various parts of the world and generates high-quality scientific output, anchoring highly qualified jobs in Lisbon that would normally migrate to the major technological centers of northern Europe or North America.
The revolution planned for 2026 is not just about raw transmission capacity. Multicore fibers enable flexible network architectures, where different cores can be assigned to different service providers or critical functions, increasing the resilience and cybersecurity of the internet’s physical infrastructure itself.
By opening its tunnels to this experiment, the Lisbon Metro consolidates a role few transport operators worldwide dare to assume: that of a dual innovation platform, where urban mobility and cutting-edge research feed off each other. There is no coincidence in the geographical alignment that placed ISCTE exactly above the Yellow Line — the city now becomes a strategic node of global communications.
Lisbon’s underground transformation teaches that century-old public infrastructures can be re-activated at the technological forefront without losing their social function. The same tunnels that shorten distances in the Portuguese capital now also shorten the distance between the present and the planet’s digital future.
