The BNDES diagnosis exposes a brutal paradox: the country needs massive investment in mobility, but the most efficient mode requires high costs that challenge public budgets.

<p>When technique organizes space, society gains time to live. Brazil faces a monumental challenge in urban mobility, with the need for massive expansion of rail systems to avoid the collapse of large cities.</p>
<p>For Brazil to reach a minimal civilizational level in public transport by 2054, it will be necessary to double the current metro network — jumping from the current 376 operational kilometers to nearly 700 — and quadruple the BRT (Bus Rapid Transit) and light rail (VLT) networks, which should be expanded from 631 to an impressive 2,500 kilometers.</p>
<p>There is in this diagnosis a paradox that challenges any planner. Why is the metro, being the mode with the highest carrying capacity and the backbone of any modern metropolis, precisely the one that most increases public investment costs?</p>
<p>The answer lies in the brutal arithmetic of the underground and electrification. While a BRT corridor requires around R$50 million per kilometer, a heavy metro system, with electrified double tracks, deep tunnels, and platform screen doors, can consume up to R$1 billion per kilometer.</p>
<p>This 20-fold disproportion in implementation cost explains the predominance of rapid bus options in managers' choices, even though the rail's carrying capacity is unbeatable for reorganizing the urban fabric in a lasting way.</p>
<p>The onerous complexity of the metro lies not in the rolling stock technology itself, but in the civil excavation works, the cost of the third rail, the signaling systems, and the need for fare integration with a capillary surface network.</p>
<p>Brazilian engineering, which has already demonstrated competence in the expansion of lines in São Paulo and Rio de Janeiro, stumbles upon a financing model that often neglects long-term budget predictability, creating a mismatch between demand and the supply of new trains.</p>
<p>The scenario worsens when usage data is weighed. Brazilian public transport faces significant challenges, with migration to app-based motorcycles and private cars, but it is also a direct consequence of a declining occupancy factor and routes that have become too slow for a society that urgently needs productivity.</p>
<p>The crisis of the paying passenger feeds back into the investment crisis. By losing fare revenue, systems face difficulties in funding maintenance and expansion, in a vicious cycle where the lack of new rails drives away more users and erodes the financing base.</p>
<p>State intervention becomes an unavoidable variable in the urban equation. The dichotomy between heavy rail and bus corridors imposes itself as a false choice, because all large cities will need to have BRT and metro complementing each other, well integrated, each fulfilling a specific function in the hierarchy of displacement.</p>
<p>While light rail and monorail operate in the medium capacity range with agility and a more civilized implementation cost, heavy metro acts as the structurer of very high-density corridors.</p>
<p>The Metropolitan Region of São Paulo, for example, appears with expansion projects that relieve the intermunicipal bus system. In Rio de Janeiro, the logic repeats with the expansion of Metro Line 4 and projects for new lines that seek to connect populous regions.</p>
<p>These interventions require a mobilization of massive resources over the next thirty years, an amount that requires a robust budgetary response from the federal government, with significant allocations in the current four-year cycle.</p>
<p>The potential effect of this investment could be revolutionary in cities with repressed demand. Fortaleza, for example, could experience a leap in service if the future network is implemented with integrated fares and car disincentive policies, completely reorganizing the logistics of the Ceará capital.</p>
<p>This optimistic horizon, however, depends on variables that go far beyond civil engineering or track electrification. The implementation of a public fare that commits at most 6% of the average workers' income, the physical and fare integration between different modes, and traffic signal priority for collective transport are preconditions so that the expansion of rails does not turn into underutilized white elephants.</p>
<p>The complexity of financing rail mobility in Brazil also echoes the historical challenge of expanding freight railways. While a kilometer of highway costs an average of R$8 million, the freight rail demands about R$27 million, as pointed out by an infrastructure sector analysis on the new railway auctions that the government is trying to unlock.</p>
<p>The crucial difference is that, in the metro universe, the return comes not in tons of iron ore, but in regained time, avoided emissions, and urban productivity. The chronicle of delay in Brazilian mobility is written, therefore, not by the absence of plans, but by the collective hesitation to pay the price of modernity on rails.</p>
