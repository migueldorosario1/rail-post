The SP-RJ HST is a monument to technical incapacity and the political farce that Brazil insists on cultivating in place of a mature and sovereign railway engineering.

Modernity is not abstract. It has ballast, gauge, energy and direction. In Brazil, modernity on high-speed rails remains a metallic mirage shimmering over a swamp of studies, postponements and chronic technical incapacity. The High-Speed Train (TAV) between São Paulo and Rio de Janeiro, which has been an electoral flag, a World Cup promise and a central piece of the PAC, completes eighteen years of purely rhetorical existence, without a single meter of permanent way for 320 km/h having been laid.

The project, officially cancelled in 2023 by the federal government (Ordinance No. 1,022/2023 of the Ministry of Transport), with definitive archiving by the TCU in 2024, no longer has prospects for progress. There is no "postponement to 2028" nor "R$ 60 billion accumulated" in active costs, because the project has been dead for over two years.

The 417-kilometer route, with four planned stops — São Paulo, São José dos Campos, Volta Redonda and Rio de Janeiro — promised a journey of less than two hours between the two metropolises. Integration with subways and urban trains would create a mobility link that regional aviation and highways have never been able to forge.

However, the project runs into a wall of difficulties that go far beyond the fiscal deficit. Brazilian railway engineering, forged in the heavy work of ore and grain, masters the cycle of 80 km/h to 100 km/h on long freight trains, a technical universe absolutely distinct from that required by a high-speed service.

The necessary technological leap is radical. Operating at 320 km/h with a stable overhead catenary demands mastery over dynamic vehicle-track interaction, high-precision signaling systems, and expertise in tunnel and viaduct construction that the country simply does not possess autochthonously.

Specialists consulted are unanimous: the work, if it gets off the drawing board, will require a complete package of imported know-how. The crossing of the Serra do Mar and urban interferences on an already densely occupied axis represent geotechnical and land engineering challenges that would further increase implementation and maintenance costs, as pointed out by Thomaz Schoeffel, director-secretary of the Brazilian Association for Railway Preservation (ABPF).

Economist Cláudio Frischtak, in acid criticism, highlights that the nature of the project, equipment and components is completely different from anything done here. He recalls that the country has never had construction experience with this mode and that the bill is very difficult to close without robust state subsidies, in a scenario of permanent fiscal crisis.

The choice for pure high speed, discarding alternatives such as modernizing the existing line for a medium-speed train (150 km/h) with nighttime freight sharing, was a political decision from the Dilma Rousseff era that echoes to this day. The option for maximum technological performance, without ballast in the maturity of national engineering, created an unbridgeable technical gap.

While Brazil stumbles with the bullet train dream, the country tries to advance in freight railway projects that are the antechamber of logistics modernity. The Ferrogrão, with its 933 kilometers between Sinop and Miritituba, still faces legal imbroglios in the STF and millionaire disputes over the value of studies, as the Ministry of Transport and the AGU try to unlock the bidding against the resistance of EDLP.

In parallel, the federal government is preparing the launch of a National Railway Plan with a pipeline of R$ 100 billion in investments, including Fiol, Fico and the Southeastern Railway Ring. This is a vital expansion, but still tied to the freight mode and broad gauge, far from the millimeter precision of the standard gauge required by high-performance passenger trains.

The bi-oceanic connection itself, which Brazil <a href='https://g1.globo.com/economia/noticia/2025/07/08/ferrovia-para-ligar-o-atlantico-ao-pacifico-veja-o-que-se-sabe-ate-agora-do-projeto-do-brasil-com-a-china.ghtml' target='_blank'>is studying with China</a> to link the Atlantic to the Pacific, reveals the paradox: the country launches itself into continental-scale projects, but operates its railway engineering at a basic level. Dreaming of an HST in this context is like wanting to launch an orbital rocket without first mastering jet aviation.

The legal framework for railways, with Law No. 14,437/2022 (Railway Law), did not authorize any company to explore the service for 99 years without a traditional bidding process. Despite the Lula government stating that the project "is not the government's" and will depend on private financing via BNDES, Ibama has not even received the EIA/Rima to begin environmental licensing.

The gap between political ambition and technical execution capacity is the portrait of a country that consumes futuristic projects as a distraction. The cruising speed of the discourse is high, but the real work remains stopped at the station of bureaucracy and incompetence, waiting for a know-how that is never planted on national soil.
